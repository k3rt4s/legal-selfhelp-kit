# South Dakota: handling a dispute with your own lawyer

This section is for a person in South Dakota who has a problem with their own lawyer over
fees, billing, communication, getting their file back, or an advance payment that was never
earned or never refunded. It does not cover legal malpractice claims or how to run one
yourself. Retrieved August 20, 2026.

What is different about South Dakota:

- South Dakota does not keep its lawyer conduct rules in a stand-alone court rule book. They
  are printed as the Appendix to SDCL chapter 16-18, inside the state's codified statutes, so
  the correct citation form is "SDCL 16-18, Appendix, Rule 1.5," commonly shortened to
  "Rule 1.5" the same way most states do. Read section 1.
- This kit could not confirm a statewide, or even a local, attorney fee arbitration program
  in South Dakota. The State Bar's own page on lawyer discipline tells a client that "controversy
  over fees may be resolved by court action," and searches of the State Bar site, the state
  judiciary site, and both sites' full Wayback Machine history turned up no fee arbitration or
  fee dispute program page. Read section 3 before you assume this remedy exists.
- A South Dakota lawyer has a statutory lien on your file. SDCL 16-18-21 gives an attorney a
  lien on "any paper belonging to his client" that came into the lawyer's hands during the
  representation, for the lawyer's unpaid fee balance. This is a real, separate piece of state
  law behind the ordinary ethics rule on returning a file, and it can justify a lawyer
  withholding your papers until you post security for the disputed fee. Read section 2.
- South Dakota small claims judgments cannot be appealed. SDCL 15-39-57 says plainly, "No party
  may appeal any decision entered under this procedure." The only way out of small claims court
  is a defendant's pre-judgment petition to remove the case to the regular civil docket, filed
  before the hearing, not an appeal after judgment. Read section 6.
- South Dakota's legal malpractice period is three years and runs from when the "malpractice,
  error, mistake, or omission" occurred, not from when the client discovered it. This is
  shorter than South Dakota's own six-year period for an ordinary contract claim. Read
  section 7 and get advice quickly if you suspect malpractice rather than a billing dispute.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct: numbering and adoption

South Dakota's lawyer conduct rules are adopted by the South Dakota Supreme Court under its
inherent authority to supervise attorneys as officers of the court, codified in SDCL 16-19-20.
The rules themselves are printed in the state's codified statutes as the Appendix to SDCL
chapter 16-18, titled "South Dakota Rules of Professional Conduct." This is the first thing a
reader from another state needs to know: South Dakota does not maintain its ethics rules as a
freestanding court rule set the way many states do. It carries them inside its statute book,
as an appendix following the ordinary attorney-powers statutes in chapter 16-18.

The numbered rules track the ABA Model Rules numbering directly, from Rule 1.0 (Terminology)
through the 8-series rules, with no added chapter or article prefix. A reader should cite a
rule as "Rule 1.5" or, for precision in a letter, "SDCL 16-18, Appendix, Rule 1.5." Unlike a
state such as Utah, South Dakota does not renumber its rules with a chapter prefix, so a
reader looking for "Rule 1.5," "Rule 1.4," "Rule 1.15," or "Rule 1.16" will find them under
those exact numbers.

The current version of the rules carries Source notes tracing most individual rules to SL
2004, ch 327 (Supreme Court Rule 03-26), effective January 1, 2004, which appears to be when
South Dakota recompiled its rules into the current Model Rules-based numbering. Some
individual rules have been amended since. The Preamble and Scope section, which section 8
below relies on, was last amended by SL 2022, ch 249 (Supreme Court Rule 21-08), effective
September 1, 2021. This kit did not find a separate, dedicated Source note for every
individual rule in the text it retrieved, so treat January 1, 2004 as the date of the current
numbering scheme, not necessarily the date of every subsection's current wording; confirm the
Source note under the specific rule you are relying on at the official publication point
below.

Lawyer discipline is administered by the Disciplinary Board of the State Bar of South Dakota,
a seven-member board created under SDCL 16-19-24, which appoints its own board counsel under
SDCL 16-19-29. The Client Security Fund, described in section 5, is administered separately by
a five-member committee appointed by the President of the State Bar of South Dakota.

The rules are published by the South Dakota Legislative Research Council at
<https://sdlegislature.gov/Statutes/Codified_Laws/2044875> (SDCL chapter 16-18) and through
the underlying data endpoint at
<https://sdlegislature.gov/api/Statutes/Statute/16-18-A>, which serves the Appendix in full.
The Legislature's statute pages are served through a JSON-backed viewer rather than plain
HTML, so if a bare chapter page does not show rule text, use the data endpoint above or a
Wayback Machine snapshot of it.

## 2. How South Dakota's rules differ from the model

Rule 1.5's reasonableness standard tracks the ABA Model Rule closely. Rule 1.5(a) lists the
same eight reasonableness factors as the model, in the same order and with no added or removed
factor: the time, labor, novelty, and skill the matter required; whether taking the case
precluded other work; the customary local fee; the amount involved and the results obtained;
any time limits imposed by the client or circumstances; the length of the lawyer-client
relationship; the lawyer's experience, reputation, and ability; and whether the fee is fixed or
contingent. Rule 1.5(b) on communicating the fee basis, Rule 1.5(c) and (d) on contingent fees
including the same two flat bans in domestic relations matters and criminal defense, and Rule
1.5(e) on fee-splitting between lawyers, all track the model rule's substance as well. This
kit did not find a South Dakota-specific reasonableness factor, a state-specific contingent
fee percentage cap, or a nonrefundable-fee rule beyond the ordinary trust-account requirement
in Rule 1.15(a). "No deviation found in Rule 1.5" is itself a real finding here, checked
against the full text of the rule as published in the SDCL 16-18 Appendix.

The real deviation sits in Rule 1.4, not Rule 1.5. South Dakota added paragraphs (c), (d), and
(e) to the model's four-part communication rule, and the model rule has no equivalent for any
of them. Rule 1.4(c) requires a lawyer who does not carry at least $100,000 of professional
liability insurance, or whose coverage lapses during a representation, to disclose that fact
on the lawyer's letterhead, using specific required language: either "This lawyer is not
covered by professional liability insurance" or "This firm is not covered by professional
liability insurance." Rule 1.4(d) requires that disclosure in every written communication with
the client, and Rule 1.4(e) exempts certain government and in-house lawyers who do not
represent outside clients. If your lawyer's letterhead does not carry this statement and you
have reason to think the lawyer is uninsured, that itself may be a rule violation independent
of anything about your fee.

Rule 1.15(a) on safekeeping property adds a specific record-retention period the model rule
leaves blank for each state to fill in: complete trust account records must be kept for five
years after the representation ends. Rule 1.15(a) otherwise tracks the model closely, including
the requirement that advance fees and expenses go into a trust account and can only be
withdrawn as earned or incurred.

Rule 1.16(d) on ending a representation matches the model rule, including the closing sentence
that a lawyer may retain client papers "to the extent permitted by other law." That phrase
matters in South Dakota specifically because "other law" includes a real statutory lien. SDCL
16-18-21 gives a lawyer a lien for the unpaid balance of fees on any client paper in the
lawyer's possession from the representation, on money in the lawyer's hands belonging to the
client, and on money due the client from an adverse party once written notice is given. SDCL
16-18-22 lets a client release that lien by posting a bond for double the amount claimed, or
for whatever smaller sum a judge fixes, which matters if you cannot raise double, and
requires the lawyer, on demand, to furnish an itemized bill of the services and amount claimed
within ten days or lose the lien. SDCL 16-18-24 and 16-18-25 say a lawyer who withholds money
or property under a claimed lien is not guilty of the state's misdemeanor for refusing to turn
over client funds (SDCL 16-18-23) as long as the client is offered adequate security, or the
lawyer posts security to guarantee eventual payment. In practical terms, yes, a South Dakota
lawyer may hold your file for unpaid fees, on the authority of this statutory lien, not just as
a matter of the ethics rule's residual "other law" language. Demanding a bill of particulars
under SDCL 16-18-22 is one concrete step available to a client trying to get papers released.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

This kit could not confirm that South Dakota runs a statewide, or any local, attorney fee
arbitration or mediation program, after checking multiple sources as instructed.

- The State Bar of South Dakota's own "Lawyer Discipline" page, aimed at consumers, tells a
  client directly that "fee matters are not often a basis for discipline... controversy over
  fees may be resolved by court action." It does not mention an arbitration or mediation
  alternative. <https://www.statebarofsouthdakota.com/lawyer-discipline/>
- A site search of statebarofsouthdakota.com for "fee arbitration" returned no results, and a
  search for "fee dispute" surfaced only that same Lawyer Discipline page.
- A full CDX listing of the State Bar's Wayback Machine history
  (<http://web.archive.org/cdx/search/cdx?url=statebarofsouthdakota.com&matchType=domain&output=json&collapse=urlkey&limit=5000&fl=original>),
  over 3,000 archived URLs, has no page whose path mentions "arbitration" or "dispute"; the
  only "fee" hits are RSS feeds and one page called "virtual-coffee."
- A full CDX listing of the state judiciary's Wayback Machine history for ujs.sd.gov
  (<http://web.archive.org/cdx/search/cdx?url=ujs.sd.gov&matchType=domain&output=json&collapse=urlkey&limit=8000&fl=original>),
  over 8,000 archived URLs, has no "arbitration" or "dispute" hit either; its "fee" hits are
  ordinary court filing fees, child support late fees, and court-appointed counsel fees.
- SDCL chapter 16-19 (attorney discipline), read in full text, contains no reference to
  arbitration or mediation of any kind, fee-related or otherwise.
- The Rules of Professional Conduct appendix uses "arbitrator" and "mediator" in a handful of
  places: the Terminology rule's definition of "tribunal," Rule 1.12 on a lawyer who formerly
  served as a judge, arbitrator, or mediator, and Rule 2.4, governing a lawyer who serves as a
  third-party neutral for other people's disputes. None of them describe a program for disputes
  between a lawyer and that lawyer's own client.

If your fee dispute cannot be resolved directly with your lawyer, the money routes this kit
could confirm for South Dakota are small claims or circuit court, covered in section 6, and the
Client Security Fund in section 5 if the lawyer's conduct involved dishonesty rather than an
ordinary billing disagreement. If a South Dakota program does exist through a channel this kit
did not reach, such as a local bar association's own informal fee-mediation service, confirm
directly with the State Bar of South Dakota at 605-224-7554 or the State Bar's own current
website before ruling it out for your situation.

## 4. Filing a bar complaint

South Dakota's disciplinary body is the Disciplinary Board of the State Bar of South Dakota, a
seven-member board created under SDCL 16-19-24, working alongside the Attorney General and the
South Dakota Supreme Court under the self-discipline system described in SDCL chapter 16-19.

A complaint is filed by sending a signed letter to the Disciplinary Board at the State Bar of
South Dakota's Pierre address, describing the facts, names, dates, and sources of information.
This kit did not find a stated deadline for filing a complaint, either in SDCL chapter 16-19 or
on the State Bar's own consumer-facing discipline page; treat this as an open question rather
than an assumption that no deadline exists, and confirm directly with the Disciplinary Board.

The State Bar's own page for the public is direct about what a complaint will and will not do.
It states that "fee matters are not often a basis for discipline... because they generally do
not involve questions of ethics or professional misconduct," except where a fee is "either
illegal or clearly excessive." It also states plainly that the Disciplinary Board "does not,
and cannot, give individual legal service or advice to any person making a complaint," and that
"any loss the client suffers as a result of the matter involved cannot be recovered through
disciplinary proceedings." Discipline against your lawyer is not a path to getting your money
back; a client seeking money must sue the lawyer directly or use one of the routes in sections
5 and 6.

This kit did not find a separate informal or consumer-assistance intake track distinct from the
formal complaint process described above; South Dakota's page describes one complaint channel,
reviewed by the Board, rather than a split between an informal track and a formal one.

South Dakota does have a limited disciplinary restitution mechanism. Under SDCL 16-19-87, when
the Supreme Court reinstates a suspended or disbarred attorney, it may condition reinstatement
on "the making of partial or complete restitution to parties harmed by the misconduct which led
to petitioner's suspension or disbarment." This is discretionary, tied to a reinstatement
proceeding rather than something a complainant can request directly, and not a substitute for
the routes in sections 5 and 6.

The Disciplinary Board's public information and the complaint-filing instructions are published
at <https://www.statebarofsouthdakota.com/lawyer-discipline/>.

## 5. Client protection fund

South Dakota's fund is the Client Security Fund, administered by a five-member committee
appointed by the President of the State Bar of South Dakota, funded from a portion of every
State Bar member's dues.

The Fund reimburses a person who lost money or property because of the "fraudulent or dishonest
act" of a South Dakota lawyer. It excludes losses arising from the lawyer's negligence or
malpractice, and losses already covered by another source such as the lawyer's own malpractice
insurance. To be eligible, the lawyer generally must have died, been adjudged mentally ill,
voluntarily surrendered the license, left South Dakota's jurisdiction and cannot be found with
reasonable diligence, or been disbarred or suspended. A judgment based on the lawyer's
fraudulent or dishonest act is generally required to support a claim, except for claims under
$750.00.

The maximum payment allowed from the Fund for a single claim is $10,000. This cap is set on the
State Bar's own administrative Client Security Fund page, not in a published SDCL statute or
Supreme Court rule this kit could locate; this kit did not find a published aggregate or
per-lawyer annual cap, and did not find a stated filing deadline running from the loss or from
a disciplinary action. Payments are discretionary, "a matter of grace and not of right," and
may cover only a portion of a claim.

A claim is filed in writing, addressed to the Secretary/Treasurer of the State Bar of South
Dakota, who forwards it to the Committee chairperson. A claimant is not required to retain a
lawyer to file or pursue a claim.

The State Bar of South Dakota's Client Security Fund page is currently returning a 404 error on
the live site (checked August 20, 2026), consistent with a broader site redesign; several other
State Bar pages checked for this pack, including the By-Laws page, currently show placeholder
template text instead of real content. This kit sourced the Fund's terms from the last complete
Wayback Machine snapshot of the official page, dated April 20, 2024, at
<http://web.archive.org/web/20240420193925/https://www.statebarofsouthdakota.com/client-security-fund/>.
Confirm the current $10,000 per-claim figure and any deadline directly with the State Bar before
relying on it, since the live page cannot currently be checked.

## 6. Small claims court

South Dakota does not have a separate small claims court. Small claims procedure runs inside
the existing circuit and magistrate courts, under SDCL chapter 15-39, "Small Claims Procedure."

The jurisdictional limit is $12,000, not including allowable costs or attorney fees, under SDCL
15-39-45.1. This is a single statewide figure; this kit did not find any county-level variation.

A lawyer is allowed to appear. SDCL 15-39-48 refers throughout to "the plaintiff initiating the
action or the plaintiff's attorney," and SDCL 15-39-47 defines who may act as an authorized
representative, including an attorney-at-law who is an active State Bar member in good
standing, alongside authorized non-lawyer representatives for entities such as corporations and
LLCs. Nothing in the chapter bars a party from using a lawyer from the outset.

By filing under this procedure, a plaintiff waives the right to a jury trial and the right to
appeal, under SDCL 15-39-56, unless the case is removed to the regular civil docket. SDCL
15-39-57 states plainly: "No party may appeal any decision entered under this procedure." There
is no post-judgment appeal in South Dakota small claims court. The only escape route is a
defendant's own petition, filed at least five days before the date the defendant is due to
appear or answer, to remove the case to the regular civil docket of the circuit or magistrate
court for a full civil trial, either to the court or a jury. That petition must be supported by
an affidavit showing good reason the formal process is justified and that the defendant has a
meritorious defense. Removal costs $35 to enter the case on the regular docket, plus a $250
deposit to secure the plaintiff for costs or damages the court may later find the plaintiff is
entitled to, for $285 total, under SDCL 15-39-57. Because only a defendant can remove the case,
and only before judgment, a plaintiff who chooses small claims accepts that the resulting
judgment is final with no appeal.

The base statutory filing fee at SDCL 15-39-52 ($4, $10, or $20 depending on the amount) is not
the true cost to file. The state judiciary's own current fee schedule, "Small Claims Filing
Fees" dated July 12, 2026, adds a Law Library fee, a Court Automation Surcharge, certified-mail
certification and return charges, and stamp fees on top of the base filing fee. For a claim
between $4,000 and the current $12,000 limit, the total cost shown on that schedule is $42.84,
not the $20 figure the statute alone would suggest. For a claim of $100 or less, the total is
$24.84 against a $4 statutory base. Use the court's own schedule, not the bare statutory number,
to estimate what filing will actually cost. Published at
<https://ujs.sd.gov/files/small-claims-filing-fees/>.

This kit did not find a rule specific to suing your own lawyer in small claims court; an
ordinary fee dispute within the $12,000 limit is treated the same as any other small claims
case.

The governing statutes are published by the South Dakota Legislative Research Council at
<https://sdlegislature.gov/api/Statutes/Statute/15-39> (chapter 15-39, Small Claims Procedure).

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A dispute over what you owed your lawyer, or what your lawyer owed you back, under a fee
agreement is a contract claim. SDCL 15-2-13 gives an action "upon a contract, obligation, or
liability, express or implied," six years from accrual. South Dakota's contract limitation
period does not distinguish a written contract from an oral one; both get six years under this
same subdivision, unlike some states that split the period by whether the agreement is in
writing.

South Dakota has a dedicated legal malpractice statute, SDCL 15-2-14.2: "An action against a
licensed attorney, his agent or employee, for malpractice, error, mistake, or omission, whether
based upon contract or tort, can be commenced only within three years after the alleged
malpractice, error, mistake, or omission shall have occurred." This period is shorter than the
six-year contract period, applies whether the claim sounds in contract or in tort, and by its
own text runs from when the error occurred, not from when the client discovered it. This kit
did not find a codified discovery rule or a separate statute of repose attached to this section;
the statute's own accrual language, running from occurrence rather than discovery, functions
similarly to a repose period in that it does not obviously wait for the client to learn of the
problem. This kit located South Dakota Supreme Court decisions addressing legal malpractice
accrual, including Peterson v. Burns (2001) and Schoenrock v. Tappe (1988), through case
metadata on CourtListener, but could not reach the opinion text itself because the source
returned an access challenge rather than the document; this kit therefore does not state
whether South Dakota case law has engrafted any judicial discovery rule onto this statute's
occurrence language. Treat three years from the act or omission as the outer edge you must beat,
not a deadline softened by when you found out, and get your own lawyer's advice quickly if you
suspect malpractice rather than a billing problem.

This kit does not cover running a legal malpractice claim. These periods are here so you know a
clock exists and can get counsel in time if you need it, not so you can calculate your own
deadline.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

South Dakota's Rules of Professional Conduct carry the same Scope section language as the ABA
model rule, in numbered paragraph [20] of the Scope note that precedes the numbered rules:
"Violation of a Rule should not itself give rise to a cause of action against a lawyer nor
should it create any presumption in such a case that a legal duty has been breached... They are
not designed to be a basis for civil liability." The same paragraph adds that, because the
Rules do establish standards of conduct, "a lawyer's violation of a Rule may be evidence of
breach of the applicable standard of conduct," meaning it can support a malpractice claim's
standard-of-care element even though the rule itself does not create the claim.

In practical terms, filing a rule-violation complaint against your South Dakota lawyer does not
by itself put money in your hands. Your actual money routes are the ones covered above: a
contract claim over the fee itself under section 7, the Client Security Fund in section 5 if
the conduct was dishonest in the sense described there, small claims or circuit court under
section 6, or a separate malpractice claim if that applies to you, which is outside what this
kit covers.
