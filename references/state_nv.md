# Nevada: handling a dispute with your own lawyer

This section is for a person in Nevada who has a problem with their own lawyer over fees,
billing, communication, getting their file back, or an advance payment that was never
earned or never refunded. It does not cover legal malpractice claims or how to run one
yourself. Retrieved August 20, 2026.

What is different about Nevada:

- Nevada has a genuine statutory attorney's lien on your file. Under NRS 18.015, a lawyer
  who has not been paid can place a lien on your papers and property in the lawyer's
  possession and hold onto them until the fee dispute is resolved. This is the direct
  answer to "can my lawyer hold my file hostage": in Nevada, within limits, yes. Read
  section 2 before you assume you can simply demand your file back.
- Nevada's fee arbitration program, run by the State Bar of Nevada's Fee Dispute
  Committee, is voluntary for both sides in the ordinary case, but it becomes mandatory
  for the lawyer if that lawyer has already been the subject of three or more fee
  disputes in the two years before your dispute is filed. This exact trigger is written
  into the Committee's own Rules of Procedure, not just described on a bar web page.
  Read section 3.
- Nevada requires a contingent fee agreement to carry specific boldface warnings that go
  beyond what many other states require, including a warning about liability for the
  other side's fees and costs if you lose, and a warning about liability for bringing a
  suit found to be brought only to harass. Read section 2.
- Nevada enacted its own interpretive guide, Rule 1.0A, as an actual numbered rule rather
  than leaving that kind of guidance as unenacted preamble commentary. It states plainly
  that the ABA Model Rules' preamble and comments are not themselves enacted in Nevada.
  Read section 1.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct: numbering and adoption

Nevada's rules governing lawyer conduct are the Nevada Rules of Professional Conduct,
adopted by order of the Nevada Supreme Court (In re: Amendments to the Nevada Rules of
Professional Conduct, ADKT 370), effective May 1, 2006, and amended since. They are
published in full, as currently amended, by the Nevada Legislature's own site at
<https://www.leg.state.nv.us/CourtRules/RPC.html>.

Nevada uses the same numbering as the ABA model rules, without a state-specific prefix.
The fee rule is Rule 1.5, the communication rule is Rule 1.4, the safekeeping-property
rule is Rule 1.15, and the rule on terminating representation is Rule 1.16, the same
numbers a reader from most other states would expect. The published rule text itself
refers to these only as "Rule 1.5" and so on; it does not use an abbreviation like "RPC"
or "NRPC" as part of the rule's own citation form, so this kit uses "Rule 1.5" as the
citation form to match the source.

The broader structure that governs the State Bar of Nevada itself, lawyer discipline,
fee arbitration, and the client protection fund sits in a separate rule set, the Nevada
Supreme Court Rules (SCR), also adopted by the Nevada Supreme Court and published at
<https://www.leg.state.nv.us/CourtRules/SCR.html>. The State Bar of Nevada is a public
corporation created and supervised under these rules; it is not a private association.
Discipline is handled by the Office of Bar Counsel and Southern and Northern Nevada
Disciplinary Boards under SCR 99 through 123, while fee arbitration and the client
protection fund are separately authorized under SCR 84 and SCR 85. A reader looking only
at the numbered Rules of Professional Conduct will not find the fee-arbitration or
client-protection-fund rules there; they are in the separate SCR set.

One structural point worth knowing: Nevada added its own Rule 1.0A, "Guidelines for
Interpreting the Nevada Rules of Professional Conduct," as an actual numbered rule. It
states that the preamble and comments to the ABA Model Rules are not themselves enacted
by Nevada's rule, though they may be consulted for guidance where they do not conflict
with the Nevada rules. This kit did not find a published date for the most recent
amendment to any specific numbered rule cited in this pack beyond the rule set's own
"[Added; effective May 1, 2006]" and later amendment annotations that appear inline in
the source text next to each rule.

## 2. How Nevada's fee rule differs from the model

Rule 1.5(a) lists the same eight reasonableness factors as the ABA model rule, in the
same order: time and labor required and the novelty and difficulty of the questions
involved; whether accepting the matter would preclude other employment; the customary
local fee; the amount involved and results obtained; time limitations imposed by the
client or circumstances; the nature and length of the professional relationship; the
lawyer's experience, reputation, and ability; and whether the fee is fixed or
contingent. Nevada adds nothing to that list and removes nothing from it.

Where Nevada goes beyond the model rule is in what a contingent fee agreement must
disclose. Rule 1.5(c) requires the agreement to be in writing, signed by the client,
and to state, in boldface type at least as large as the largest other type used in the
agreement, all of the following: the method by which the fee is to be determined,
including the percentage that accrues to the lawyer on settlement, trial, or appeal;
whether litigation and other expenses are to be deducted from the recovery, and whether
those expenses are deducted before or after the contingent fee is calculated; whether
the client is liable for expenses regardless of outcome; a statement that in the event
of a loss, the client may be liable for the opposing party's attorney fees and will be
liable for the opposing party's costs as required by law; and a statement that a suit
brought solely to harass or to coerce a settlement may result in liability for
malicious prosecution or abuse of process. That last item is a warning about tort
liability to the other side, not a citation to a specific fee-shifting statute; this
kit originally attached NRS 18.010 to it, but the rule's own text does not cite that
statute for this disclosure, so that citation has been removed. This disclosure set,
covering exposure to the other side's fees on a loss and exposure for a
harassment-or-coercion suit, is more extensive than the plain percentage and expense
disclosures the ABA model rule requires and goes directly to what a client signing a
contingency agreement needs to understand before they sign. The boldface-type
requirement itself is stated directly in the rule text, not just a drafting custom.

Rule 1.5 also flatly bars two categories of contingent fee the model rule bars as well:
a fee contingent on securing a divorce or on the amount of alimony, support, or property
settlement in a domestic matter, and a fee for representing a defendant in a criminal
case.

This kit looked specifically for a Nevada-specific rule on "nonrefundable" or
"earned upon receipt" retainer language and did not find a separate rule addressing it
by that name. The general rule that governs it is Rule 1.15, safekeeping property:
advance fees paid by a client must be held and disbursed under a written fee agreement,
and any portion of a fee that has not actually been earned remains the client's
property regardless of how the retainer agreement labels it. A label like
"nonrefundable" in an agreement does not by itself override this rule or the
reasonableness requirement in Rule 1.5(a).

On the file-hostage question: Nevada gives lawyers a real statutory tool to hold a
client's file over unpaid fees, and the statute is more specific than a single general
lien. NRS 18.015(1) actually creates two different liens: paragraph (a) gives a lien on
any claim, demand, or cause of action placed in the lawyer's hands for suit or
collection, and paragraph (b) separately gives a lien, in any civil action, on any file
or other property properly left in the lawyer's possession by the client. Subsection
4(b) confirms that the paragraph (b) lien "attaches to any file or other property
properly left in the possession of the attorney by his or her client, including,
without limitation, copies of the attorney's file if the original documents received
from the client have been returned to the client, and authorizes the attorney to retain
any such file or property" until the lien is adjudicated. So the lien on the file
itself is real and separate from the lien on a claim or recovery; the two should not be
confused, and the pack now cites the correct paragraph. Rule 1.16(d), on terminating
representation, requires a lawyer to take steps reasonably practicable to protect a
client's interests, including surrendering papers and property to which the client is
entitled and refunding any advance payment that has not been earned, but adds that the
lawyer "may retain papers relating to the client to the extent permitted by other law."
NRS 18.015 is that other law.

Two things temper this. First, the lien is not automatic: under subsection 3, the
lawyer perfects it by serving written notice on the client (and, for the paragraph (a)
lien, on the opposing party) stating the lien and its amount. A lawyer who has not
served that notice has not perfected the statutory lien. Second, subsection 6 gives
either the lawyer or the client a direct way to force the issue: on a motion filed by
the attorney, the client, or another interested party, the court must, after 5 days'
notice to all interested parties, adjudicate the rights of the parties and enforce the
lien. That motion is the mechanism for a client to get a court to decide, on short
notice, whether the lawyer can keep holding the file.

In practice, this means a Nevada lawyer can lawfully withhold your file, at least the
portion properly subject to a perfected lien, while a genuine fee dispute is pending,
though the lawyer still owes the underlying duty to protect your interests, and you
have a statutory path (the subsection 6 motion) to ask a court to resolve it quickly if
a deadline is looming. This kit did not find a Nevada rule or statute specifically
defining what counts as prejudicing a client's interests in that setting, or carving
out an automatic exception to the lien for time-sensitive documents; treat that
boundary as unresolved rather than settled in your favor, and consider the subsection 6
motion if you cannot get your lawyer to agree.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

Nevada runs a single statewide program, the State Bar of Nevada's Fee Dispute
Arbitration Program, administered through the Bar's Fee Dispute Committee. Its
authorizing rule is SCR 84(12), which gives the State Bar's Board of Governors the power
to establish a committee for resolving fee disputes and the power to make attorney
arbitration of fee disputes mandatory. SCR 84(12) does not itself spell out when
participation becomes mandatory; it leaves that to the fee-dispute system the Board
implements. There is a right to de novo review in district court of any award arising
out of a fee dispute system implemented under this rule, stated directly in SCR 84(12)
itself. The program is described by the State Bar of Nevada at
<https://nvbar.org/disputing-a-lawyers-fee/petitioners/>, and its governing Rules of
Procedure are published at
<https://nvbar.org/wp-content/uploads/Fee-Dispute-Rules-of-Procedure_2026-1.pdf>.

As actually run, the program is voluntary for both sides in the ordinary case: with few
exceptions, neither the client nor the lawyer has to participate. The exception is set
out in the Committee's own Rules of Procedure: if, during the two years before the
client's petition is filed, the lawyer has already been the subject of three or more
fee disputes within the Committee's jurisdiction, participation becomes mandatory for
that lawyer, and a lawyer who then refuses to participate can be referred to the Office
of Bar Counsel for investigation. This is the single most useful asymmetry to know: an
ordinary first-time dispute against a given lawyer is voluntary on both sides, but a
client can effectively force a repeat-disputed lawyer into the process by filing.

The process starts with mediation. If mediation does not resolve the dispute and the
parties have not separately agreed to binding arbitration, the file is closed without a
binding result. Binding arbitration happens only when both sides have signed a binding
arbitration agreement; once an award is issued under a binding agreement, it binds the
petitioner, and it binds the respondent lawyer as well if the lawyer agreed to be bound.

An appeal is not an appeal on the merits. The Rules of Procedure allow a party to appeal
only on narrow grounds: that an arbitrator failed to be disqualified when required,
that the Rules of Procedure were violated, or that there was actual fraud by an
arbitrator. The Notice of Appeal must be filed with the State Bar's Las Vegas office
within 33 days after service of the written arbitration award, not 30 days as this kit
previously stated. The State Chair reviews the notice only for compliance with the
filing requirements and dismisses one that does not comply; the appeal itself is
decided by the Committee's Executive Council (the State Chair together with the
Regional Panel Chairs), which must decide within 45 days and may hold a hearing at its
discretion. SCR 84(12)'s separate right to de novo review in district court applies on
top of whatever this internal appeal process provides.

The program excludes claims for less than $250.00 and claims above "the mandatory
arbitration limit identified in N.R.S. Chapter 38," which the Rules of Procedure cite
rather than restate. NRS 38.250 sets that limit at $100,000 per plaintiff, exclusive of
attorney's fees, interest, and court costs, for Nevada's court-annexed mandatory
arbitration program generally. A single arbitrator hears disputes of $10,000 or less; a
three-member panel hears disputes above that amount, confirmed directly in the Rules of
Procedure. The State Bar describes the program on its own page as informal and free,
and this kit found no filing-fee provision anywhere in the Rules of Procedure, so treat
"no filing fee" as confirmed at the level of an official program description rather
than a numbered rule. A claim must be filed within six years after the
attorney-client relationship ended, per NRS 11.190, though the Rules of Procedure allow
that period to be waived with the consent of both parties. This kit did not find a rule
requiring a Nevada lawyer to notify a client of the right to arbitrate before suing the
client for fees.

If your dispute exceeds what the program will hear, or your lawyer will not agree to a
binding result and is not one of the repeat-disputed lawyers subject to mandatory
participation, your money routes are small claims or district court described in
section 6, or the client protection fund in section 5 if the conduct involved dishonesty
rather than a plain billing disagreement.

## 4. Filing a bar complaint

Nevada's disciplinary regulator is the Office of Bar Counsel, operating under the
Nevada Supreme Court Rules, SCR 99 through 123, and the State Bar of Nevada's own
Disciplinary Rules of Procedure, most recently amended November 2, 2023. A complaint
can be filed online, by mail, or in person; the Bar does not require a special form. The
complaint process is described at <https://nvbar.org/for-the-public/file-a-complaint/>.

This kit did not find a separate informal or consumer-assistance intake track in Nevada
comparable to a formal client-assistance program that contacts the lawyer on the
client's behalf before any complaint is opened. The Office of Bar Counsel's own public
page states directly that it is a licensing and discipline authority, not a civil court,
and that it cannot award damages, order a refund, reverse a court's decision, or provide
you with money. The same page points a complainant with a money goal toward the Fee
Dispute Committee described in section 3, as a separate and independent path a
complainant may pursue at the same time as, or instead of, a bar complaint.

This kit did not find a stated deadline for filing a bar complaint, after reading both
the Office of Bar Counsel's complaint page and the full text of the Disciplinary Rules
of Procedure. The Disciplinary Rules of Procedure do set internal deadlines once a
complaint is opened, such as a 21-day deadline for the attorney to answer a formal
complaint, but nothing found sets an outer deadline for when a client must first file.

Nevada does have a disciplinary restitution mechanism. Under the Disciplinary Rules of
Procedure, a Screening Panel reviewing bar counsel's investigation can, among other
options, issue an Admonition with conditions, and the rule text names restitution
specifically as one condition that may be attached to an Admonition, alongside a fine of
up to $1,000 and costs. Separately, the Disciplinary Rules of Procedure state that
readmission after a suspension may be conditioned on payment of costs and restitution to
injured parties, including the Clients' Security Fund described in section 5 if the
Fund already paid out on the same conduct. This restitution option is discretionary,
depends on the case reaching an Admonition or a readmission proceeding, and is not
something a complainant can request directly or count on; it is not a substitute for the
money routes in sections 3, 5, and 6.

## 5. Client protection fund

Nevada's fund is the Clients' Security Fund, established under SCR 85 and administered
by the State Bar of Nevada's Board of Governors through a volunteer committee, described
at <https://nvbar.org/csf/>. The Fund's detailed eligibility rules, exclusions, cap, and
deadline are set out in the Fund's own Rules of Procedure, a document last marked
"Updated June 2016."

The Fund reimburses a loss caused by a lawyer's dishonest act, defined to include
embezzlement or the wrongful taking or conversion of a client's money or property,
occurring while the attorney-client relationship existed and the attorney was
practicing in Nevada. It does not cover losses caused by simple negligence or
malpractice; the Rules of Procedure state this exclusion directly. A claim must show
that the accused attorney has died, been declared incompetent, disappeared, relinquished
their license, or been disbarred or suspended, or that the Committee otherwise finds
circumstances justifying consideration of the claim, and that the applicant made a
reasonable effort to recover the loss from other available sources first.

There is a deadline running from discovery: a claim must be filed no later than four
years after the claimant knew or should have known of the attorney's dishonest conduct.

The cap is $50,000.00 per applicant arising from a relationship with a given attorney.
In cases of extreme hardship or special and unusual circumstances, the Committee may
recommend to the Board of Governors that this amount be adjusted for a particular claim.
This kit did not find a separate, stated aggregate cap on what a single dishonest
lawyer's total payouts across all claimants can reach, or a fund-wide annual cap,
beyond the per-applicant figure; if the Fund itself is short of money for approved
claims in a given period, the Rules of Procedure describe pro-rating payouts rather than
paying every approved claim in full.

This kit flags the Rules of Procedure document's currency: it is dated on its face to
June 2016, and no newer version was found linked from the Bar's Clients' Security Fund
page. Treat the $50,000 figure and the four-year deadline as the best currently
available sourced figures, and confirm them directly with the Fund before relying on
either one, since a ten-year-old document could have been superseded without the public
page being updated to say so.

## 6. Small claims court

Nevada's small claims process runs inside the justice courts, the same trial courts
that handle other lower-value civil matters, under Nevada Revised Statutes Chapter 73.
Nevada is divided into justice court townships by county, so which specific justice
court you file in depends on where you live or where the dispute arose, but the
statutory dollar limit and procedure are set by state statute and do not vary by county.

The small claims jurisdictional limit is stated directly in NRS 73.010(1): a justice of
the peace has jurisdiction "in all cases arising in the justice court for the recovery
of money only, where the amount claimed does not exceed $10,000." An earlier version of
this kit said this figure had to be inferred from the filing-fee schedule; that is
wrong, the statute states the $10,000 ceiling itself. The fee schedule in NRS
4.060(1)(b) is consistent with that ceiling and separately sets the filing fees: tiered
by amount claimed, $45.00 up to $1,000, $65.00 for more than $1,000 up to $2,500, $85.00
for more than $2,500 up to $5,000, $125.00 for more than $5,000 up to $7,500, and
$175.00 for more than $7,500 up to $10,000.

Attorney's fees are generally not available in Nevada small claims court at all. NRS
73.040 states that, with two named exceptions (NRS 597.860 and 597.870, which this kit
did not research further as outside its scope), "no attorney's fees are allowed either
party to an action" covered by Chapter 73. If the case is appealed to district court and
the appealing party loses, NRS 73.050 lets the district court award the prevailing party
an attorney fee, but caps it at $15.00, a figure that has not been updated in the
statute for decades. A reader deciding whether hiring a lawyer for a Nevada small claims
case is worth the cost should know both of these before spending money on one.

A party may appear without a lawyer in a Nevada justice court small claims action; the
Justice Court Rules of Civil Procedure do not require attorney representation. This kit
did not find a rule in the Justice Court Rules of Civil Procedure specifically barring
attorney appearance in a chapter 73 small claims matter, so lawyers appear to be
permitted, but this kit also did not find an explicit statement, the way some states have
one, saying a corporation must or must not be represented by counsel in small claims
specifically.

Either party may appeal a justice court civil judgment to the district court by filing a
notice of appeal with the justice court clerk within 21 days after service of written
notice of entry of judgment (Justice Court Rules of Civil Procedure, Rule 72B(a)).
Filing a notice of appeal costs $25.00 under NRS 4.060(1)(h). Separately, Rule 73 of the
same rules requires the appellant to file a bond for costs on appeal, in the amount of
$250 unless the justice court sets a different amount or the appellant is exempt by law
or has already posted an equivalent bond; this bond is on top of, not instead of, the
$25.00 filing fee, and this kit's earlier version did not mention it.

This kit read the current Justice Court Rules of Civil Procedure (amended effective
October 29, 2024), Rules 72, 72A, and 72B, in full and did not find explicit language
stating whether the district court appeal is a trial de novo, a full new hearing, or a
review confined to the record made in the justice court. Rule 72(f) describes the
justice court clerk forwarding the docket entries and other filed documents to the
district court, which suggests a record exists to review, but the rule text does not
itself say whether the district court reviews that record or holds a new trial. This
kit is stating that boundary as unresolved rather than guessing; confirm directly with
the district court or a Nevada lawyer how an appeal from your justice court will actually
be handled before relying on it.

This kit did not find a rule specific to suing your own lawyer in Nevada small claims
court; an ordinary fee dispute is treated the same as any other small claims case.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A dispute over what you owed your lawyer, or what your lawyer owed you back, under your
fee agreement is a contract claim, and Nevada splits that period by whether the
agreement was written or not. NRS 11.190(1)(b) gives an action on a contract, obligation,
or liability founded on a written instrument six years. NRS 11.190(2)(c) gives an action
on a contract, obligation, or liability not founded on a written instrument four years.

Nevada has a dedicated statute for legal malpractice. NRS 11.207(1) gives an action
against an attorney for malpractice, whether based on a breach of duty or on contract,
four years after the plaintiff sustains damage, or two years after the plaintiff
discovers, or through reasonable diligence should have discovered, the material facts
that make up the claim, whichever occurs first. NRS 11.207(2) tolls this period for any
time during which the attorney conceals an act, error, or omission the claim is based
on, if that concealment is or reasonably should have been known to the attorney. Because
NRS 11.207 covers a malpractice claim "whether based on a breach of duty or contract,"
it appears to apply regardless of which legal theory a malpractice claim against a
lawyer is framed under, rather than leaving a separate contract-based malpractice claim
to the ordinary contract periods above; this kit did not find a case interpreting that
overlap and states it as the statute's own plain wording rather than as settled case law.

This kit does not cover running a legal malpractice claim. These periods are here so you
know a clock exists and can get counsel in time if you need it, not so you can calculate
your own deadline.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

Nevada's Rules of Professional Conduct carry the same kind of scope language as the ABA
model rule, and Nevada put it in an actual numbered rule rather than unenacted
commentary. It sits in Rule 1.0A, "Guidelines for Interpreting the Nevada Rules of
Professional Conduct," paragraph (d), not in Rule 1.0 (which is a terminology
definitions rule and does not contain this language; this kit's earlier citation to
Rule 1.0 was wrong and has been corrected). Rule 1.0A(d) states: failure to comply with
a rule is a basis for the disciplinary process, but violation of a rule "should not
itself give rise to a cause of action against a lawyer nor should it create any
presumption in such a case that a legal duty has been breached." The same paragraph
goes on to say the rules are not designed to be a basis for civil liability, and their
purpose can be subverted if opposing parties use them as procedural weapons. It then
adds the same qualifier the model rule carries: because the rules do establish
standards of conduct, a lawyer's violation of a rule may still be evidence of a breach
of the applicable standard of conduct, meaning it can be used as evidence in an
otherwise-recognized claim such as malpractice, without being a claim by itself.
Because Nevada enacted this as part of Rule 1.0A itself, rather than leaving it in
unenacted preamble commentary the way many states do, this no-private-right-of-action
rule is itself an example of the pattern described in section 1: Nevada made its
interpretive guidance an actual numbered rule.

In practical terms, filing a rule-violation complaint against your Nevada lawyer does
not by itself put money in your hands. Your actual money routes are the ones covered
above: a contract claim over the fee itself under section 7, the fee arbitration program
in section 3, the client protection fund in section 5 if the conduct was dishonest in
the sense described there, or a separate malpractice claim if that applies to you, which
is outside what this kit covers.

## Before you rely on this

Confirm the current version of these rules and figures for yourself. Start with the
Nevada Rules of Professional Conduct and the Nevada Supreme Court Rules published at
leg.state.nv.us/CourtRules, Nevada Revised Statutes Chapters 4, 11, 18, and 73 at
leg.state.nv.us/NRS, and the State Bar of Nevada's own pages for the Fee Dispute
Arbitration Program, filing a complaint, and the Clients' Security Fund at nvbar.org.
Every claim above has a row in references/verification_nv.md, with the source and the
date it was checked. If something here does not match what you find on those sites,
trust the site, not this file.
