# New Mexico: handling a dispute with your own lawyer

This section is for a person in New Mexico who has a problem with their own lawyer over
fees, billing, communication, getting their file back, or an advance payment that was never
earned or never refunded. It does not cover legal malpractice claims or how to run one
yourself. Retrieved August 20, 2026.

What is different about New Mexico:

- New Mexico cites its lawyer conduct rules as "Rule 16-105 NMRA," not a bare "Rule 1.5."
  The Rules of Professional Conduct occupy the 16-100 through 16-119 numbering block. Read
  section 1 before you cite anything in a letter.
- New Mexico's fee arbitration program, run by the State Bar, is voluntary for both sides and
  free to use. Neither side can be forced into it. Read section 3 before you count on it as a
  forced remedy.
- New Mexico's fee reasonableness factors (Rule 16-105 NMRA) match the ABA model's eight
  factors closely, and its termination rule (Rule 16-116 NMRA) uses "reasonably
  practicable," not "any and all steps necessary." A lawyer may keep a client's file over
  unpaid fees only "to the extent permitted by other law," a hook into New Mexico's own
  retaining-lien case law. Read section 2 before you write a demand letter.
- Two different courts hear money disputes up to $10,000 in New Mexico: magistrate court
  everywhere in the state, and, only in Bernalillo County (Albuquerque), the separate
  Bernalillo County Metropolitan Court. Both share the same $10,000 dollar limit. Read
  section 6.
- New Mexico's own Client Protection Fund rule sets a five-year discovery deadline for a
  claim, but does not itself state a dollar cap. The commonly cited $50,000 maximum comes
  from the Fund's own webpage, not the rule text, and the rule lets the Commission change
  that figure by policy. Read section 5.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct: numbering and adoption

The Supreme Court of New Mexico adopts and amends the state's Rules of Professional Conduct.
They are officially cited in the form "Rule 16-105 NMRA," where "NMRA" stands for New Mexico
Rules Annotated, the state's official rule-numbering system covering all of its court rules,
not just lawyer conduct. The Rules of Professional Conduct occupy the 16-100 through 16-119
numbering block within that system. New Mexico does not use the bare ABA model numbers (1.5,
1.4, 1.15, 1.16) in its own citation form, even though the rules largely track the model's
substance and structure. The fee rule the ABA calls "Rule 1.5" is New Mexico's "Rule 16-105
NMRA." Communication duties are Rule 16-104 NMRA. Safekeeping property is Rule 16-115 NMRA.

Each rule's current published text carries a heading stating "Supreme Court Approved," the
rule number, and an effective date, since the court amends rules one at a time, not as a
package. Rule 16-104 NMRA (Communication) is effective November 1, 2021; Rule 16-115 NMRA
(Safekeeping property) is effective November 1, 2023. Cite the rule number alone ("Rule
16-105 NMRA"); the effective date matters only when checking whether an online copy is
current.

New Mexico separates the body that disciplines lawyers from the body that runs the fee and
client-protection programs a client is more likely to use first. The Disciplinary Board of
the New Mexico Supreme Court investigates and prosecutes rule violations; it does not run
fee arbitration or the Client Protection Fund. The State Bar of New Mexico, acting through
its Office of General Counsel, runs the Fee Arbitration Program and, through a separate
Client Protection Fund Commission, administers the Client Protection Fund. If you want money
back rather than discipline, start with the State Bar's programs, covered in sections 3
through 5 below.

The Supreme Court's own document server, supremecourt.nmcourts.gov, hosts each rule as an
individual PDF. The New Mexico Compilation Commission also publishes the rules through
nmonesource.com, the state's official legal-research platform, as a single combined "Rule
Set 16" document covering the Preamble, Scope, and every numbered rule from 16-100 through
the 16-9xx series, at <https://nmonesource.com/nmos/nmra/en/5699/1/document.do>. Downloading
that PDF does not require a login, though some of the site's search features do. Rule 16-100
NMRA is a short terminology section defining words like "informed consent," "screened," and
"reasonable"; it does not carry the Scope commentary, which sits in the Preamble immediately
before it. Section 8 below quotes that Scope language directly.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

Rule 16-105 NMRA, New Mexico's fee rule, was read in full this session from the Supreme
Court's own compiled rule text. Its reasonableness factors in Paragraph A match the ABA
model rule's eight factors, in the same order and close to word for word: time and labor
required, and the novelty, difficulty, and skill involved; whether the matter will preclude
other work; the customary local fee; the amount involved and results obtained; time
limitations imposed by the client or circumstances; the nature and length of the
lawyer-client relationship; the lawyer's experience, reputation, and ability; and whether
the fee is fixed or contingent. This kit found no New Mexico-specific ninth factor and no
reworded standard. If your lawyer's fee looks unreasonable, these eight factors, not a
different New Mexico list, are what a court or arbitrator would weigh.

Paragraph B requires the scope of representation and the basis or rate of the fee to be
given to the client in writing before or within a reasonable time after the representation
begins, and any later change to that basis or rate to be given in writing too, unless the
lawyer already regularly represents that client on the same terms. New Mexico does not
require a signed written fee agreement for every matter, only written notice of the basis or
rate, except that a contingent fee agreement (Paragraph D) must be signed by the client.

Paragraph F, governing how two lawyers outside the same firm split a fee, requires three
things: the division is proportional to each lawyer's work, or each lawyer takes joint
responsibility for the whole representation; the client agrees to the arrangement, including
each lawyer's share, and that agreement is confirmed in writing; and the total fee is
reasonable. The rule requires the client's affirmative agreement to the split, confirmed in
writing, not silence or the absence of an objection. If you were told about a fee split only
after the fact, or never asked to agree to it, that is itself worth raising.

New Mexico's trust-account rule, Rule 16-115 NMRA (Safekeeping property), requires a lawyer
to deposit legal fees and expenses paid in advance into a client trust account, withdrawn
only as the fees are actually earned or the expenses actually incurred, matching the ABA
model rule's advance-fee requirement; this kit found no New Mexico-specific
nonrefundable-fee or flat-fee exception, and no rule letting a lawyer treat any part of an
advance fee as earned immediately on receipt. Rule 16-115 NMRA's own commentary cites two
further rules, Rule 17-204 NMRA and Rule 24-109 NMRA, establishing that New Mexico requires
lawyer trust accounts to be IOLTA accounts (interest on lawyer trust accounts).

On termination, Rule 16-116 NMRA (Declining or terminating representation) requires a
lawyer, on ending a representation, to "take steps to the extent reasonably practicable to
protect a client's interests," including reasonable notice, time to hire other counsel,
surrendering papers and property the client is entitled to, and refunding any advance
payment not earned or incurred. It is a lower, more flexible standard than "any and all
steps necessary," and it does not require a written receipt when the file is returned.

The same paragraph adds a sentence that matters most to a client trying to get their file
back: "The lawyer may retain papers relating to the client to the extent permitted by other
law." New Mexico case law answers what that other law is. In In re Behles, 2019-NMSC-016,
citing its own earlier decisions in In re Venie and Computer One, Inc. v. Grisham & Lawless,
P.A., the New Mexico Supreme Court described retaining and charging liens that attach to a
client's funds or property "only ... when the client has failed to pay the attorney for
legal services." New Mexico recognizes a lawyer's right to hold client funds or property,
potentially including a file, as security for genuinely unpaid fees, limited by that doctrine
and by Rule 16-116 NMRA's "reasonably practicable" duty not to prejudice the client
meanwhile. No case applying the doctrine to a paper file by name was found, only to funds and
property generally: a real but unsettled argument on the lawyer's side. A demand letter
should ask for the file back and cite the "reasonably practicable" duty, while being aware
the lawyer may have a colorable lien argument if fees are genuinely unpaid.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

New Mexico runs one statewide fee arbitration program, administered by the State Bar of New
Mexico's Office of General Counsel. Either the client or the lawyer may request it, and it
is a free service. Participation is voluntary for both sides: the State Bar's own program
page states directly that participation is not mandatory for either party, and if either
side declines, the arbitration does not go forward and the case is closed. New Mexico does
not use the asymmetric arrangement some states have, where the program becomes mandatory on
the lawyer once the client requests it.

The dollar range matters for how the case is heard, not whether it can be filed: amounts of
$1,000 or less are not eligible, disputes between $1,000 and $25,000 go to a single
arbitrator, and above $25,000 either party may request a panel of three, one of whom may be a
non-lawyer. There is no published ceiling above which the program will not hear a case. Once
both sides have agreed to arbitrate, the award is final and binding and may be confirmed and
enforced by a court; before that, either side can simply decline, and there is no automatic
right to a later trial once an award has issued.

The State Bar's Fee Arbitration Rules document, dated April 2020, gives a respondent twenty
business days to sign and return the arbitration agreement or be treated as having refused
to arbitrate. The State Bar's current program page, fetched this session, instead states
thirty (30) days. This kit treats the current page as the more likely accurate figure, since
it is more recently published, but could not confirm which document governs today. If the
exact number of days matters, ask the program administrator directly rather than relying on
either figure here.

This kit did not find a published deadline to request arbitration, running from the bill,
the payment, or the end of representation, or a rule requiring a lawyer to notify a client of
the right to arbitrate before suing for unpaid fees. Treat both as gaps, not confirmed
absences, and ask the program administrator before you assume neither applies to you.

To start the process, a party requests the Petition for Fee Arbitration form from the State
Bar's Office of General Counsel and files the completed, signed, and dated petition with
supporting documents. The program's own page is published at
<https://www.sbnm.org/FeeArbitration>.

## 4. Bar complaint process

The Disciplinary Board of the New Mexico Supreme Court is the state's lawyer-discipline
regulator, at nmdisboard.org. A complaint may be filed by mail, fax, or phone, free of
charge. The Board's own complaint-filing material states directly that a disciplinary
complaint's outcome is not compensation for the complainant; discipline and money are two
different tracks.

The Board's process moves through screening, investigation, and, where warranted, formal
proceedings before a hearing committee, with Board and Supreme Court review for more serious
matters, under the Rules Governing Discipline (Rule 17-1xx through 17-3xx NMRA), a separate
rule set from the numbered Rules of Professional Conduct.

Rule 17-303 NMRA, read directly this session, states this without qualification: "No statute
of limitation or other time limitation restricts filing a complaint or bringing a proceeding
under these rules, but the passage of time since an act of misconduct occurred may be
considered in determining what, if any, action or sanction is warranted." There is no
deadline to file a bar complaint in New Mexico, though an old complaint may carry less
weight.

Rule 17-206 NMRA describes sanctions short of a full formal proceeding: disciplinary counsel
may resolve a matter with a letter of caution or an informal admonition before formal
charges are filed, and may also try to resolve informally any allegation that, even if true,
would not violate the Rules of Professional Conduct but still concerns the Board. This is
not a separate consumer-assistance intake track; it is discretion built into the same
complaint process. This kit found no distinct informal track for a straightforward billing
or communication problem; ask the Board directly.

Rule 17-206 NMRA, Paragraph C, does give the disciplinary process a restitution mechanism: a
disciplined attorney "may be required to make restitution and, also, to reimburse the Client
Protection Fund ... for any expenditure it has made arising out of the attorney's
misconduct." An order of restitution "shall constitute an enforceable judgment," separate
from and not a substitute for a civil damages award. A bar complaint can, in some cases, lead
to a restitution order, not only discipline, but the complaint process stays primarily
regulatory, not compensatory; restitution is not guaranteed or the norm. Rule 17-303 NMRA
and the rest of the Rules Governing Discipline are published as the Compilation Commission's
"Rule Set 17" document at <https://nmonesource.com/nmos/nmra/en/5705/1/document.do>.

## 5. Client protection fund

New Mexico's Client Protection Fund, administered by a Client Protection Fund Commission
under the State Bar, exists to reimburse losses caused by the dishonest conduct of a lawyer
admitted and licensed to practice in New Mexico's courts.

To be eligible, Rule 17A-010 NMRA requires the loss to be caused by the lawyer's dishonest
conduct and to arise out of a client-lawyer or other fiduciary relationship. The rule
defines "dishonest conduct" as theft, embezzlement, or wrongful taking or conversion of
money or property, including failure to refund an unearned advance fee, or borrowing money
from a client without intent or reasonable ability to repay it. An ordinary fee
disagreement, without dishonest conduct, is not what this Fund is for. The rule also
excludes several categories of loss from reimbursement, including losses to a lawyer's own
family, partners, or employees, and losses already covered by a bond or insurance policy.

The rule's own five-year discovery deadline was read directly and confirmed word for word:
"The claim shall have been filed no later than five (5) years after the claimant knew or
should have known of the dishonest conduct of the lawyer" (Rule 17A-010(B) NMRA). The
commonly cited $50,000 maximum for a single claim, by contrast, did not appear in the rule
text itself. Instead, Rule 17A-014(A) NMRA gives the Commission ongoing authority to "fix a
maximum amount on reimbursement per attorney or per claim that is payable by the fund
subject to Supreme Court approval." The dollar cap is set by Commission policy, with Supreme
Court sign-off, not written into the rule as a fixed number, and can change without a rule
amendment. The Fund's own public webpage currently states the maximum is $50,000; treat that
as the Commission's current policy, not a number fixed by the rule, and confirm the current
figure with the Fund directly rather than assume it is permanent. This kit found no
published aggregate cap across all claims against one lawyer, or a per-year cap on total
payouts, in either the rule text or the Fund's webpage.

Reimbursement is discretionary by rule: "No person shall have the legal right to
reimbursement from the fund," and "there shall be no appeal from a decision of the
commission" (Rule 17A-015 NMRA). A claimant may request reconsideration in writing within
thirty days of a denial or of the amount determined (Rule 17A-013 NMRA); after that, the
decision is final. To file a claim, a claimant completes a Claim Form, supports it with
proof of payment and documents such as the fee agreement and billing records, and submits it
to the Commission. The named lawyer is notified and has twenty days to respond (Rule
17A-012(E) NMRA) before the Commission investigates and decides. The Fund's own rules, Rule
Set 17A, are published at <https://nmonesource.com/nmos/nmra/en/5723/1/document.do>; the
Fund's public page, including its current dollar figures, is at
<https://www.sbnm.org/For-Public/Client-Protection-and-Fee-Arbitration/Client-Protection-Fund>.

## 6. Small claims or equivalent court

New Mexico does not have a court literally named "small claims court." Magistrate court is
the general forum for civil money disputes up to the state's jurisdictional limit, statewide.
Bernalillo County, where Albuquerque sits, uses its own separate court instead of a
magistrate court for the same purpose: the Bernalillo County Metropolitan Court. Both courts
share the same jurisdictional dollar limit: $10,000, exclusive of interest and costs. For
magistrate court, this figure comes from NMSA 1978, Section 35-3-3(A); for metropolitan
court, the same $10,000 figure comes from NMSA 1978, Section 34-8A-3(A)(2). No county-level
variation in the magistrate court limit was found beyond Bernalillo County's use of its own
metropolitan court for the same dollar range.

Individuals may represent themselves. An official New Mexico district court self-help page
confirms attorney representation is required only for companies and corporations.

A party unhappy with a magistrate court judgment has a right to a new trial, a trial de
novo, in the district court for the county where the magistrate court sits. The deadline to
file a Notice of Appeal is fifteen days after the magistrate court files its final decision
or judgment. If a party files a timely notice, any other party may file its own notice of
appeal within ten days after the first notice was served.

A dated (Revised 10/2023) official district court self-help pamphlet states the district
court filing fee for a civil case appealed from magistrate court is $132.00, and describes no
second fee. District-level fee schedules are not perfectly consistent, though: one other
judicial district's own fee page lists a distinct "Magistrate Civil Appeal Cases" fee at
$117.00. This kit treats $132.00, from the more recently dated pamphlet, as the more likely
current figure, but confirm with the clerk of the filing district before relying on either
number.

This kit did not find a magistrate or metropolitan court rule written specifically for a
client suing a lawyer, distinct from an ordinary contract claim for money owed.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

NMSA 1978, Chapter 37, Article One sets New Mexico's general contract limitation periods. A
claim founded on a written contract must be brought within six years, under Section
37-1-3(A). A claim founded on an unwritten (oral) contract, along with several other
categories not otherwise covered in the chapter, must be brought within four years, under
Section 37-1-4. A written fee agreement dispute is a six-year claim; an oral fee arrangement,
or billing under a written agreement in a way that does not turn on the writing itself, is
more likely a four-year claim. Confirm which applies before you count on either figure.

Legal malpractice in New Mexico is governed by that same four-year catch-all in Section
37-1-4, not the separate three-year personal-injury period elsewhere in the same chapter.
This is the express holding of the New Mexico Supreme Court in Sharts v. Natelson, 118 N.M.
721, 885 P.2d 642 (1994), a legal malpractice case against the client's own lawyer that
squarely applied the four-year period in Sections 37-1-1 and 37-1-4.

New Mexico's discovery rule for legal malpractice comes from case law, not a separate
statute. The four-year clock starts once two things are both true: the client has sustained
actual injury, and the client has discovered, or through reasonable diligence should have
discovered, the facts essential to the claim. This two-part test traces to an earlier
decision, Jaramillo v. Hood, 93 N.M. 433, 601 P.2d 66 (1979), reaffirmed in Sharts, which
also declined to adopt a "continuous representation" doctrine that would pause the clock
while the same lawyer keeps representing the client; do not assume New Mexico's clock pauses
just because your lawyer is still handling your case.

This kit did not find a statute of repose specific to legal malpractice, a separate outer
deadline running regardless of when the client discovers the problem. Treat the four-year
period as the outer edge you know about, not a deadline to plan around, and get your own
lawyer's advice quickly if you suspect malpractice rather than a billing problem.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

New Mexico's Rules of Professional Conduct open with a Preamble and Scope section, read
directly this session, immediately before the numbered rules begin at Rule 16-100 NMRA. The
Scope states, in the court's own words: "Violation of a rule should not itself give rise to
a cause of action against a lawyer nor should it create any presumption in such a case that
a legal duty has been breached." It goes on to say the rules "are not designed to be a basis
for civil liability" and warns against their use "by opposing parties as procedural
weapons." This tracks the ABA model rules' own Scope language closely.

In practice, a lawyer's rule violation, on its own, is not something you sue over directly.
The actual money routes, each sourced elsewhere in this pack, are: a contract claim over the
fee itself (section 7); the State Bar's fee arbitration program (section 3); the Client
Protection Fund, for dishonest conduct rather than a plain disagreement (section 5); a bar
complaint, which can in some cases lead to a restitution order though its primary purpose is
discipline, not compensation (section 4); and a separate legal malpractice claim, which New
Mexico courts do recognize, per Sharts v. Natelson, though running one is outside what this
kit covers.
