# Delaware: handling a dispute with your own lawyer

This section is for a person in Delaware who has a problem with their own lawyer over
fees, billing, communication, getting their file back, or an advance payment that was
never earned or never refunded. It does not cover legal malpractice claims or how to run
one yourself. Retrieved August 20, 2026.

What is different about Delaware:

- Delaware's fee rule requires something the ABA model rule does not have at all: if a
  lawyer takes any fee in advance, Rule 1.5(f) requires a written statement telling the
  client the fee is refundable if not earned, and spelling out exactly when it counts as
  earned. A retainer labeled "nonrefundable" or "earned upon receipt" does not override
  this rule. Read section 2.
- Delaware has no court-run or bar-run fee arbitration program with a filing fee, a
  deadline, or a dollar ceiling written into it. It has a Delaware State Bar Association
  standing committee, the Fee Dispute Committee, and both sides have to agree in writing
  before it can decide anything, and only then is the decision binding. Read section 3
  before you count on this as a forced remedy.
- Delaware's small claims-equivalent court is the Justice of the Peace Court, a $25,000
  limit statewide, with an appeal to the Court of Common Pleas that starts the case
  completely over (a trial de novo) and costs more than the appeal rule itself suggests.
  Read section 6.
- The Lawyers' Fund for Client Protection does not publish a fixed dollar cap on what it
  will pay you. Instead its own rule caps any single payment at 10 percent of the Fund's
  balance at the time, and caps total payments in a year at 25 percent of that balance.
  Read section 5.
- A dispute over your fee agreement and a legal malpractice claim run on the exact same
  three-year deadline in Delaware, because both are swept into the same general statute,
  10 Del. C. section 8106. There is no dedicated legal malpractice limitations statute at
  all. Read section 7.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct: numbering and adoption

Delaware's lawyer conduct rules are adopted by the Delaware Supreme Court under its
inherent authority over the legal profession, published as the Delaware Lawyers' Rules of
Professional Conduct (often abbreviated DLRPC in the court's own order titles). The
numbered rules follow the same 1.0 through 8.5 numbering the ABA model rules use, so a
reader looking for "Rule 1.5," "Rule 1.4," "Rule 1.15," or "Rule 1.16" will find them
exactly where expected, unlike some states that renumber everything into a combined court
rule set. Cite them as "Rule 1.5 of the Delaware Lawyers' Rules of Professional Conduct,"
or the shorter "DLRPC Rule 1.5," which is the form the Supreme Court itself uses in order
titles.

Delaware does add two numbered rules the ABA model set does not have: Rule 1.15A, Trust
account overdraft notification, and Rule 1.17A, Dissolution of law firm. Both sit right
next to the rule they extend (1.15 and 1.17), so a reader will not need to search far to
find them, but a plain search for "the model rules" will not turn either one up, because
they are Delaware additions.

The current numbered Rules of Professional Conduct took effect July 1, 2003, replacing an
earlier Delaware code of conduct; this kit confirmed the July 1, 2003 effective date from
several of the rules' own internal revisor's notes and comment histories. The version
published as of this pack's retrieval date is current through November 18, 2025, the date
of the most recent Supreme Court order amending Rule 1.15 and the Lawyers' Fund rules. The
official publication, with comments and notes to decisions, is at
<https://courts.delaware.gov/rules/?court=Supreme> (see the "Delaware Lawyers' Rules of
Professional Conduct" panel), with the current PDF itself at
<https://courts.delaware.gov/forms/download.aspx?id=160568>.

The disciplinary structure runs through three separate bodies, each appointed by the
Supreme Court: the Office of Disciplinary Counsel (ODC), which screens and investigates;
the Preliminary Review Committee (PRC), which reviews the ODC's recommendations; and the
Board on Professional Responsibility, which holds hearings and recommends dispositions
to the Court. These bodies and their powers are set out in the Delaware Lawyers' Rules of
Disciplinary Procedure, effective March 9, 2000 and amended through October 8, 2025,
published at <https://courts.delaware.gov/forms/download.aspx?id=160798>. The Lawyers'
Fund for Client Protection is a separate body, administered by a board of Trustees under
its own set of Supreme Court rules, covered in section 5 below.

## 2. How Delaware's fee rule differs from the model

Rule 1.5(a) lists the same eight reasonableness factors as the ABA model rule, in the same
order, word for word in substance: time and labor and the difficulty of the questions
involved; whether taking the case would preclude other work; the customary local fee; the
amount involved and the result obtained; time limits imposed by the client or the
circumstances; the length of the lawyer-client relationship; the lawyer's experience,
reputation, and ability; and whether the fee is fixed or contingent. Delaware adds nothing
to this list and removes nothing from it. Rule 1.5(b), on communicating the scope and
basis of the fee, and Rule 1.5(c) and (d), on contingent fees, including the same two flat
bans on a contingent fee in a domestic relations matter or in a criminal defense, also
track the model rule closely with no material change this kit could find.

Where Delaware genuinely departs from the model is Rule 1.5(f), and this is the single
most useful thing in this section. The ABA model Rule 1.5 stops at paragraph (e). Delaware
adds an entire paragraph (f) that the model rule does not have in any form: a lawyer who
requires an advance fee payment must (1) give the client a written statement that the fee
is refundable if it is not earned, (2) state in writing the basis on which the fee will be
considered earned, in whole or in part, and (3) keep all unearned fees in the lawyer's
trust account, giving the client a statement of what was earned each time money is
withdrawn from that account. So Delaware does not let a lawyer contract around the
refundability of an unearned fee by calling it "nonrefundable" or "earned upon receipt."
If your agreement uses either phrase, Rule 1.5(f) still required your lawyer to tell you in
writing when the money becomes earned, and anything never earned by that written standard
is still your money.

Rule 1.15, safekeeping property, is also more detailed than the model rule, though this
kit found no rule that changes what property a client is entitled to. Rule 1.15(a) permits
a lawyer to keep up to $2,000 of the lawyer's own money in the client trust account solely
to cover bank charges, an amount the model rule does not specify at all. Rule 1.15(d)
requires a lawyer to keep detailed trust account books and records, including bank
statements, canceled checks, and records of every electronic transfer, for at least five
years. Delaware also adds a separate numbered rule, 1.15A on trust account overdraft
notification, which the model rule set does not have. It governs how banks report
overdrafts to the disciplinary office rather than anything a client acts on directly.

Rule 1.4 (communication) tracks the ABA model language with no material difference this kit
could find. Rule 1.16(d) (duties on discharge) also tracks the model rule closely, but its
second sentence matters and is easy to miss: after requiring the lawyer to take steps such
as "surrendering papers and property to which the client is entitled," it adds, in the same
paragraph, "The lawyer may retain papers relating to the client to the extent permitted by
other law." That is the textual hook for a common law retaining lien: it does not name one
directly, but "other law" is where one would have to come from.

This kit found real, old Delaware precedent for a retaining lien, not just a rule
cross-reference: Royal Insurance Co. v. Simon, 174 A. 444, 446 (Del. Ch. 1934) and Eagle
Poultry Co. v. Camden Fire Insurance Ass'n, 1963 WL 64648 (Del. Ch. July 18, 1963). The
Court of Chancery applied and refined that doctrine in Judy v. Preferred Communication
Systems, Inc., 29 A.3d 248 (Del. Ch. 2011), where a firm withheld discovery documents from
a former client over unpaid bills. This kit could not reach the court's own text of Judy
through an official Delaware archive or CourtListener's opinion-text service (both attempts
are logged in references/verification_de.md), so it read the opinion through a commercial
case law site instead. That is not a primary source, so treat this as sourced but not
independently confirmed; see the flagged table.

What that source shows: the lien is real but not absolute. The court weighed the lawyer's
interest against the client's need for the documents and the court's interest in moving the
case along, using factors including the client's ability to pay, prejudice to the client,
and whether alternative security would protect the lawyer as well as holding the file does.
The firm did not get to keep the documents outright; the court ordered them released once
the client posted security, cash or a bond equal to 70% of the disputed fee. This kit found
no Delaware ethics opinion addressing file retention for unpaid fees.

In practical terms: if your Delaware lawyer is holding your file over unpaid fees, that is
not automatically a rule violation, but it is not an unconditional right either. A court can
order the file released while you post security for the disputed amount instead of paying
it in full up front. If your case is active and you need the file, that is a reason to talk
to another lawyer or the court directly, not just to wait on the routes below, which settle
who owes what but do not by themselves get your file back quickly.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

Delaware does not run a court-administered or bar-mandated fee arbitration program with
its own dollar limits, filing fee, or filing deadline set by rule. What it has is the Fee
Dispute Committee, a standing committee of the Delaware State Bar Association established
under Section 6.14 of the DSBA's Bylaws, which hears disputes both between a lawyer and a
client and between successor lawyers on the same matter. The DLRPC's own comment [14] to
Rule 1.5 is what gives the Committee its footing: it says that if a bar procedure for
resolving fee disputes exists, a lawyer must comply when it is mandatory and should
conscientiously consider it when it is voluntary. Delaware's own program is the voluntary
kind. The Committee's page is at
<https://www.dsba.org/sections-committees/standing-committees/fee-dispute/>, and its full
rules and forms are published at
<https://media1.dsba.org/public/media/pdfs/FDCUnitaryRulesAppendices.pdf>.

Participation is not mandatory on either side. A petitioner starts the process by signing
and delivering a Petition and an Agreement to Submit to Common Law Arbitration. The
Committee's Chairperson sends both to the other party (the Respondent) with a request to
participate, and the Respondent has 20 days to respond, extendable another 30 days for
good cause. If the Respondent, lawyer or client, does not agree to arbitrate, the rules do
not give the Committee any way to compel participation. Where the Respondent is a lawyer,
the Chairperson's only tool is to "attempt to persuade the attorney to agree to submit in
the best interests of the profession," which is persuasion, not compulsion. Only where a
submission has been signed by both parties does the Committee's Decision and Award become,
in the rules' own words, "final and binding on the parties."

The Committee excludes three categories from its jurisdiction: matters already subject to
resolution by a court or administrative agency, matters where the fee is fixed by law, and
matters involving a question of ethical misconduct that falls within the jurisdiction of
the Board on Professional Responsibility. There is no dollar floor or ceiling on what the
Committee will hear, but the amount in controversy changes who decides it: a single
attorney member decides disputes up to $100,000, and a two-member panel, including one
layperson, decides disputes above that amount. This kit did not find any filing fee stated
anywhere in the Committee's published rules or forms, and did not find any stated filing
deadline either; the rules are silent on both points, so confirm current practice directly
with the Committee before you rely on there being no fee or no deadline. This kit also did
not find any rule requiring a lawyer to notify a client of the right to use this Committee
before suing the client for fees.

If your dispute is not one the Committee will hear, or the other side declines to
arbitrate and cannot be made to, your money routes are the Justice of the Peace Court or a
higher civil court described in section 6, or the client protection fund in section 5 if
the conduct involved dishonesty rather than an ordinary billing disagreement.

## 4. Filing a bar complaint

Delaware's disciplinary regulator is the Office of Disciplinary Counsel (ODC), which
operates under the Delaware Supreme Court and screens and investigates complaints against
lawyers admitted or practicing in Delaware. Complaints are filed using the ODC's own
Complaint Form, submitted electronically where possible, published with full instructions
at <https://courts.delaware.gov/odc/complaint.aspx>. The ODC's own instructions state
plainly, before you even get to the form, that "ODC will not adjudicate fee disputes
between lawyers and their clients," and directs anyone with a fee amount dispute to the
DSBA Fee Dispute Committee covered in section 3 instead. That single line is the clearest
statement in any Delaware source this kit found of the difference between a discipline
complaint and a money remedy: filing an ODC complaint over your bill will not get your
money back, and the ODC's own process tells you where to go instead.

This kit did not find a separate informal or consumer-assistance intake track inside the
Delaware disciplinary system, comparable to a program that contacts your lawyer informally
before a complaint becomes formal. The process runs through one channel: you file the
Complaint Form, the ODC screens it for a plausible rule violation, and if it finds one, it
may seek a response from the lawyer, gather information, and either dismiss the matter,
resolve it informally, or move toward formal proceedings before the Board on Professional
Responsibility. There is no time limit on filing a disciplinary complaint in Delaware. Rule
26 of the Delaware Lawyers' Rules of Disciplinary Procedure says so directly: "There shall
be no statute of limitations with respect to any proceedings under these Rules."

Delaware's disciplinary sanctions do include a mechanism that can put money back in a
client's hands, which not every state has. Rule 8(a)(9) of the Disciplinary Procedure
rules lists, among the possible sanctions the Court can impose, "Court-ordered restitution
to persons financially injured and Court-ordered reimbursement to the Lawyers' Fund for
Client Protection." This is discretionary, tied to a sanction the Court decides to impose,
and not something a complainant can request as of right or count on receiving. It sits
alongside, and does not replace, the fee arbitration, client protection fund, and small
claims routes described elsewhere in this pack.

## 5. Client protection fund

Delaware's fund is the Lawyers' Fund for Client Protection of the Supreme Court of
Delaware, governed by the Trustees' own rules and administered by a board of Trustees with
help from an Executive Director. The current rules, in force through the November 18, 2025
amendment order, are published at
<https://courts.delaware.gov/forms/download.aspx?id=32698>, with the amendment order
itself and the Supreme Court's rules index at
<https://courts.delaware.gov/rules/?court=Supreme>.

The Fund considers claims for losses arising from (1) the misappropriation or dishonesty
of a member of the Delaware Bar, or (2) the death or incapacity of a Delaware lawyer. The
spouse, law partner, attorney stockholder in a professional corporation, associate, or
conspirator of the lawyer at fault has no right to file a claim. Claims must be filed
within two years after the claimant discovered, or should have discovered, the
misappropriation or dishonesty (this two-year period is discovery-based, not a flat
two years from the loss itself). No claim can be recognized for conduct before January 1,
1967, which will not affect a current dispute.

Delaware's caps are not published as fixed dollar figures at all, which is a real
difference from most states. Rule 3(f) of the Trustees' rules caps any single payment at
no more than 10 percent of the Lawyers' Fund's balance at the time of payment, and caps
the total of all payments made to claimants in a calendar year at no more than 25 percent
of that balance. This kit did not find any published current balance of the Fund, so it
could not translate these percentages into an actual dollar amount; you would need to ask
the Fund's Executive Director directly for both the current balance and any indication of
what a given claim might realistically receive. The rule also lets the Trustees carry
forward unpaid portions of an allowed claim to future years, and gives the Trustees broad
discretion, including considering the size and number of other claims likely to come in,
the claimant's hardship, and whether the claimant's own negligence contributed to the
loss. No claim is allowed to the extent the lawyer was bonded or the loss otherwise
insured. Under Rule 3(i), no person has a legal right to reimbursement from the Fund, and
there is no appeal from a Trustees' decision. This kit found no requirement in the rules
that a disciplinary complaint be filed or resolved first as a precondition to a Fund
claim.

An ordinary billing disagreement, where the lawyer did the work but you dispute the
amount, is not what this Fund covers. It covers dishonest conduct: theft of client money,
a lawyer who took a retainer and vanished, that kind of loss. Fee disputes belong in
sections 3 and 6.

## 6. Small claims court

Delaware does not have a single court called small claims. The Justice of the Peace Court
is the limited-jurisdiction civil trial court for smaller claims, and the Court of Common
Pleas hears the appeal.

The Justice of the Peace Court's civil jurisdiction is capped at $25,000 by statute, 10
Del. C. section 9301(1): "Jurisdiction over such actions shall be limited to actions in
which the matter in demand, damage claimed, or the value of the property whose return is
sought does not exceed $25,000." If your claim is worth more, you may still file there, but
you give up anything above $25,000. The statute sets one number for the whole state, so
there is no county variation in the limit. The statute is published at
<https://delcode.delaware.gov/title10/c093/index.html>, and the Justice of the Peace
Court's own self-help page confirming the $25,000 figure and describing the process is at
<https://courts.delaware.gov/help/proceedings/jp_startcivil.aspx>.

An individual does not need a lawyer to appear in Justice of the Peace Court. A
corporation or other entity generally does need one, unless it files a Certificate of
Representation under Supreme Court Rule 57, renewed annually for a $20 registration fee,
which lets a non-lawyer representative appear instead.

Filing fees scale with the amount claimed and the type of claim, under the Court's own fee
schedule published at <https://courts.delaware.gov/help/fees/jpfees.aspx> (10 Del. C.
section 9801). A fee dispute lawsuit, whether the client is suing for a refund or the
lawyer is suing for unpaid fees, would fall under the schedule's general "Debt Claims"
category: $35 where the amount in controversy is under $1,000, $40 between $1,000 and
$5,000, and $45 above $5,000.

Either side may appeal a Justice of the Peace Court civil judgment to the Court of Common
Pleas in the same county, and the appeal is a trial de novo, meaning the case starts over
completely rather than being reviewed on the existing record. Court of Common Pleas Civil
Rule 72.3(a) is the rule that makes it de novo. The appellant (whichever side appeals) must
file a Notice of Appeal, a Praecipe, a Summons on Appeal, and a Complaint within 15 days of
the Justice of the Peace Court's decision, and within 10 additional days must pay the
filing fee and a separate sheriff's service fee.

The appeal costs more than the filing fee alone. The self-help appeal instructions give a
non-refundable $135 filing fee, including the Court Security Assessment, and the Court of
Common Pleas fee schedule separately lists "Justice of the Peace Court Appeal $135.00," so
the two agree. On top of that comes a separate non-refundable sheriff's service fee: $30
for the first address served, $30 for each additional address, and $5 for each additional
person served at the same address. For one defendant at one address the realistic total is
about $165, not $135. The appeal instructions are published at
<https://courts.delaware.gov/help/appeals/ccpjpplaintiff.aspx>, and the Court of Common
Pleas fee schedule confirming the $135 figure is at
<https://courts.delaware.gov/help/fees/ccpfees.aspx>. This kit did not confirm whether a
further appeal from the Court of Common Pleas trial de novo decision to the Superior Court
is available in an ordinary civil case, or what that would cost, and stopped once it had
confirmed the JP-to-CCP path most readers will actually use.

This kit did not find any rule specific to suing your own lawyer in either court; an
ordinary fee dispute is treated the same as any other civil claim of that size.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

Delaware does not separate a fee agreement dispute from a legal malpractice claim for
limitations purposes the way some states do, because it has no dedicated legal malpractice
limitations statute at all. Both run on the same general period, 10 Del. C. section 8106,
which gives a flat three years, running from the accruing of the cause of action, to "no
action based on a promise" (which covers most contract claims, written or oral, that are
not under seal) and, in the same subsection, to "no action to recover damages caused by an
injury unaccompanied with force," a catch-all that covers ordinary negligence claims,
including legal malpractice. In practical terms, whether you frame your claim as a breach
of your fee agreement or as malpractice, the deadline is the same three years in Delaware,
which is not true everywhere. There is one narrow exception worth knowing: under section
8106(c), a written contract, agreement, or undertaking involving at least $100,000 can set
its own limitation period in the writing itself, as long as it does not exceed 20 years
from accrual; this would only matter if your fee agreement is written, involves at least
$100,000, and specifically addresses its own limitations period, which is unusual.

This kit searched Chapter 81 of Title 10, Delaware's general limitations chapter, for a
malpractice-specific provision and found only one: 10 Del. C. section 8128, which sets the
limitations period for "health care malpractice" claims by cross-reference to 18 Del. C.
section 6856, a statute for medical providers, not lawyers. No comparable section exists
for legal malpractice, so the general three-year period in section 8106 is what governs.

This kit did not find a codified discovery rule inside Chapter 81 that applies to legal
malpractice specifically. Delaware courts have recognized a "time of discovery" or
"inherently unknowable injury" rule in at least one professional-negligence context; this
kit located a real Delaware Supreme Court case on this general subject, Coleman v.
PricewaterhouseCoopers, LLC, decided in 2004, through CourtListener's case search, but
could not retrieve the opinion's full text (CourtListener's opinion-text endpoint returned
empty), and did not independently confirm how, or whether, its holding extends to legal
malpractice specifically. Treat any discovery-rule argument in a Delaware legal
malpractice matter as a question for your own lawyer to research from the actual case law,
not as something this pack has confirmed. This kit also found no statute of repose in
Chapter 81 that applies to legal malpractice.

This kit does not cover running a legal malpractice claim. These periods are here so you
know a clock exists and can get counsel in time if you need it, not so you can calculate
your own deadline.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

The Delaware Lawyers' Rules of Professional Conduct carry the same Scope language as the
ABA model rule, in paragraph [20] of the Scope section that precedes the numbered rules:
"Violation of a Rule should not itself give rise to a cause of action against a lawyer nor
should it create any presumption in such a case that a legal duty has been breached." The
same paragraph adds that the rules are not designed to be a basis for civil liability, and
that a rule violation does not necessarily support any other nondisciplinary remedy, such
as disqualifying a lawyer in pending litigation.

In practical terms, filing a rule-violation complaint against your Delaware lawyer does
not by itself put money in your hands. Your actual money routes are the ones covered
above: the fee arbitration process in section 3, a contract claim over the fee itself in
Justice of the Peace Court or the Court of Common Pleas under section 6, the client
protection fund in section 5 if the conduct involved dishonesty in the sense described
there, court-ordered restitution as part of a disciplinary sanction under section 4 if the
ODC pursues a case that far, or a separate legal malpractice claim if that applies to you,
which is outside what this kit covers.

## Before you rely on this

Confirm the current version of these rules and figures for yourself. Start with the
Delaware Lawyers' Rules of Professional Conduct and the Delaware Lawyers' Rules of
Disciplinary Procedure at courts.delaware.gov/rules, the Delaware Code Title 10 at
delcode.delaware.gov, and the Delaware State Bar Association's own pages for the Fee
Dispute Committee at dsba.org. Every claim above has a row in
references/verification_de.md, with the source and the date it was checked. If something
here does not match what you find on those sites, trust the site, not this file.
