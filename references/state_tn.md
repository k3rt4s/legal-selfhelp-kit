# Tennessee

This pack is for a person in Tennessee who has a fee, billing, communication, or file-return
dispute with their own lawyer. It does not cover legal malpractice as a claim to run yourself.
Retrieved 2026-08-10, with several rows updated 2026-08-19 as noted in
[verification_tn.md](verification_tn.md).

What a Tennessee reader most needs to know, that a reader in another state does not:

- Tennessee has no statewide fee arbitration program. Only a handful of local committees
  exist, and most counties have none.
- Tennessee's ten reasonableness factors for a fee add two the model rule does not have:
  whether the lawyer previously advertised or made statements about the fee, and whether the
  fee agreement is in writing.
- There is no deadline for filing a Board of Professional Responsibility complaint. Tennessee
  courts have held that laches does not apply to attorney discipline at all.
- The Client Protection Fund has two separate clocks: a 60-day gate tied to having a Board
  complaint on file, and a 3-year/5-year loss-based deadline that runs independently of it.
- General Sessions court, Tennessee's small claims equivalent, has a $25,000 civil limit, and
  that figure is statewide. Attorney fees and court costs do not count against it.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct, numbering and adoption

Tennessee adopts the Rules of Professional Conduct as Rule 8 of the Rules of the Supreme Court
of Tennessee. Cite them as "RPC" followed by the rule number, for example "RPC 1.5," not
"Model Rule 1.5." Tennessee does not renumber the rules a client is most likely to need: RPC
1.4 (communication), RPC 1.5 (fees), RPC 1.15 (safekeeping property), and RPC 1.16 (declining
or terminating representation) all sit at the same numbers a reader would expect from the
model rules.

The official publication point is the Tennessee Administrative Office of the Courts, at
<https://www.tncourts.gov/rules/supreme-court/8>. That page has been unreachable to automated
retrieval on every attempt, including a retry on 2026-08-19 by direct fetch, by a

does and does not support.

The version checked is the Tennessee Bar Association's *Tennessee Rules of Professional

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

Tennessee lists ten factors for whether a fee is reasonable. Eight track the ABA model rule.
Two do not:

9. Prior advertisements or statements by the lawyer about the fees the lawyer charges. If the
   lawyer advertised a rate and then billed differently, this factor speaks to it directly.
10. Whether the fee agreement is in writing. Without a written agreement, that absence itself
    becomes relevant to whether the fee is reasonable, rather than something the client has to
    argue around separately.

These two factors, 9 and 10, are the single most useful items in this pack for a Tennessee
client comparing a bill against what was promised or written down.

RPC 1.5(b) requires the scope of representation and the basis or rate of the fee to be
communicated to the client, preferably in writing, before or within a reasonable time after
the representation begins, with the same requirement applying to any later change in the
basis or rate. A rate increase you were never told about is a communication problem and a
reasonableness problem at the same time.

RPC 1.5(c) requires a contingent fee agreement to be in writing, signed by the client, and to
state the method for determining the fee, including the percentages on settlement, at trial,
and on appeal; which expenses are deducted from the recovery; and whether those expenses come
out before or after the fee is calculated. At the end of the matter the lawyer must give the
client a written closing statement showing the outcome and how any remittance was calculated.
If you never received that statement, you can ask for it in writing.

Contingent fees are prohibited in two situations: a domestic relations matter where payment or
amount is contingent on securing a divorce, on custodial rights, or on the amount of alimony,
support, or a property division (unless the matter is only about collecting arrearages or
enforcing a division of the marital estate, and the arrangement is disclosed to the court), and
criminal defense representation.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

Tennessee has no statewide attorney fee arbitration or mediation program. Fee dispute handling
is run by local bar associations and varies by county; many counties offer nothing at all. The
Board of Professional Responsibility's Consumer Assistance Program page lists the local
committees it knows of, at
<https://www.tbpr.org/for-the-public/consumer-assistance-program>.

Programs exist in or around: Memphis, Knoxville, Chattanooga, Bristol, Kingsport, Montgomery
County, and Washington County. This list may be incomplete, and each program's own rules,
whether both sides must agree to participate, and any fee to file should be confirmed directly
with the local bar association before you rely on it.

Filing deadlines are not uniformly confirmed:

- No filing deadline was found for the Knoxville
  (<https://www.knoxbar.org/?pg=fee-dispute-resolution>), Memphis
  (<https://www.memphisbar.org/?pg=fee-disputes>), or Chattanooga
  (<https://www.chattanoogabar.org/how-to-file-a-fee-dispute>) programs, confirmed directly
  against each program's own materials.
- For Bristol, Kingsport, Montgomery County, and Washington County, no deadline appears in the
  Board's own consolidated description of these programs, but each program's own rules could
  not be independently located and read. Ask the local bar association directly before you
  assume there is no deadline.

If your county has a program, it is usually the cheapest and fastest path for a genuine
disagreement about the size of a bill. If it does not, the realistic path is a written demand
followed by General Sessions court (Section 6).

## 4. Bar complaint process

The Board of Professional Responsibility is Tennessee's disciplinary body, at
<https://www.tbpr.org>, public line 1-800-486-5714 (confirm before using). It runs the
Consumer Assistance Program, or CAP, as an intake point for the public. CAP describes itself as
a clearinghouse: it identifies the problem and finds the resource that can help.

CAP can give process information, contact your lawyer including a letter of concern if the
lawyer has not responded to you, informally mediate a dispute, and refer the matter to a fee
dispute committee, a lawyer referral service, or Disciplinary Counsel. CAP will not give legal
advice, recommend a lawyer, or intervene in a pending court matter, and it declines matters
involving serious misconduct such as theft of client funds, which go to Disciplinary Counsel
instead. If your lawyer does not respond to your written correspondence, CAP will follow up
once the lawyer has gone unanswered for ten days.

When you file, at <https://www.tbpr.org/for-the-public/file-complaint>, the intake form asks
you to choose between a formal complaint, for alleged ethical violations of a serious nature,
and an informal request for assistance, for concerns that do not rise to that level. A billing
disagreement is usually the informal track. Choosing "formal complaint" for what is really a
billing disagreement tends to produce a closed file, not a refund.

There is no deadline for filing a disciplinary complaint. The Tennessee Supreme Court held in
*Long v. Board of Professional Responsibility*, Tenn. 2014, No. M2013-01042-SC-R3-BP, at
<https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/tn-supreme-court/1669117.html>, that
laches, the defense that a claim was brought too late, does not apply to attorney disciplinary
proceedings at all, because the Board's purpose is protecting the public rather than deciding
a private claim. If you are wondering whether it is too late to complain about something years
old, it is not; the complaint itself has no clock.

Discipline is not a refund. It puts the lawyer's conduct on record and can lead to sanctions,
but getting money back runs through a separate forum: a contract claim, fee arbitration where
available, or the Client Protection Fund (Section 5).

RPC 1.4 requires a lawyer to promptly inform the client of decisions needing the client's
consent, to reasonably consult about how objectives will be pursued, to keep the client
reasonably informed of status, to promptly comply with reasonable requests for information,
and to explain a matter enough for the client to make informed decisions. Most complaints that
feel like a fee problem start as a communication problem: unanswered calls, no status updates,
and a surprise invoice are one story, not three.

RPC 1.16(d) covers what a lawyer must do on discharge or withdrawal, and applies as far as
reasonably practicable:

1. Giving reasonable notice.
2. Allowing time to hire other counsel.
3. Cooperating with successor counsel.
4. Promptly surrendering papers and property the client is entitled to, along with any work
   product the lawyer has already been paid for.
5. Surrendering any other work product the lawyer prepared, except to the extent other law
   lets the lawyer keep it.
6. Refunding any advance payment of fee or expense that has not been earned or incurred.

These are six separate steps, not five; surrendering papers and property the client is
entitled to is distinct from surrendering other work product. A request for your file and a
request for a refund of unearned fees are separate requests, and you can make both in the same
written discharge letter. If the lawyer ignores that letter, CAP is the next step.

If the underlying problem looks like a lawyer's impairment rather than a fee dispute, the
Tennessee Lawyer Assistance Program, (877) 424-8527, is a help resource for lawyers, not a
client remedy, but it explains why some conduct gets handled the way it does.

## 5. Client protection fund

The Tennessee Lawyers' Fund for Client Protection reimburses losses caused by a Tennessee
lawyer's dishonest conduct, at <https://tlfcp.tn.gov>, line (615) 741-3097. It is not a fee
dispute forum and it is not malpractice insurance.

What it covers: unearned fees where the lawyer showed a pattern of behavior evidencing no
good-faith intention to perform the services, or committed a clear disciplinary violation
involving dishonest conduct; and certain losses on an "investment" that would not have
happened but for the attorney-client relationship.

What it excludes: fee disputes. The Board distinguishes a fee dispute from dishonest conduct
by asking whether the lawyer did any work, whether the billing was reasonable, and whether
there was actual dishonesty rather than a breach of contract. The Fund's policies and rules are
published at
<https://tlfcp.tn.gov/policies-and-rules-of-the-tennessee-lawyers-fund-for-client-protection/>.

RPC 1.15 requires client funds to be held separately from the lawyer's own funds, in a trust
account, and unearned fees paid in advance generally belong in trust until earned. If a lawyer
took a retainer, did little or no work, and the money is gone, that is a materially different
problem from a billing disagreement. It is a trust account and dishonesty question, and it
belongs in front of the Board and the Fund rather than in a negotiation.

Two deadlines apply, and missing either can end a claim before it reaches the merits:

1. The 60-day gate: a written complaint must be on file with the Board. If the Board notifies
   the Fund that no complaint is on file, you have 60 days from that notice to file one, or the
   Fund claim is dismissed.
2. The loss-based deadline: for a loss on or after October 1, 2013, a claim must be filed
   within 3 years of the loss or of when you reasonably should have discovered it, and the Fund
   will not consider a claim more than 5 years after the loss no matter when it was discovered.
   A different, older rule applies to losses before October 1, 2013. See the Fund's FAQ page,
   <https://tlfcp.tn.gov/faq/>.

File the Board complaint promptly, then file with the Fund; waiting to see how the Board
complaint turns out before starting the Fund claim risks the 3-year and 5-year clocks.

There is no published fixed maximum award. Approved claims are paid
at a percentage set at the Board's discretion, based on the fund balance and budget, so a
valid claim does not guarantee full reimbursement.

## 6. Small claims or equivalent court

Tennessee's small claims equivalent is General Sessions court, designed so a non-lawyer can
appear without counsel.

The civil jurisdictional limit is $25,000, and it is the same in every county. Attorney fees,
court costs, and discretionary costs are not counted when deciding whether a judgment exceeds
that limit, so a claim close to the ceiling is not pushed over it by the cost of bringing it.

Some Tennessee counties do have special General Sessions provisions set by population bracket,
but those grant the court concurrent jurisdiction over particular case types such as workers'
compensation, divorce, and domestic relations. They do not change the money limit.

No rule specific to suing a lawyer in General Sessions was found. Confirm the current limit and
the filing fee with the clerk in the county where you plan to file; limits are set by statute
and change.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A fee agreement dispute, such as a lawyer's failure to refund an unearned fee, is a contract
claim. Tennessee's statute of limitations for a contract claim not otherwise covered by a more
specific statute is six years from the breach, at Tenn. Code Ann. § 28-3-109(a)(3), reproduced
at <https://codes.findlaw.com/tn/title-28-limitation-of-actions/tn-code-sect-28-3-109.html>.
The clock
generally starts at the breach itself. A narrow exception can push the start date later for a
breach that is genuinely undiscoverable, but Tennessee courts have treated that exception as
limited, so do not count on it to extend the six years.

Legal malpractice, a claim that the lawyer's own negligence caused you harm, needs its own
counsel rather than self-help. It is not covered further in this kit beyond the deadline,
included so you know a clock exists and can get counsel in time. Two periods apply at once, at
Tenn. Code Ann. § 28-3-104(c): one year from when you discovered, or reasonably should have
discovered, the injury and that the lawyer's negligence caused it, and an outer limit of five
years from the lawyer's act or omission regardless of when you discovered it. The five-year
repose period yields only if the lawyer fraudulently concealed the wrongdoing, in which case
you get one year from discovering the concealment. Even a claim you discovered promptly can
already be dead if the underlying act happened more than five years ago. Tenn. Code Ann.
§ 28-3-104 is reproduced at
<https://codes.findlaw.com/tn/title-28-limitation-of-actions/tn-code-sect-28-3-104/>.

Both statutes above were read at codes.findlaw.com, a commercial mirror marked current as of
January 2, 2024, not the official Tennessee Code site. The official Tennessee Code was not
independently checked against this reproduction. See the flagged table in
[verification_tn.md](verification_tn.md).

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

The Rules of Professional Conduct are a disciplinary code. Tennessee does not give a client a
private cause of action for a rules violation. A violation can support a Board complaint and
can inform a fee dispute, but it is not by itself a claim for damages.

Your actual money route runs through the other forums in this pack instead: a contract claim
in General Sessions or another court (Section 6), local fee arbitration where available
(Section 3), or the Client Protection Fund where the conduct was dishonest rather than merely
a bad bill (Section 5). Getting the lawyer disciplined (Section 4) is a separate goal from
getting money back, and it runs through a separate forum.
