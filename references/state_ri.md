# Rhode Island

This pack is for someone in a dispute with their own lawyer over fees, billing,
communication, getting their file back, or an unearned advance payment. It does not cover
suing a lawyer for malpractice. Retrieval date: 2026-08-20.

Read [national_baseline.md](national_baseline.md) first. This file covers only what Rhode
Island does differently, plus its own programs and deadlines.

What a reader in Rhode Island most needs to know:

- Rhode Island's Rule 1.5(b) requires the fee basis to be communicated to the client in
  writing, not merely "preferably in writing" as the ABA model says, and it separately
  requires quarterly billing on any fee that is not fixed or contingent.
- Rhode Island's fee arbitration program, run by the Rhode Island Bar Association, is
  voluntary for both sides. If your lawyer will not sign the agreement, no panel is
  appointed and you are left to your ordinary remedies; no filing fee was found in the
  rules.
- Rhode Island's Lawyer's Fund for Client Reimbursement generally excludes unearned advance
  fees, unless you can show both that the lawyer did no further work after taking the fee
  and that the lawyer never intended to do the work when the fee was taken.
- Rhode Island's small claims appeal right is asymmetric. A plaintiff who uses this chapter
  must waive the right to appeal as a condition of filing. Only the defendant can appeal,
  and that appeal is a trial de novo in superior court, with its own filing costs.
- Rhode Island's legal malpractice claims have their own 3-year limitation period,
  independent of the general 10-year contract period, with a discovery-rule exception.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct, numbering and adoption

Rhode Island's Rules of Professional Conduct are Article V of the Rules of the Supreme Court
of Rhode Island, adopted and amended by order of the Rhode Island Supreme Court. Cite a rule
the way most readers will recognize it: "Rule 1.5" or "RPC 1.5." Rhode Island uses standard
Model Rule numbering for the rules this kit covers: 1.4 (communication), 1.5 (fees), 1.15
(safekeeping property), and 1.16 (declining or terminating representation), in the same
place as the ABA model.

The official full text is published by the Rhode Island Judiciary at
https://www.courts.ri.gov/Courts/SupremeCourt/Documents/Supreme-Rules-Article5.pdf, marked
"Revised October 2023," but that PDF is stale on one point: a Supreme Court order entered
June 17, 2026 amended Rule 1.1's technology-competence comment to address generative AI, and
the posted PDF still shows the pre-amendment text. Rules 1.4, 1.5, 1.15, 1.16, and the
Preamble and Scope, the only provisions this pack relies on, were not touched by that order
or any other 2024-2026 amendment found, so the text quoted here is current; confirm any
other rule against the court's orders, not the PDF's header date. The date the current
numbering scheme was first adopted was not independently confirmed and is noted in the gap
list below.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

Rhode Island Rule 1.5(a) lists the same eight reasonableness factors as the ABA model, in
the same order and substantially the same wording; no deviation was found there. Two real
deviations exist in Rule 1.5(b), and the first is the most consequential single fact in this
pack:

1. Mandatory writing requirement. The model rule says the scope of representation and the
   fee basis should "preferably" be communicated in writing. Rhode Island's Rule 1.5(b)
   drops that qualifier: "The scope of the representation and the basis or rate of the fee
   and expenses for which the client will be responsible shall be communicated to the
   client, in writing, before or within a reasonable time after commencing the
   representation," with the same regularly-represented-client exception the model rule has.
   If your Rhode Island lawyer never gave you anything in writing about the fee basis, that
   is itself a rule violation, not just a best-practices lapse.
2. Quarterly billing requirement. Rhode Island Rule 1.5(b) adds a sentence the model rule
   does not have: "Where the fee is not fixed or contingent, billings regarding the fees,
   costs, and expenses shall be provided to the client on a quarterly basis or as otherwise
   provided in the agreement." If you were on an hourly or similar open-ended fee and went a
   long stretch without any bill, and your agreement did not set a different billing
   schedule, that silence is itself a deviation from the rule.

Rule 1.16(d) on ending the representation tracks the model rule's structure and content,
including the same "papers and property to which the client is entitled" language and the
same closing sentence: "The lawyer may retain papers relating to the client to the extent
permitted by other law." No separate Rhode Island rule comparable to a state's own numbered
"client file" rule was found; Rhode Island answers the file-retention question instead
through this "other law" clause together with Ethics Advisory Panel opinions and the general
statutes described next.

On whether a lawyer can hold your file for unpaid fees: four primary sources bear on this,
and together they point against it.

- The Ethics Advisory Panel (EAP), created by the Supreme Court in 1986 to advise lawyers on
  the Rules of Professional Conduct, has repeatedly held that "a client-file is the property
  of the client," that a client is entitled to the file's contents excluding the lawyer's
  work product, and that a lawyer may not charge for copying the file when returning it. EAP
  Op. 2026-01, 2026-04, and 2026-08 (all 2026, read in full) trace this back to earlier
  opinions including EAP Op. 2010-06, 92-88, 93-84, 90-19, and 96-35.
- EAP Op. 96-35 (Nov. 14, 1996), the opinion those three cite and the one the EAP's subject
  index lists under "Attorney lien upon termination," was located through an archived copy
  of the court's site and read in full. A discharged attorney asked whether he could bill a
  client for retrieving and transferring the file, and whether he could still claim a lien
  for services already performed. The Panel held he could not charge for the transfer,
  because then-Rule 1.17(d), renumbered since as today's Rule 1.16(d), requires surrendering
  the client's papers on termination, but that he could separately file an attorney's lien
  for the reasonable value of services performed before discharge. The opinion treats
  surrender of the file and the lien as separate: one owed regardless, the other a claim
  pursued afterward. It never says the file itself may be held back until fees are paid.
- Rhode Island's statutory attorney's lien is G.L. section 9-3-1 through 9-3-3 (a fourth,
  unrelated section in the same chapter, 9-3-4, is a hospital's lien on a personal injury
  recovery, not part of the attorney's lien). These sections create a lien "to the value of
  [the attorney's] contractual interests" attaching to "the cause of action, claim, demand,
  counterclaim, or other matter," enforced against the money or thing of value that is the
  consideration for a settlement or judgment: a charging lien on money recovered, not a
  retaining lien on papers, and EAP Op. 96-35 treats it the same way.
- G.L. section 8-14-2 does not require an attorney to keep a file for seven years; it
  permits destroying case records after seven years have passed since disposition. Read with
  the surrender duty, that means a lawyer cannot destroy your file early, not that the file
  is owed only after a waiting period.

Taken together, no rule or statute this pack could reach lets a Rhode Island lawyer
condition return of your file on payment. Rule 1.16(d) requires surrendering papers "to the
extent permitted by other law," and no other law permitting retention for unpaid fees was
found; EAP Op. 96-35 treats surrender and the lien as separate obligations; and the
statutory lien reaches money, not papers. This pack does not assert an absolute rule, since
no opinion addresses a lawyer outright refusing to hand over a file until paid, but the
weight of the sources runs against a right to withhold.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

Rhode Island runs one statewide program, the Fee Arbitration Committee of the Rhode Island
Bar Association, under the Rules for Proceeding Before the Fee Arbitration Committee,
published at https://ribar.com/?pg=fee-arbitration and effective January 1, 1996.

- Who can start it: either the client or the lawyer can file a Petition for Arbitration and
  an Agreement to Submit Controversy to Arbitration with the Rhode Island Bar Association
  (Rule 4). The forms are also available directly from the Bar's fee arbitration office.
- Consent required on both sides: arbitration is instituted only once both the petitioner
  and the respondent have signed the agreement. If the respondent does not sign and return
  it within 30 days, the rules "conclusively presume" the respondent is unwilling to
  arbitrate, and no panel is appointed; the petitioner is left to whatever other remedies
  exist (Rule 4, Rule 6). This is fully voluntary on both sides, unlike a state where the
  lawyer must participate if the client asks.
- Panel size: the formal Rules and the petition instructions agree on only one figure, a
  $1,500 split (three-member panel above $1,500, single arbitrator at or below), with no
  lower floor mentioned. The Bar's current webpage separately adds, in a "Please Note"
  banner, that the Committee does not handle disputes under $1,000 and that $1,000-$1,500
  disputes go to a single arbitrator. Neither document is dated, so this could not be
  resolved by date; treat the $1,000 floor as current practice, not a rule.
- Right to counsel: each party may be represented by their own attorney, at their own
  expense, at any stage (Rule 8). Not found in the rules read: a filing fee or charge for
  the arbitrators' or Committee's services, a filing deadline specific to this program, or a
  requirement that a lawyer notify a client of the right to arbitrate before suing for fees.
  Nothing ties the filing window to your contract limitation period (section 7); filing
  before that period runs is the safer course.
- Governing law and enforcement: hearings run under the Rhode Island Arbitration Act, G.L.
  Title 10, Chapter 3 (Rule 10). A decision issues within 60 days of the close of the
  hearing or written submissions, is a majority decision in writing stating only the amount
  and terms of payment, and if the panel believes a fee is "illegal or clearly excessive"
  under Rule 1.5 it must also notify Disciplinary Counsel (Rule 11). Once both sides have
  agreed to be bound, the award can be confirmed and enforced in court, or challenged only
  on the narrow statutory grounds in G.L. sections 10-3-11 through 10-3-14, such as fraud,
  corruption, or an arbitrator exceeding their powers.
- Confidentiality: all records, proceedings, and hearings are confidential except that the
  decision may be shared with those having a legitimate interest in it (Rule 13). A petition
  may also arrive by referral from the Disciplinary Board after a complaint is filed there
  (Rule 3), and Disciplinary Counsel may try to resolve a fee dispute informally as part of
  a complaint (Rule 5).

## 4. Bar complaint process

The Disciplinary Board of the Supreme Court of Rhode Island, assisted by the Office of
Disciplinary Counsel, handles attorney discipline. Its program page is
https://www.courts.ri.gov/programs-services/Pages/Disciplinary-Board.aspx. The Board's own
FAQ, published December 12, 2023, at
https://www.courts.ri.gov/about/Documents/Disciplinary%20Board%20Frequently%20Asked%20Questions%20December%2012,%202023.pdf,
states the filing process directly.

- How to file: call (401) 823-5710 to request a complaint form, or write a letter with your
  name, address, phone number, the lawyer's information, and a description of the complaint,
  to the Chief Disciplinary Counsel, Philip W. Noel Judicial Complex, 222 Quaker Lane, Room
  1083, Warwick, RI 02886. Filing costs nothing; the system is funded by annual assessments
  on every practicing Rhode Island lawyer.
- No separate informal consumer-assistance track and no time limit on filing were found in
  the FAQ or the sources read; the FAQ notes many attorney-client problems get resolved
  without ever reaching the Board.
- What it produces: the Board reviews the complaint, may investigate, and can authorize
  formal charges that go to the Supreme Court, which can impose censure, suspension, or
  disbarment. The FAQ states directly that "the function of the Board is solely disciplinary
  and does not have the power to recover fees or damages," and separately states the Board
  does not help recover money a client alleges was stolen, pointing instead to the Client
  Reimbursement Fund described in section 5.
- Restitution: a screening panel that dismisses a complaint with an admonition or recommends
  a letter of reprimand (the lesser, non-formal dispositions) may attach conditions
  including restitution, counseling, or case monitoring (Rules of Procedure of the
  Disciplinary Board, Sections 1.3(b) and 2.3(d)(5)). This is discretionary and tied to
  those lesser dispositions, not a general remedy in every complaint or formal proceeding.
- If you cannot afford a lawyer, the FAQ lists Rhode Island Legal Services, the Office of
  the Public Defender, and the Bar's Volunteer Lawyer Program as resources; the Bar's Lawyer
  Referral Service is (401) 421-7799.

## 5. Client protection fund

Rhode Island's fund is the Rhode Island Bar Association's Lawyer's Fund for Client
Reimbursement, established in 1968 and administered by a Committee of the Bar Association.
The program page is https://ribar.com/?pg=client-reimbursement-fund. The operative rules,
Rules of the Lawyer's Fund for Client Reimbursement, as of 7/1/07 with amendments through
June 17, 2025, were obtained through the same page.

- What it covers: losses of money or other property caused by a Rhode Island lawyer's
  "dishonest conduct," defined as defalcation or embezzlement of money, or the wrongful
  taking or conversion of money, property, or other things of value, occurring during a
  lawyer-client or fiduciary relationship (Rules 1, 4.A, 10.C).
- The exclusion that matters most for this kit: an unearned advance fee is not reimbursable
  unless the claimant can show both "(1) that the lawyer performed no services after receipt
  of the fees, and (2) that it appears that there was no intent to perform services at the
  time of receipt of the fees" (Rule 4.D(5)). In plain terms, a lawyer simply failing to
  earn or refund a retainer is not, by itself, enough; you would need to show the lawyer
  took the money never intending to do the work at all.
- Other exclusions: losses to the lawyer's spouse, children, parents, grandparents,
  siblings, partners, associates, or employees; losses covered by a bond, surety, or
  insurance policy; losses to a non-publicly-held business the lawyer had an interest in, or
  to a government entity; unrepaid loans to the lawyer; interest or collection costs;
  undisclosed attorney's fees; and claims by commercial debt collectors beyond what they
  actually paid (Rule 4.D).
- Per-claimant cap: $100,000. Aggregate cap for losses from a single lawyer or group of
  lawyers acting in collusion: $350,000, with approved claims prorated if total claims
  against one lawyer would exceed that cap (Rule 6.G, confirmed on the Fund's own FAQ page).
- Deadline: a claim must be filed within one year of when the claimant knew or should have
  known of the loss, or of when disciplinary action against the lawyer is made public,
  whichever comes first, with a discretionary hardship exception (Rule 4.B, Rule 4.E). No
  requirement that a disciplinary complaint be on file first was found; the Committee often
  waits at least a year after public discipline to let other claims come in before paying
  (Fund FAQ).
- Process and finality: the lawyer is notified and given 20 days to respond, the Committee
  may hold a hearing, and a claimant must show by a preponderance of the evidence that the
  loss resulted from dishonest conduct and that they made reasonable efforts to recover from
  other sources first (Rules 6, 7.A). Reimbursement is explicitly discretionary, not a legal
  entitlement, per the required legend on every claim form: "no person has a legal right to
  reimbursement... Reimbursement of a loss is a matter of grace in the sole discretion of
  the Committee" (Rule 5.B). A denied or disputed claimant may request reconsideration
  within 30 days (Rule 6.H).

## 6. Small claims or equivalent court

Rhode Island's small claims and consumer claims procedure runs in the District Court under
G.L. Title 10, Chapter 16, "Small Claims and Consumer Claims."

- Jurisdictional limit: $5,000, exclusive of interest and costs, for contract actions, tax
  recovery actions, consumer retail sale or services actions, and actions on a negotiable
  instrument, set statewide with no county-level variation found. G.L. section 10-16-1.
- The plaintiff's waiver of appeal: using this chapter at all is "at the plaintiff's
  election, shown by his or her waiver of appeal" (section 10-16-1), and the plaintiff must
  file a written waiver of the right of appeal along with the claim (section 10-16-4(b)). A
  plaintiff who wants to preserve an appeal right cannot use this small claims procedure.
- The defendant's counterclaim is separately capped lower than the plaintiff's claim: if a
  defendant's counterclaim would exceed $2,500 and the court finds more than $2,500 is due
  the defendant, the court dismisses the plaintiff's claim (as a nonsuit) and enters
  judgment for the defendant's costs only, without deciding the counterclaim itself, leaving
  the defendant free to sue on it separately. G.L. section 10-16-9.
- Attorneys: nothing in sections 10-16-1 through 10-16-16 restricts attorney appearance. The
  only representation provision found is narrow: certain small, closely held corporations
  may designate a non-lawyer officer to prosecute a claim without that being unauthorized
  practice of law (section 10-16-3.1). No small claims rule specific to suing a lawyer was
  found.
- Appeal: only the defendant has a right of appeal, on "the same right of appeal, and under
  the same terms and conditions, as are provided under the practice in ordinary civil
  actions in district courts" (section 10-16-14(a)). The phrase "trial de novo in superior
  court" is not in subsection (a) itself; it appears in subsection (b), which takes that
  right away in one narrow case: if a consumer-suing-over-a-retail-product plaintiff wins by
  default because the defendant never answers, that judgment is final and the defendant
  "shall not be entitled to a trial de novo in superior court" (section 10-16-14(b)), which
  confirms an ordinary district-to-superior appeal under this chapter is a trial de novo.
  G.L. section 9-12-10 independently confirms this, sending the case "for trial on all
  questions of law and fact to the superior court." The appeal must be claimed in writing
  with the clerk within two days (excluding Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays) after
  judgment, paying at that time a $50 attorney's fee to the opposing party's counsel, costs
  of not less than $25, and a technology surcharge under G.L. section 8-15-11 of up to $25,
  set by the state court administrator. Budget at least $100 for an appeal, on top of the
  underlying case costs, and confirm the current surcharge before filing since it is
  administratively set and can change.
- Filing fee for the initial small claims case: a $55.00 entry fee, of which $20 goes to a
  small claims mediation restricted-receipt account, plus an amount equal to the prevailing
  postal rate for mailing notices, plus a technology surcharge under section 8-15-11 of up
  to $25. G.L. section 10-16-4(a). Confirm the current total with the District Court clerk
  before filing, since the mailing and surcharge components can change.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

- General civil (contract) limitation: 10 years from accrual, "except as otherwise specially
  provided." G.L. section 9-1-13(a). A fee agreement dispute not otherwise carved out is
  ordinarily governed by this period.
- Legal malpractice: a separate, specific 3-year period, expressly overriding both the
  10-year general period and the 3-year general personal-injury period: "an action for legal
  malpractice shall be commenced within three (3) years of the occurrence of the incident
  which gave rise to the action." G.L. section 9-1-14.3. Because this statute expressly
  displaces both section 9-1-13 and section 9-1-14 ("notwithstanding the provisions of ss
  9-1-13 and 9-1-14"), a Rhode Island legal malpractice claim is governed by this 3-year
  period regardless of whether it might otherwise be framed in tort or in contract.
- Discovery rule: where the injury could not, with reasonable diligence, have been
  discovered at the time of the incident, the 3-year period instead runs from when it should
  have been discovered. G.L. section 9-1-14.3(2). A person under a disability (age, mental
  incompetence, or otherwise) who brought no action within the 3-year period may still bring
  it within 3 years of the disability's removal. G.L. section 9-1-14.3(1). No separate
  statute of repose beyond these exceptions was found.
- General personal-injury actions (a different, non-malpractice tort period, included only
  to show why it does not govern a malpractice claim): 3 years, G.L. section 9-1-14(b).

This section exists so you know a clock is running and can get your own counsel in time;
this kit does not cover how to bring a malpractice claim.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

Article V's Preamble and Scope, comment 20, tracks the ABA model closely: "Violation of a
Rule should not itself give rise to a cause of action against a lawyer nor should it create
any presumption in such a case that a legal duty has been breached... The Rules are designed
to provide guidance to lawyers and to provide a structure for regulating conduct through
disciplinary agencies. They are not designed to be a basis for civil liability." The same
comment adds the model's qualifier: "since the Rules do establish standards of conduct by
lawyers, a lawyer's violation of a Rule may be evidence of breach of the applicable standard
of conduct." Source: Article V, Preamble and Scope, comment 20, in the full text at
https://www.courts.ri.gov/Courts/SupremeCourt/Documents/Supreme-Rules-Article5.pdf.

This pack did not independently research Rhode Island case law on how courts actually apply
a rule violation as evidence in a malpractice case; that is a malpractice-strategy question
outside this kit's scope, and is listed in the gap list below rather than asserted. Your
actual money routes in Rhode Island are: a contract claim on the fee agreement (section 7),
the voluntary fee arbitration program (section 3), and the Lawyer's Fund for Client
Reimbursement for dishonest conduct, with its narrow advance-fee exception (section 5).
Discipline, including the narrow restitution-as-a-condition mechanism in section 4, is a
separate track run by the Disciplinary Board, not a lawsuit you control.

## Confirm before you rely on this

Before you act, confirm the current text of Rule 1.5 and Rule 1.16 against the Rhode Island
Judiciary's own posted Article V, since court rules are amended from time to time. Confirm
current fee arbitration and Client Reimbursement Fund forms, and any filing fees or
surcharge amounts, directly with the Rhode Island Bar Association and the District Court
clerk, since administratively set fees change even when the underlying rule does not.
