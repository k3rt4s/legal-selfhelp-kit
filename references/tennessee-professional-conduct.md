Tennessee lawyers are governed by the Tennessee Rules of Professional Conduct, adopted as Rule 8 of the Rules of the Supreme Court of Tennessee. The official publication point is the Tennessee Administrative Office of the Courts, at <https://www.tncourts.gov/rules/supreme-court/8>. The rule text summarized below was checked against the Tennessee Bar Association's Tennessee Rules of Professional Conduct, 2023 Edition, reflecting amendments in effect through November 30, 2022, at <https://www.tba.org/docDownload/2181161>.

## RPC 1.5: Fees

The rule opens with two requirements that carry most of the weight in a fee dispute.

1. A lawyer's fee and charges for expenses shall be reasonable.
2. A lawyer shall not make an agreement for, charge, or collect an unreasonable fee or an unreasonable amount for expenses.

That second sentence matters more than clients realize. Collecting an unreasonable fee is itself a violation, so a fee that was arguably reasonable when quoted can still become a problem in how it was billed and collected.

### The ten reasonableness factors

Tennessee lists ten factors for determining whether a fee is reasonable. Eight track the ABA model rule. Two do not, and those two are often the most useful to a Tennessee client.

1. The time and labor required, the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, and the skill requisite to perform the legal service properly.
2. The likelihood, if apparent to the client, that accepting the employment will preclude other employment by the lawyer.
3. The fee customarily charged in the locality for similar legal services.
4. The amount involved and the results obtained.
5. The time limitations imposed by the client or by the circumstances.
6. The nature and length of the professional relationship with the client.
7. The experience, reputation, and ability of the lawyer or lawyers performing the services.
8. Whether the fee is fixed or contingent.
9. Prior advertisements or statements by the lawyer with respect to the fees the lawyer charges.
10. Whether the fee agreement is in writing.

Factors 9 and 10 are the Tennessee additions. If the lawyer advertised a rate and then billed differently, factor 9 speaks to it directly. If there is no written fee agreement, factor 10 makes that absence relevant to whether the fee is reasonable at all, rather than something the client has to argue around.

### Communicating the fee

The scope of the representation and the basis or rate of the fee and expenses must be communicated to the client, preferably in writing, before or within a reasonable time after the representation begins. The exception is a regularly represented client being charged on the same basis as before. Any change in the basis or rate of the fee or expenses must also be communicated to the client.

The practical consequence: a rate increase you were never told about is a communication problem and a reasonableness problem at the same time.

### Contingent fees

A contingent fee agreement must be in writing, signed by the client, and must state:

1. The method by which the fee is determined, including the percentages that apply on settlement, at trial, and on appeal.
2. Which litigation and other expenses are deducted from the recovery.
3. Whether those expenses come out before or after the contingent fee is calculated.

The agreement must clearly tell the client which expenses the client owes whether or not the client wins. At the end of the matter, the lawyer must give the client a written statement showing the outcome and, if there was a recovery, the remittance to the client and how it was calculated.

If you never received that closing statement, ask for it in writing. It is required, and asking for it is not an accusation.

Contingent fees are prohibited in two situations. The first is a domestic relations matter where the payment or amount is contingent on securing a divorce, on the award of custodial rights, or on the amount of alimony, support, or a property division, unless the matter is only about collecting arrearages or enforcing an order dividing the marital estate and the arrangement is disclosed to the court. The second involves criminal defense representation.

## RPC 1.4: Communication

A lawyer must:

1. Promptly inform the client of any decision or circumstance requiring the client's informed consent.
2. Reasonably consult with the client about how the client's objectives are to be accomplished.
3. Keep the client reasonably informed about the status of the matter.
4. Promptly comply with reasonable requests for information.
5. Consult with the client about any relevant limitation on the lawyer's conduct when the lawyer knows the client expects assistance the rules do not permit.

A lawyer must also explain a matter to the extent reasonably necessary to let the client make informed decisions.

Most client complaints that feel like a fee problem start as a communication problem. Unanswered calls, no status updates, and a surprise invoice are one story, not three.

## RPC 1.16: Declining or terminating representation

You can fire your lawyer. On discharge or withdrawal, the lawyer must take reasonably practicable steps to protect your interests, which depending on the circumstances includes:

1. Giving reasonable notice.
2. Allowing time to hire other counsel.
3. Cooperating with successor counsel.
4. Promptly surrendering papers and property you are entitled to, along with any work product the lawyer has already been paid for.
5. Surrendering any other work product the lawyer prepared, except to the extent other law lets the lawyer keep it.
6. Refunding any advance payment of fee or expense that has not been earned or incurred.

The practical consequence: a request for your file and a request for a refund of unearned fees are separate requests, and you can make both in the same letter.

## RPC 1.15: Safekeeping property

Client funds must be held separately from the lawyer's own funds, in a trust account. Unearned fees paid in advance generally belong in trust until earned. If a lawyer took a retainer, did little or no work, and the money is gone, that is a materially different problem from a billing disagreement, and it belongs in front of the Board rather than in a negotiation.

## What these rules are not

The Rules of Professional Conduct are a disciplinary code. Their purpose is to regulate lawyers, not to create a right for you to sue. A violation can support a complaint to the Board of Professional Responsibility and can inform a fee dispute, but it is not by itself a cause of action for damages. Getting money back is usually a contract question, a fee arbitration question, or a claim to the client protection fund. Getting the lawyer disciplined is the Board's question. Keep the two goals separate, because the forums are separate.
