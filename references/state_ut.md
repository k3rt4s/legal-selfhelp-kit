# Utah: handling a dispute with your own lawyer

This section is for a person in Utah who has a problem with their own lawyer over fees,
billing, communication, getting their file back, or an advance payment that was never
earned or never refunded. It does not cover legal malpractice claims or how to run one
yourself. Retrieved August 20, 2026.

What is different about Utah:

- Utah does not number its lawyer conduct rules the way most states do. They sit inside a
  single combined rule book, the Supreme Court Rules of Professional Practice (SCRP), so
  the fee rule is officially "Rule 3-1.5," not a bare "Rule 1.5." The Utah State Bar's own
  ethics opinions still call it "Rule 1.5" informally. Use "Rule 3-1.5" when you cite it in
  a letter. Read section 1 below.
- Utah's fee arbitration program is voluntary for both the lawyer and the client. Neither
  side can be forced into it. It only becomes binding once both sides have separately
  agreed to it in writing, and at that point neither side can back out alone. Read section
  3 before you count on this as a forced remedy.
- Utah licenses a second category of legal service provider below a lawyer, called a
  Licensed Paralegal Practitioner (LPP), who can handle a limited set of matter types. The
  fee arbitration program, the bar discipline process, and the client protection fund all
  cover LPPs the same way they cover lawyers.
- Utah's own Fund for Client Protection rule states a $10,000 per-claim and $25,000
  per-year cap in its main text, but the rule's own amendment history shows both figures
  were later raised administratively: the per-claim limit to $20,000 and the per-lawyer
  annual limit, after being eliminated and reinstated, ending at $75,000 as of 2010. A 2025
  Utah State Bar Commission report independently states the current per-claim limit is
  $20,000, which matches the amendment history and not the $10,000 rule text. The rule
  text was not rewritten to match. Read section 5 and confirm the current figures directly
  with the Fund.
- Utah's small claims dollar limit is on a scheduled increase. It is $20,000 through the
  end of 2029, not the older $15,000 figure you may see in outdated sources.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct: numbering and adoption

Utah's rules governing lawyers are adopted by the Utah Supreme Court as the Supreme Court
Rules of Professional Practice (SCRP), a single rule set that covers court administration,
lawyer discipline, the client protection fund, fee arbitration, and the numbered rules of
professional conduct all together, organized into five chapters. This combined structure
is itself the first thing a reader from another state needs to know: Utah does not keep
its ethics rules in a stand-alone rule book the way many states do.

The numbered rules of professional conduct, the ones that track the ABA model numbering
(1.0 through 8.5), sit inside SCRP Chapter 3. Because of that placement, the court's own
rule pages give the fee rule the official citation "Rule 3-1.5," not a bare "Rule 1.5."
The chapter-3 prefix applies to every rule in that chapter: communication duties are "Rule
3-1.4," safekeeping property is "Rule 3-1.15," and duties on discharge are "Rule 3-1.16."
Use the "3-" prefix when you cite these rules to your lawyer or in a written demand, since
that is the form the court itself uses on the current rule pages.

You will also see these rules cited without the chapter prefix, as plain "Rule 1.5" or
"1.5(a)," in the Utah State Bar's own ethics opinions and in older material. That looser
form is common enough that a lawyer or bar staff will understand it, but it is not the
citation the court's current rule pages use. When precision matters, such as in a letter
you want taken seriously, use "Rule 3-1.5."

Fee arbitration and the client protection fund sit in a different chapter of the same rule
set, SCRP Chapter 4, cited with a "4-" prefix (for example "Rule 4-1102" for fee
arbitration, "Rule 4-913" for the client protection fund's payment caps). Lawyer discipline
procedure sits in Chapter 1, Article 5, cited with a "1-" prefix (for example "Rule 1-505"
for the disciplinary statute of limitations). A reader who only knows "Rule 1.5" and does
not know this chapter structure will not find the discipline or fee-arbitration rules by
guessing at nearby numbers, because they are not numbered near 1.5 at all.

The current rule text is published by the Utah Courts on a site with a "legacy" hostname,
<https://legacy.utcourts.gov/rules/>, which despite its name is the live, current site for
these rules; the newer-looking <https://www.utcourts.gov/rules/> address simply redirects
there. Rule 3-1.5, the fee reasonableness rule, carries an effective date of May 1, 2021 on
its current published text.

## 2. How Utah's fee rule differs from the model

Rule 3-1.5(a) lists the same eight reasonableness factors as the ABA model rule, in the
same order: the time and labor required and the novelty and difficulty of the questions
involved; whether taking the matter would preclude other employment; the customary local
fee; the amount involved and the results obtained; time limits imposed by the client or
circumstances; the nature and length of the professional relationship; the lawyer's
experience, reputation, and ability; and whether the fee is fixed or contingent. Utah adds
nothing to that list and removes nothing from it.

Rule 3-1.5(b) and (c), covering how the fee basis must be communicated and what a
contingent fee agreement must contain, also track the model rule closely, including the
same two flat prohibitions on contingent fees in domestic relations matters and in
representing a criminal defendant.

Where Utah goes beyond the model rule is in what it requires of the newer class of
Licensed Paralegal Practitioner. Rule 3-1.5(e) bars an LPP from entering into a contingent
fee agreement with a client at all, a flat prohibition the model rule does not have for any
class of provider. Rule 3-1.5(f) requires an LPP, before providing any services, to give
the client a written agreement stating the purpose of the engagement, the services to be
performed, the fee or rate and what costs the client will owe, and a boldface 12-point
statement that the LPP is not an attorney. None of this applies to a fully licensed lawyer.

This kit looked specifically for a Utah-specific ban on "nonrefundable" or "earned upon
receipt" fee language, the kind some states add as its own separate rule, and did not find
one beyond the ordinary trust-account requirement in Rule 3-1.15(c), which matches the
model rule: advance fees and expenses go into a trust account and can only be withdrawn as
they are actually earned or incurred. If your fee agreement labels a retainer
"nonrefundable" or "earned upon receipt," that label does not override Rule 3-1.15(c) or
the reasonableness requirement in Rule 3-1.5(a); an unearned portion of any advance fee is
still the client's money.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

Utah runs a single statewide program under SCRP Chapter 4, Article 11: the Utah State
Bar's Fee Dispute Resolution Committee. This is a Bar committee, not a court, staffed by
members appointed annually by the Bar president.

Participation is not mandatory on either side. Rule 4-1102(c) states plainly that
participation in the fee arbitration process is non-mandatory, but if all the necessary
parties elect in writing to arbitrate, the decision is binding. That is the single most
important fact about this program: your lawyer cannot be forced into it by your request
alone, and you cannot be forced into it by your lawyer's request alone. Once both sides
sign the agreement to arbitrate, though, the decision binds both, and under Rule 4-1102(d)
a party cannot withdraw from that agreement afterward unless every party agrees to the
withdrawal in writing.

The Committee will not hear a dispute over alleged malpractice; under Rule 4-1103(a)(1) it
may consider malpractice or misconduct evidence only to the extent it bears on the fee
itself, and it cannot award damages for the underlying claim. It also will not hear a
dispute already decided by court order, and at the executive director's or chair's
discretion it can decline a dispute that is administratively too complex.

There is a filing deadline. Under Rule 4-1103(a)(3), a request for arbitration or mediation
is barred if filed more than four years after the lawyer-client relationship ended, or more
than four years after the client received the final bill, or after the underlying civil
claim would itself be barred by the statute of limitations described in section 7 below,
whichever of those is later.

There is no dollar floor or ceiling on what the Committee will hear, but the dollar amount
changes who decides it. Rule 4-1105(a) sets a default three-member panel: one Utah lawyer,
one current or retired judge or commissioner, and one non-lawyer. Rule 4-1105(b) directs a
single-lawyer arbitrator instead when the amount in controversy is less than $10,000, and
when the amount is between $7,500 and $10,000 the petitioner may choose to request the
full three-member panel instead of a single arbitrator.

The process is inexpensive once both sides agree: Rule 4-1104(c) sets a $10 fee, paid by
the petitioner, due only after both parties have agreed to binding arbitration; if
respondent refuses to sign, no fee is owed and the matter does not go forward as binding
arbitration. Under Rule 4-1107(a), the hearing is set within about 60 days of receiving the
signed agreement and completed filings, and the panel or arbitrator must issue its award
within 20 days after the hearing closes. This kit did not find a rule requiring a lawyer to
notify a client of the right to arbitrate before suing the client for fees, after reviewing
the full text of Rules 4-1101 through 4-1110.

If your dispute is not one the Committee will hear, or your lawyer declines to arbitrate
and cannot be made to, your money routes are small claims or district court described in
section 6, or the client protection fund in section 5 if the conduct involved dishonesty
rather than a plain billing disagreement.

The Fee Dispute Resolution Committee's governing rules are published at
<https://legacy.utcourts.gov/rules/view.php?type=scrp&rule=4-1102>, within SCRP Chapter 4,
Article 11.

## 4. Filing a bar complaint

Utah's disciplinary regulator is the Office of Professional Conduct (OPC), operating under
the Utah Supreme Court's Rules of Discipline, Disability and Sanctions in SCRP Chapter 1,
Article 5. A complaint against a lawyer or LPP must reach the OPC within four years of when
the complainant discovered, or reasonably should have discovered, the alleged misconduct,
under Rule 1-505(a).

Utah separates an informal track from the formal OPC complaint. The Utah State Bar's
Consumer Assistance Program (CAP) is the informal track: a CAP attorney reaches out to
both the consumer and the lawyer or LPP to try to resolve a problem directly, such as
getting a file released, locating a lawyer who has gone silent, or clearing up a
communication breakdown, and once the matter is resolved the CAP file is closed and its
contents destroyed. A plain billing disagreement or a communication problem, the kind this
kit is about, generally belongs with CAP first, not with a formal OPC complaint. Filing a
fee dispute as a formal OPC complaint usually results in a closed file rather than money in
your hands, because OPC's own process states directly that it "is not designed to provide
you with reimbursement or other monetary compensation."

The OPC's formal complaint process, once a complaint implicates a possible rule violation,
moves through an initial investigation, a follow-up investigation seeking the lawyer's
formal response, and, if the case is not dismissed first, a screening panel hearing. OPC
will share what you submit with the lawyer named, cannot give you legal advice, cannot
direct the lawyer to do or stop doing anything, and cannot find you a new lawyer.

Utah does have a limited disciplinary restitution mechanism, unlike many states. Under
Rule 1-581, when a lawyer is placed on probation as a disciplinary sanction, restitution
can be one of the conditions the Committee or the court attaches to that probation,
alongside things like continuing education, practice limits, or a required ethics exam.
This is discretionary, applies only if a lawyer ends up on probation as a sanction, and is
not something a complainant can request or count on. It is not a substitute for the money
routes in sections 3, 5, and 6.

CAP's request form and contact information are published by the Utah State Bar at
<https://www.utahbar.org/consumer-assistance-program/>. OPC now operates its own site,
separate from the Utah State Bar and the utcourts.gov domain; its complaint process and
form are published at <https://www.opcutah.org/file-a-complaint/>.

## 5. Client protection fund

Utah's fund is the Lawyers' Fund for Client Protection, governed by SCRP Chapter 4,
Article 9, and administered by a Board with recommendations from a Committee under the
Utah State Bar's oversight.

The Fund reimburses a loss caused by the "dishonest conduct" of a lawyer or LPP, arising
out of a lawyer-client, LPP-client, or fiduciary relationship, under Rule 4-910(a). A claim
must be filed within one year of the final order of discipline against the lawyer or LPP,
or within one year of the lawyer's or LPP's death or formal disability order, under Rule
4-910(b). Losses tied to a loan or investment transaction with the lawyer are reimbursable
only under a multi-factor test in Rule 4-910(c) looking at whether the lawyer's status as a
lawyer, rather than an ordinary business relationship, is what made the loss possible.

Rule 4-910(d) lists explicit exclusions: losses of the lawyer's own spouse, children,
parents, grandparents, siblings, partners, or associates; losses already covered by a
bond, surety, agreement, or insurance contract; losses recoverable under a banker's
blanket bond; losses through a business entity the lawyer or those insiders control;
losses of a governmental entity; assigned claims, third-party claims, and claims of heirs
or estates; claims where the claimant has not exhausted other reasonably available
recovery methods; and a set of investment-loss categories including pyramid or Ponzi
schemes, offshore investments, and tax-shelter schemes. Rule 4-910(e) gives the Committee
discretion to recognize an otherwise-excluded claim in a case of extreme hardship or
unusual circumstances. Fee disputes and ordinary malpractice are not on this exclusion
list because they are not covered in the first place; the Fund only reaches dishonest
conduct.

The dollar caps carry a discrepancy worth flagging directly. Rule 4-913(a) states in its
main text that the Board's maximum is $10,000 per claim and $25,000 total within a
calendar year for an individual lawyer or LPP, and Rule 4-913(a)(1) sets a lifetime cap of
$425,000 per lawyer or LPP. But the same rule's own published Advisory Committee Notes
describe a documented history of later Board votes raising those calendar-year figures:
the per-claim limit was raised to $20,000 in 2000, and the per-lawyer annual limit, after
being eliminated and then reinstated, was raised to $50,000 in 2001 and then to $75,000 on
October 29, 2010. The rule's own operative paragraph was not rewritten to reflect that
history, so the two figures inside the same official rule page do not agree. A Utah State
Bar Commission meeting packet from August 2025, summarizing an Office of the State
Auditor performance review, independently states that "claims against the Fund are
limited to $20,000 per claimant," which lines up with the Advisory Committee Notes and not
with the $10,000 figure in the rule text, so treat $20,000 as the better-supported current
per-claim figure. No comparably current source was found for the annual per-lawyer total
or the lifetime cap; do not assume the $75,000 or $425,000 figures are still current.
Call the Fund and confirm all three numbers before you rely on any of them.

Utah's Fund for Client Protection is administered through the Utah State Bar; general
information is published at <https://www.utahbar.org/fund-for-client-protection/>.

## 6. Small claims court

Utah's small claims court is a limited-jurisdiction division that sits inside both the
district courts and the justice courts, under Utah Code 78A-8-101.

The dollar limit is on a scheduled increase, set by Utah Code 78A-8-102(1)(a)(i): $15,000
through December 31, 2024, $20,000 from January 1, 2025 through December 31, 2029, and
$25,000 from January 1, 2030 onward. As of this pack's retrieval date, the current limit is
$20,000, including any attorney fees claimed but excluding court costs and interest. This
is a statewide statutory limit; this kit did not find any county-level variation in the
dollar limit itself, since the statute sets one figure for the whole state regardless of
which district or justice court hears the case.

A party does not need a lawyer. Under Utah Code 78A-8-102(6), a person or corporation may
appear on their own or through an authorized employee, and Rule 13 of the Utah Rules of
Small Claims Procedure confirms that a party may be self-represented, represented by an
attorney admitted to practice in Utah, represented by an employee, or, with the court's
express approval, represented by an uncompensated third party. Lawyers are allowed to
appear; they are simply not required.

Either party may appeal to the district court within 28 days of judgment, under Utah Code
78A-8-106. The appeal is a trial de novo, a full new hearing rather than a review of the
first one, and the trial de novo decision itself generally cannot be appealed further
unless it rules on the constitutionality of a statute or ordinance.

The filing fee scales with the amount claimed: $60 for claims of $2,000 or less, rising
through several tiers to $185 for claims between $7,500 and the current $20,000 limit. An
appeal costs more than a first look at the small claims fee schedule suggests. A notice of
appeal filed with the justice court carries its own $10 fee, but Utah Code 78A-2-301(1)(g)(i)
separately sets a $240 fee for a trial de novo of a justice court or small claims judgment,
payable when the case moves to district court. Budget for both fees together, about $250
total, not just the $10 justice-court fee. This kit did not find any rule specific to suing
your own lawyer in small claims court; an ordinary fee dispute is treated the same as any
other small claims case.

The governing statute, Utah Code 78A-8-102, is published as a PDF at
<https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title78A/Chapter8/C78A-8-S102_2022050420220504.pdf>, and the
current fee schedule is published at
<https://www.utcourts.gov/en/legal-help/legal-help/procedures/fees.html>.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A dispute over what you owed your lawyer, or what your lawyer owed you back, under your
fee agreement is a contract claim, and Utah splits that period by whether the agreement
was written or not. Utah Code 78B-2-309 gives a written contract, obligation, or liability
six years. Utah Code 78B-2-307(1)(a) gives a contract not founded on a written instrument,
including an open account for services rendered, four years, running from the last charge
made or the last payment received.

Utah's statutes of limitation chapter, Title 78B, Chapter 2, does not contain a
stand-alone limitation period naming legal malpractice specifically. The only
malpractice-specific limitations provision this kit found in that chapter, Section
78B-2-114, governs professional negligence claims against physicians, surgeons, dentists,
nurses, and other listed medical professions, not lawyers. Absent a dedicated period, a
legal malpractice claim appears to fall under the four-year catch-all in Utah Code
78B-2-307(4), for relief not otherwise provided for by law, though this kit did not find a
statute that says so in those words.

This kit did not find a codified discovery rule inside Title 78B, Chapter 2 that applies
specifically to legal malpractice claims; the discovery-rule provisions that do appear in
that chapter are written for other specific torts, such as trespass, fraud, and mistake,
not for claims against a lawyer. Utah case law addresses when a legal malpractice claim
accrues, including a Utah Supreme Court decision on the subject, but this kit did not
independently confirm the current judicial rule on accrual against the full text of that
opinion, so no accrual rule beyond the statute's own terms is stated here. Treat the
four-year catch-all period as the outer edge, not a safe deadline to plan around, and get
your own lawyer's advice quickly if you suspect malpractice rather than a billing problem.

This kit does not cover running a legal malpractice claim. These periods are here so you
know a clock exists and can get counsel in time if you need it, not so you can calculate
your own deadline.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

Utah's Rules of Professional Conduct carry the same Scope section language as the ABA
model rule, in paragraph [20] of the Scope commentary that precedes the numbered rules:
violation of a rule should not itself give rise to a cause of action against a lawyer, nor
create any presumption that a legal duty has been breached, and the rules are not designed
to be a basis for civil liability. The same paragraph adds that a rule violation may still
be considered as evidence of a standard-of-care breach in a case that is otherwise a
recognized claim, such as a malpractice action.

In practical terms, filing a rule-violation complaint against your lawyer does not by
itself put money in your hands. Your actual money routes are the ones covered above: a
contract claim over the fee itself under section 7, the fee arbitration program in section
3, the client protection fund in section 5 if the conduct was dishonest in the sense
described there, or a separate malpractice claim if that applies to you, which is outside
what this kit covers.

Supreme Court Rules of Professional Practice at legacy.utcourts.gov/rules, Utah Code Title
