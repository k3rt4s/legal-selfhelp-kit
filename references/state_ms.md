# Mississippi: handling a dispute with your own lawyer

This section is for a person in Mississippi who has a problem with their own lawyer over
fees, billing, communication, getting their file back, or an advance payment that was
never earned or never refunded. It does not cover legal malpractice claims or how to run
one yourself. Retrieved August 20, 2026.

What is different about Mississippi:

- Mississippi's fee rule opens with an affirmative command, "A lawyer's fee shall be
  reasonable," rather than the model rule's prohibition on charging an unreasonable fee.
  The eight reasonableness factors underneath it match the model list word for word. Read
  section 2.
- Mississippi's fee arbitration program, the Fee Dispute Resolution Committee, is entirely
  voluntary. Neither side can be forced into it, and there is no dollar floor, ceiling, or
  filing fee. It only becomes binding if both sides choose the binding arbitration track
  and sign on. Read section 3 before you count on this as a forced remedy.
- Mississippi's conduct rules use the same plain ABA-style numbering as the model rules
  (Rule 1.1 through 8.5), with no chapter prefix. Cite them exactly as you would the model
  rule, for example "Rule 1.5."
- The Mississippi Bar splits a billing or communication problem into two tracks: an
  informal Consumer Assistance Program that contacts your lawyer directly and resolves
  most problems without a formal filing, and a formal grievance process that can discipline
  a lawyer but the Bar's own materials state directly does not recover money for you.
- Mississippi's Clients' Security Fund exists under the Bar's bylaws, but this kit could
  not find any publicly published rule stating its coverage terms, exclusions, per-claim
  cap, or filing deadline. Read section 5 and the gap list before assuming any figure.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct: numbering and adoption

The Supreme Court of Mississippi has exclusive and inherent jurisdiction over attorney
discipline, reinstatement, and a lawyer's personal capacity to practice, stated directly in
Rule 1 of the Rules of Discipline for the Mississippi State Bar. The Mississippi Rules of
Professional Conduct, effective July 1, 1987, are the court's own rule book governing
lawyer conduct, and they use the same numbering as the ABA Model Rules: Rule 1.1 through
Rule 8.5, organized under the same headings (Client-Lawyer Relationship, Counselor,
Advocate, and so on). There is no chapter or article prefix. Cite the fee rule to your
lawyer or in a written demand exactly as "Rule 1.5," the same form used nationally.

Mississippi is a mandatory, unified bar state. Under Miss. Code Section 73-3-119,
enrollment on the list of Bar members and payment of annual enrollment fees are
prerequisites to practicing law in the state, for both lawyers already admitted and those
admitted afterward. The Mississippi Bar itself, not a separate voluntary association, is
the entity every practicing lawyer must belong to, and it is also the body that runs the
Consumer Assistance Program and the Fee Dispute Resolution Committee described below. The
disciplinary function is legally distinct: the Supreme Court holds ultimate authority, and
day-to-day grievance handling runs through the Bar's Office of General Counsel and a Bar
committee, the Committee on Professional Responsibility, acting under the court's Rules of
Discipline rather than under the Bar's own bylaws.

The Mississippi Rules of Professional Conduct are published by the Mississippi judiciary at
<https://courts.ms.gov/research/rules/msrulesofcourt/rules_of_professional_conduct.pdf>.
The Rules of Discipline for the Mississippi State Bar are published at
<https://courts.ms.gov/research/rules/msrulesofcourt/rules_of_discipline.pdf>. Rule 1.5
carries no separate amendment note in the current text beyond the July 1, 1987 adoption
date of the Rules of Professional Conduct as a whole.

## 2. How Mississippi's fee rule differs from the model

Rule 1.5(a) opens with a sentence the model rule does not have: "A lawyer's fee shall be
reasonable." The model rule instead states the same idea as a prohibition, that a lawyer
shall not charge an unreasonable fee. The practical effect is similar either way, but the
Mississippi version states it as an affirmative duty rather than a ban, which changes how a
letter to your lawyer can be framed: you are not just alleging a prohibited act, you can
point to a duty the fee itself failed to meet. Underneath that opening sentence, Mississippi
lists the same eight reasonableness factors as the model rule, in the same order, with
nothing added and nothing removed: the work required and its difficulty, whether the matter
precluded other work, the customary local fee, the amount involved and the result, time
pressure, the length of the lawyer-client relationship, the lawyer's experience and
reputation, and whether the fee is fixed or contingent.

Rule 1.5(b) tracks the model rule closely: when the lawyer has not regularly represented the
client, the fee basis must be communicated, preferably in writing, before or within a
reasonable time after the representation begins. Mississippi does not add a dollar
threshold or a matter-type trigger requiring a written fee agreement beyond this
"preferably in writing" language; none was found in Rule 1.5 or its comment.

Rule 1.5(c) requires a contingent fee agreement to be in writing, state the method of
calculating the fee including the percentages at settlement, trial, or appeal, state which
expenses are deducted from the recovery and whether before or after the fee is calculated,
and requires a written closing statement at the end of the matter. Rule 1.5(d) prohibits a
contingent fee in a domestic relations matter tied to securing a divorce or to the amount of
alimony, support, or a property settlement, matching the model rule's two flat prohibitions.
No Mississippi-specific nonrefundable-fee rule or added advance-fee disclosure requirement
was found in Rule 1.5, its comment, or Rule 1.15.

Rule 1.15 requires a lawyer to keep client and third-person property, including advance
fees, separate from the lawyer's own funds in a trust account, and to keep complete trust
account records for seven years after the representation ends, longer than the plain
safekeeping duty described in the national baseline.

On the file-return question: Rule 1.16(d) requires a lawyer, on termination, to take
reasonably practicable steps to protect the client, including surrendering papers and
property the client is entitled to and refunding any unearned advance payment. But
Mississippi's Rule 1.16(d) adds a sentence the national baseline's model text does not
carry: "The lawyer may retain papers relating to the client to the extent permitted by other
law." This is a real, sourced deviation, confirmed verbatim in the operative rule text (not a
comment), and it means Mississippi does not flatly bar a retaining lien over your file for
unpaid fees the way a simple reading of the surrender duty might suggest; whether a lien can
actually be asserted against you depends on other Mississippi law this kit did not research.
The Comment to Rule 1.16 repeats the point: a lawyer "may retain papers as security for a fee
only to the extent permitted by law," citing two Mississippi Bar ethics opinions, Nos. 49 and
105, which this kit did not itself read. If your former lawyer is withholding your file over
a fee dispute, this is the rule to raise, but do not assume the surrender duty is absolute.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

Mississippi runs a single statewide program: the Fee Dispute Resolution Committee, a
committee of the Mississippi Bar, not a court. Attorney members are appointed annually by
the Bar president to serve as mediators, arbitrators, or both, under the Committee's own
Rules of Procedure.

Participation is entirely voluntary for both sides. The Rules of Procedure describe the
process as voluntary throughout, and if the client, called the Petitioner, does not sign a
consent to mediation or binding arbitration within fifteen days of the request being
acknowledged, the matter is closed for lack of jurisdiction. The same applies to the
lawyer, called the Respondent, who is separately asked to consent. There is no mechanism in
the Rules of Procedure that forces either side into the process.

The Committee offers two tracks. Mediation is non-binding unless both sides sign a written
settlement agreement, which can then be enforced in court; it is meant to be completed
within about 30 days of the mediator's selection. Binding Arbitration is binding once both
sides have consented to that track; the Rules of Procedure give the arbitrator a series of
short deadlines (days, not weeks) to review the complaint, set a hearing, and proceed, and
give arbitrators the powers set out in Miss. Code Section 11-15-1 and following.

The Committee's jurisdiction covers fee disputes between a Mississippi-licensed lawyer and
a client arising from an actual or implied attorney-client relationship. It specifically
excludes disputes over which a court already has jurisdiction to fix the fee, and disputes
that involve a violation of the Rules of Professional Conduct rather than the fee amount
itself; a rule-violation complaint belongs with the bar complaint process in section 4
instead. This kit read the full eight-page Rules of Procedure and found no dollar floor or
ceiling on what the Committee will hear, no stated filing deadline running from the bill or
the end of representation, no filing fee charged to either side, and no rule requiring a
lawyer to notify a client of the right to arbitrate before suing for fees.

A request is submitted in writing to the Bar's General Counsel, at the Mississippi Bar
Center, 643 North State Street, Jackson, or by mail to P.O. Box 2168, Jackson, MS
39225-2168; the Consumer Assistance Program in section 4 is the practical starting point
and will send you the petition form.

The Fee Dispute Resolution Committee's program page is published at
<https://msbar.org/professional-regulation/fee-disputes/>. Its Rules of Procedure are
published at
<https://msbar.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Fee-Dispute-Resolution-Committee-Rules-of-Procedure.pdf>.

## 4. Filing a bar complaint

Mississippi's disciplinary process runs through the Mississippi Bar's Office of General
Counsel and the Committee on Professional Responsibility, under the Supreme Court's Rules
of Discipline. The Supreme Court itself holds exclusive and inherent jurisdiction over the
outcome, but complaints are filed with and investigated by the Bar.

Mississippi separates an informal track from the formal one. The Consumer Assistance
Program (CAP) is the first point of contact for anyone with a problem involving a
Mississippi attorney: CAP listens, answers questions, and tries to resolve the problem
directly, and the Bar's own materials state that about 70 percent of consumer problems are
resolved this way without a grievance ever being filed. A plain fee or communication
problem, the kind this kit is about, belongs with CAP first. CAP's own guidance is explicit
that if a client believes the fee is too high or cannot get a refund, the client should
first write the lawyer requesting an itemized statement and a refund of any unused
retainer, and that a lawyer must return any unearned fee or unused retainer portion on
discharge or withdrawal; if that does not resolve it, CAP provides the forms to file a fee
dispute petition with the Fee Dispute Resolution Committee in section 3. CAP can also
contact a former lawyer directly about releasing your file. CAP is reached at
(601) 948-2344, by email, or by mail to Consumer Assistance Program, P.O. Box 2168, Jackson,
MS 39225-2168.

The formal track is a written grievance filed with the Office of General Counsel, considered
by the Committee on Professional Responsibility. A grievance must be submitted in writing on
the Bar's own form and must be received, discovered, or otherwise brought to the Committee's
attention within three years after the date of discovery of the facts on which it is based.
That three-year deadline does not apply to allegations under Rule 1.15 (safekeeping
property), Rule 8.1 (bar admission and disciplinary matters), or Rule 8.4(b) through (f)
(most of the misconduct rule), which carry no stated time limit.

The Bar's own guidance answers the question this kit is built around directly: "The
disciplinary procedure of the Mississippi Bar does not recover money damages for clients."
Filing a formal grievance over a fee dispute can result in discipline against the lawyer,
but it is not a path to getting your money back; the fee arbitration program in section 3 or
a court claim are the money routes. Mississippi does have a narrow, discretionary
restitution mechanism, but it is tied to a disbarred or suspended lawyer's reinstatement,
not to a complainant's grievance: under Rule 13 of the Rules of Discipline, a lawyer seeking
reinstatement must show they made full amends and restitution to anyone who suffered a
pecuniary loss from their misconduct. This is a condition the Court can require before
letting the lawyer practice again; it is not something a complainant can request or be
awarded through the grievance process itself.

CAP is described at <https://msbar.org/for-the-public/consumer-assistance-program/>. The
formal disciplinary process is described at
<https://msbar.org/professional-regulation/attorney-disciplinary-process/>. The Rules of
Discipline are published at
<https://courts.ms.gov/research/rules/msrulesofcourt/rules_of_discipline.pdf>.

## 5. Client protection fund

Mississippi has a Clients' Security Fund, named in the Mississippi Bar's own Bylaws,
Article 9-11, which creates a six-member Clients' Security Fund Committee and states only
that the Committee "shall administer the Clients' Security Fund in accordance with the
resolution creating the same." This kit looked for the resolution itself, and for any
public-facing Bar page describing the fund's coverage, exclusions, caps, or deadlines, and
could not find either one published anywhere reachable. The Bar's own site returns a
not-found page for the fund at the URL patterns this kit tried.

Because of that, this kit cannot state what the fund covers, what it excludes, its per-claim
or aggregate cap, or any filing deadline, and none of those figures appear anywhere in this
pack. Do not rely on any number you may see quoted elsewhere for this fund. One figure this
kit found through a general search turned out, on direct inspection of the cited page, not
to mention Mississippi at all. A second, that Mississippi once capped total payments per
lawyer at $5,000, traces only to a 1995 Connecticut legislative report citing a 1993 ABA
survey, over thirty years old and from neither Mississippi nor its Bar. Neither figure is
used in this pack. Archived snapshots of a Bar committee page describe the Committee's job
as reviewing claims and recommending payouts "in accordance with the rules of procedure of
the fund," without ever publishing those rules anywhere this kit could reach. If you believe
you have a loss from a Mississippi lawyer's dishonest conduct rather than an ordinary fee
dispute, contact the Mississippi Bar directly and ask about the Clients' Security Fund by
name; do not assume it works the same way as another state's fund.

The Mississippi Bar Bylaws, including Article 9-11, are published at
<https://msbar.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/MS-Bar-Bylaws.pdf>.

## 6. Small claims court

Mississippi's court of limited civil jurisdiction for small claims is the justice court.
Under Miss. Code Section 9-11-9, justice court judges have jurisdiction over actions to
recover a debt, damages, or personal property where the amount does not exceed
$3,500. This figure is current: a 2025 bill, SB 2499, that would have raised the limit to
$5,000, did not pass and is not in effect. This is a single statewide dollar limit; this kit
did not find any county-level variation in the amount itself, since Section 9-11-9 sets one
figure applying to all 82 justice courts in the state.

The Rules of Justice Court, adopted by the Mississippi Supreme Court, govern procedure. Rule
10 and several other rules (service of process, pleadings, jury selection) pair "the
attorneys or the parties" throughout, confirming that a lawyer may appear in justice court
and that a party may also appear without one. This kit did not find a rule requiring a
corporation to be represented by counsel in justice court specifically, and did not find any
statute or rule capping how many claims a person may file there in a year; both stay open
questions.

Either party may appeal a justice court civil judgment to the circuit court. Under Miss.
Code Section 11-51-85, the appeal must be demanded and a bond given within 10 days after the
judgment, with the bond set at double the amount of the judgment or the value of the
property involved, and covering all costs accrued and likely to accrue, and not less than
$100. A defendant unable to afford the bond because of poverty may still appeal by filing an
affidavit to that effect, though the appeal then does not act as a stay of the judgment. Rule
27 of the Rules of Justice Court confirms that civil appeals from justice court are governed
by the Uniform Civil Rules of Circuit and County Court, and the appeal functions as a trial
de novo in circuit court rather than a review of the justice court record. This kit did not
find a rule specific to suing your own lawyer in justice court; an ordinary fee dispute is
treated the same as any other small claims case.

Miss. Code Section 25-7-25(1)(a) sets a single statewide filing fee for a civil case in
justice court, $25.00, paid to the clerk in advance under Section 9-11-10, with no county
variation in the statute. The same statute, Section 25-7-25(1)(g), sets a separate $5.00
justice court fee for an appeal with proceedings and bond, on top of the bond above. Miss.
Code Section 25-7-13 separately charges a circuit court civil docketing fee, $85.00 plus two
smaller fund fees, $135.00 total, when a new civil case is filed there; this kit did not find
anything stating whether that same fee applies again when a justice court appeal transcript
is docketed for trial de novo, as opposed to being covered by the bond's own "costs accrued
and likely to accrue" language. Budget for the bond and the $5.00 appeal fee at minimum, and
ask the circuit clerk whether a docketing fee also applies before you appeal.

<https://law.justia.com/codes/mississippi/title-9/chapter-11/section-9-11-9/>. The Rules of
Justice Court are published at
<https://courts.ms.gov/research/rules/msrulesofcourt/Rules%20of%20Justice%20Court.pdf>.
Section 11-51-85 is published at
<https://law.justia.com/codes/mississippi/title-11/chapter-51/section-11-51-85/>. Section
25-7-25 (justice court fees) is published at
<https://law.justia.com/codes/mississippi/title-25/chapter-7/section-25-7-25/>, Section

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A dispute over what you owed your lawyer, or what your lawyer owed you back, under your fee
agreement is a contract claim. Mississippi does not appear to split this period by written
versus oral contracts the way some states do. Miss. Code Section 15-1-29 sets a three-year
period for actions on an open account or an unwritten contract, express or implied (with a
separate one-year period specifically for an unwritten employment contract, which would not
usually apply to a fee dispute). This kit did not find a separate, longer statute for a
written contract; a written fee agreement dispute therefore appears to fall under the
general three-year catch-all in Miss. Code Section 15-1-49(1), which applies to any action
for which no other period is specifically prescribed. Both a written and an unwritten fee
agreement dispute land on the same three-year period through these two statutes.

Mississippi's dedicated malpractice limitation statute, Section 15-1-36, is written
specifically for medical, surgical, and other named health-care professions (physicians,
dentists, hospitals, nurses, and similar listed providers) and this kit confirmed it does
not name attorneys or legal services. This kit did not find any separate statute naming
legal malpractice specifically. A legal malpractice claim therefore appears to fall under
the same general three-year catch-all in Section 15-1-49(1) that governs the contract claim
above, rather than under Section 15-1-36's shorter medical-malpractice period. Section
15-1-49 also contains a codified discovery rule, but it is narrow: subsection (2) states
that for an action with no other prescribed period and involving a "latent injury or
disease," the cause of action does not accrue until the plaintiff discovered, or by
reasonable diligence should have discovered, the injury. This kit did not confirm whether
Mississippi courts have applied that latent-injury-or-disease language to a legal
malpractice claim specifically, so treat it as a possibility rather than a confirmed rule
for your situation.

This kit looked for a Mississippi statute of repose specific to legal malpractice, separate
from the ordinary limitation periods above. None was found; Section 15-1-49's full text
contains only the general three-year period and the latent-injury discovery rule in
subsection (2), with no repose provision. A seven-year statute of repose does exist in
Mississippi, but only for medical malpractice under Section 15-1-36, and it does not apply
to a claim against a lawyer.

This kit does not cover running a legal malpractice claim. These periods are here so you
know a clock exists and can get counsel in time if you need it, not so you can calculate
your own deadline.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

The Mississippi Rules of Professional Conduct carry a Scope section, preceding the numbered
rules, stating that the Rules are rules of reason designed to provide guidance to lawyers
and a structure for disciplinary regulation, and that violation of a Rule "should not give
rise to a cause of action nor should it create any presumption that a legal duty has been
breached." The same Scope section adds that the Rules are not designed to be a basis for
civil liability, and warns against the Rules being invoked by opposing parties as
"procedural weapons" in unrelated litigation. Unlike some states' current rule text, the
version of the Scope section in Mississippi's current published Rules of Professional
Conduct does not carry numbered paragraphs, so this kit cites it by section name rather than
a paragraph number.

In practical terms, filing a rule-violation complaint against your lawyer does not by itself
put money in your hands. Your actual money routes are the ones covered above: a contract
claim over the fee itself under section 7, the fee arbitration program in section 3, the
Clients' Security Fund in section 5 if you can confirm it covers your situation, or a
separate malpractice claim if that applies to you, which is outside what this kit covers.
