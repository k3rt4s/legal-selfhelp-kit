# North Carolina

This pack is for someone in North Carolina trying to resolve a fee, billing, communication, or
file-return dispute with their own lawyer. It is not a guide to suing a lawyer for malpractice.
Retrieved 2026-08-20.

What a North Carolina reader most needs to know, that a reader in another state does not:

- North Carolina runs a statewide Fee Dispute Resolution Program, and it is mandatory on the
  lawyer once you make a proper request. Your lawyer cannot simply refuse to participate. The
  program uses mediation, not arbitration, so it produces an agreement if the parties reach one,
  not a binding award either side is stuck with.
- Your lawyer must notify you in writing of the program and cannot sue you to collect a disputed
  fee until at least 30 days after you receive that notice. If you file your own request for fee
  dispute resolution within that 30 days, the State Bar's own rule says your lawyer is not
  permitted to pursue the lawsuit until the fee dispute process is finished. If you were sued for
  fees and never heard about the program, that is worth raising.
- North Carolina disciplines a lawyer for a "clearly excessive" fee, not simply an "unreasonable"
  one. That is a different, and arguably higher, bar than the model language most states use.
- A bar grievance in North Carolina has a 6-year filing deadline for most conduct, with no
  deadline at all for conduct that is a felony or that a court has found to be intentional.
- The Client Security Fund can reimburse up to $100,000 per applicant per attorney, but only for
  dishonest conduct such as theft of client funds, not for a dispute over whether a bill was fair.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct, numbering and adoption

The North Carolina Rules of Professional Conduct are adopted under the North Carolina State
Bar's rule-making authority, itself derived from N.C. Gen. Stat. § 84-23, and are administered
under Title 27 of the North Carolina Administrative Code. The Rules of Professional Conduct sit
at 27 NCAC Chapter 2; the State Bar's Grievance and disciplinary procedures sit separately at 27
NCAC Chapter 1, Subchapter B; the Fee Dispute Resolution Program and the Client Security Fund
sit at 27 NCAC Chapter 1, Subchapter D. Cite a rule of conduct as "Rule 1.5" in a letter, the
same numbering the Model Rules use; North Carolina did not renumber the fee, communication,
safekeeping, or termination rules relative to the model.

The official publication point is the State Bar's own site, ncbar.gov, which publishes each rule
as its own page (for example Rule 1.5 at
<https://www.ncbar.gov/for-lawyers/ethics-and-governing-rules/rules-of-professional-conduct/10-119-client-lawyer-relationship/15-fees/>). This
pack fetched Rule 1.5, Rule 1.16, and the Scope section directly from that site. Rule 1.5 was
originally approved by the North Carolina Supreme Court on July 24, 1997, and was last amended,
per the History Note published on the rule's own page, on April 21, 2021, with earlier amendments
on May 4, 2000, and February 27, 2003.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

Rule 1.5(a) lists eight factors relevant to whether a fee is "clearly excessive," and the list
tracks the national baseline's eight factors closely: the work required, whether taking the case
foreclosed other work, the local customary fee, the amount involved and the result, time
pressure, the length of the lawyer-client relationship, the lawyer's experience and reputation,
and whether the fee is fixed or contingent. North Carolina does not add a ninth or tenth factor
the way some states do.

The real deviation is the standard itself, not the factor list. Rule 1.5(a) does not use the
word "unreasonable." It prohibits a lawyer from making an agreement for, charging, or collecting
an "illegal or clearly excessive fee," and the eight factors are framed as what to consider in
deciding whether a fee is clearly excessive. For a client, that word matters: "clearly excessive"
is a harder standard to meet than plain unreasonableness, because a fee can be too high, in the
sense that a more careful or less busy lawyer would have charged less, without being clearly
excessive in the sense this rule requires for discipline. A fee dispute filed through the Fee
Dispute Resolution Program described in Section 3 does not require meeting this disciplinary
standard; that program looks at whether the fee was fair given the work done, not at whether the
fee crosses the "clearly excessive" line needed to discipline the lawyer. The factors themselves
still describe what makes a bill match, or fail to match, what actually happened in the matter,
so they remain useful for a fee dispute even though the rule's own trigger word for discipline is
different.

Rule 1.5(c) requires a contingent fee agreement to be in writing, signed by the client, stating
the fee method, the percentage or percentages at settlement, trial, or appeal, which expenses are
deducted from the recovery, and whether those expenses come out before or after the fee is
calculated. It also requires a written closing statement at the end of a contingent fee matter
showing the outcome and, if there was a recovery, how the amount paid to the client was
calculated. This tracks the model rule closely.

Contingent fees are barred outright by Rule 1.5(d) only for representing a defendant in a
criminal case (with a carve-out allowing a contingent fee in a criminal or civil asset forfeiture
proceeding) and for any other civil case where a contingent fee is separately prohibited "by
law." Unlike the Model Rule, Rule 1.5(d) does not itself name domestic relations matters as a
banned category in its own text; that restriction instead comes from Comment 7, which explains
that a lawyer may not charge a contingent fee where payment depends on securing a divorce or on
the amount of alimony or support obtained. Comment 7 also states this does not bar a contingent
fee for collecting a support, alimony, or other financial obligation that a court has already
ordered, because that kind of collection work does not raise the same policy concern. Whether the
comment's "divorce, alimony, or support" language extends to a property settlement, the way the
Model Rule's own text does, was not confirmed; see the gap list. No North Carolina-specific
statutory percentage cap on contingent fees generally was found within Rule 1.5. North Carolina
does separately require Industrial Commission approval of an attorney's fee in a workers'
compensation matter under G.S. 97-90, but that statute's own text does not state a percentage cap,
and workers' compensation fee approval is a specialized regime outside what this pack covers; see
the gap list.

No North Carolina-specific "nonrefundable fee" rule, separate from the general fee and
safekeeping requirements, was found in Rule 1.5.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

North Carolina runs a single, statewide Fee Dispute Resolution Program through the North
Carolina State Bar, at 27 NCAC 1D, Sections .0700 and following. This is not a local, bar-by-bar
patchwork the way it is in some states. The program's own public page is
<https://www.ncbar.gov/for-the-public/i-am-having-a-dispute-with-a-lawyer/fee-dispute-resolution-program/>.
The operative rule text of 27 NCAC 1D .0700 through .0709 is published on the State Bar's
governing-rules site, for example .0707 (processing requests) at
<https://www.ncbar.gov/for-lawyers/ethics-and-governing-rules/governing-rules/1d-rules-of-the-standing-committees-of-the-north-carolina-state-bar/0700-procedures-for-fee-dispute-resolution/0707-processing-requests-for-fee-dispute-resolution/>.

Participation is mandatory on the lawyer, not the client, once the client submits a proper
request. Rule 1.5(f) requires any lawyer who has a fee dispute with a client to make reasonable
efforts to advise the client of the program's existence at least 30 days before initiating legal
proceedings to collect the disputed fee, and, once the client requests fee dispute resolution, to
participate in the process in good faith. Comment 10 to Rule 1.5 states plainly that
participation is mandatory when a client requests it, and that a client's simple failure to pay,
not only an express dispute over amount, triggers the lawyer's duty to give notice of the
program, unless the client has expressly acknowledged owing the specific amount billed and says
only that they presently cannot pay it.

The State Bar's own procedural rule goes further than Rule 1.5(f)'s "reasonable efforts to
advise" language suggests on its own. 27 NCAC 1D .0707(a) states that a lawyer who has given the
required notice, and who has not received a client's request for resolution within 30 days after
the notice, "may" then institute suit or another proceeding to collect the fee. Read together with
Comment 10, this means the 30-day period operates as a real waiting period built into the State
Bar's own rules, not only a professional duty to make an effort: the lawyer is not authorized to
sue during that window. If the client does submit a timely request, .0707(a) further states that
the lawyer may take no further action to pursue collection of the fee, other than filing suit or
initiating a proceeding solely to preserve the lawyer's rights under an applicable statute of
limitation, until the fee dispute resolution process concludes. No case law confirming that a
North Carolina court has dismissed or stayed a fee suit for a lawyer's noncompliance with this
rule was located during this pack's research; the point above states what the State Bar's own
procedural rule requires of the lawyer, not a confirmed civil-procedure defense a court is shown
to enforce. See the gap list.

The program uses mediation, not arbitration. Comment 11 to Rule 1.5 states that the State Bar's
program "uses mediation to resolve fee disputes as an alternative to litigation," and that the
lawyer must cooperate with whoever is appointed to investigate and mediate the dispute. Because
it is mediation, the process is built to produce an agreed resolution, not an award one side can
be forced to accept the way a binding arbitration decision would bind them. If mediation does not
produce an agreement, nothing in the rule text read for this pack states that either side is
bound to a result; a client who does not settle in mediation is not shown to be worse off in a
later court action.

There is no dollar ceiling or floor on what the program will hear. 27 NCAC 1D .0701 through .0709
were each fetched and read directly for this pack, and none of them states a jurisdictional
dollar limit. There is also no fee to participate: the State Bar's own public fee dispute page
states plainly, "There is no fee to participate in this program." 27 NCAC 1D .0702(b) excludes
six categories of dispute from the program's jurisdiction: a fee already the subject of a court
judgment, arbitration award, or a pending lawsuit to collect the fee; a fee already resolved by
agreement between the lawyer and client; a fee set or approved by a court, administrative agency,
or other tribunal; a claim that is also the subject of a pending Client Security Fund
application; a fee dispute involving an out-of-state lawyer not licensed in North Carolina; and a
dispute over a matter unrelated to the reasonableness of a fee, such as an allegation of
malpractice or a disciplinary violation, which belongs in the grievance process described in
Section 4 instead.

The client's own deadline to request resolution is set by 27 NCAC 1D .0707(b): a request must be
submitted within three years after the lawyer's representation ends, or before the statute of
limitation for the lawyer to collect the fee expires, whichever is later. The State Bar's public
fee dispute page independently confirms this three-year figure, stating that a client "must
submit your request for resolution of disputed fee within three years after the last time that
your lawyer represented you," and adds a practical limit worth flagging: the program "cannot
accept a request for resolution of a disputed fee after the lawyer has filed a lawsuit to collect
the legal fee," so a client who waits until served with a collection suit has lost the ability to
use this program for that fee. Requests are submitted in writing to the coordinator of the Fee
Dispute Resolution Program at the State Bar's Raleigh office.

Rule 1.5(g) bars a lawyer from charging anything for the time spent responding to a fee dispute
resolution proceeding, the same as it bars charging for responding to a disciplinary inquiry or a
Client Security Fund claim, because Comment 13 treats those obligations as owed to the public and
the profession rather than billable client services.

## 4. Bar complaint process

The North Carolina State Bar's Grievance Committee investigates alleged violations of the Rules
of Professional Conduct, under 27 NCAC 1B. A person can start with a formal written complaint, on
the State Bar's own attorney complaint form, or, for a minor problem or a communication
breakdown, use the Attorney-Client Assistance Program (ACAP) first. The State Bar's own page
explaining this process is
<https://www.ncbar.gov/for-the-public/i-am-having-a-dispute-with-a-lawyer/having-issues-with-a-lawyer/>.

The State Bar's own site draws this split directly: ACAP "helps to resolve issues between
clients and lawyers on an informal basis," for someone with a minor problem or trouble
communicating who is not ready to file a formal complaint. A formal grievance is different in
kind. Once a complaint is submitted, the State Bar states plainly that it cannot be withdrawn,
because the State Bar has a duty to the public to complete its investigation, though a person can
still write to explain why they want to withdraw and that explanation will be considered. A pure
billing disagreement, where the lawyer did real work and the fight is over amount or
communication, more often belongs in ACAP or the Fee Dispute Resolution Program described above
than in a formal grievance, because a formal grievance is a disciplinary process aimed at whether
a rule was violated, not a process aimed at getting money back.

The Attorney-Client Assistance Program's own page lists (919) 828-4620 as its contact number for
someone who wants to raise an informal problem before deciding whether to file a formal
complaint.

There is a filing deadline for most grievances. 27 NCAC 1B .0111(f) requires that a grievance
generally be initiated within six years after the last act giving rise to it. Three categories
have no time limit at all: a grievance based on a guilty plea to, or conviction of, a felony; a
grievance based on conduct that constitutes a felony, whether or not the lawyer was ever charged
or convicted; and a grievance based on conduct a court has found to be intentional conduct
violating the Rules of Professional Conduct.

Discipline is not a refund, and this pack does not suggest otherwise. North Carolina does have a
mechanism that can touch money, though it is narrower than a straightforward restitution order.
Under 27 NCAC 1B .0129, a hearing panel deciding whether to reinstate a disbarred or suspended
lawyer may impose reasonable conditions necessary to protect the public, and the rule specifically
allows a panel to require a petitioner to show that entrusted client or fiduciary funds have been
properly reconciled and disbursed to their rightful owners before reinstatement is granted. That
is a condition attached to getting the lawyer's license back, not a court judgment ordering the
lawyer to pay a client, and it only comes into play if the lawyer has already been disbarred or
suspended and later seeks reinstatement.

## 5. Client protection fund

The Fund's own public page is
<https://www.ncbar.gov/for-the-public/i-am-having-a-dispute-with-a-lawyer/client-security-fund/>.

The Client Security Fund is administered by the North Carolina State Bar under 27 NCAC 1D,
Sections .1400 and following. It reimburses "reimbursable losses," defined as losses caused by an
attorney's dishonest conduct or by the mismanagement or mishandling of a general trust account
where a court later ordered the enjoined attorney's trust funds disbursed to the Fund. "Dishonest
conduct" is defined to mean embezzlement or the wrongful taking or conversion of money or
property entrusted to the lawyer by reason of the attorney-client or a related fiduciary
relationship, and it can include a lawyer's failure to provide meaningful legal services for fees
already advanced. An ordinary disagreement over whether a bill for work actually performed was
fair does not fit this definition and is excluded.

The rules list several explicit exclusions from a "reimbursable loss," including losses of the
attorney's own close relatives, partners, or employees; losses already covered by a bond,
security agreement, or insurance policy; losses of a business entity the attorney is affiliated
with; losses already reimbursed from another source; and losses from an investment transaction
where there was no contemporaneous attorney-client or customary fiduciary relationship between
the lawyer and the applicant.

The Fund will not reimburse an applicant more than $100,000 for losses caused by any one
attorney, under 27 NCAC 1D .1418(g). Reimbursement is expressly discretionary: the rule states in
capital letters that all reimbursements are "A MATTER OF GRACE IN THE SOLE DISCRETION OF THE
BOARD" administering the Fund and not a matter of right, and that no applicant has a right to
payment as a third-party beneficiary or otherwise. Whether awards are ordinarily paid in full up
to that cap, or reduced by percentage when the Fund's assets are limited relative to total claims
in a given year, was not clearly stated in the sections reviewed; the rule does direct the board
to weigh the total losses caused by one attorney and the Fund's available assets among the
factors it considers, which suggests a percentage reduction is possible in a heavy year, but no
confirmed statement of current practice was found. See the gap list.

No deadline running from the date of the loss or its discovery was found in the sections of the
Client Security Fund rules reviewed for this pack; see the gap list. Filing a formal disciplinary
grievance first is not stated as a strict precondition in the rule sections reviewed, and the
Fund's own public page describes filing a grievance as a recommended first step rather than an
absolute requirement in every case. The Fund's rules do require an applicant to exhaust other
available remedies, and the Fund's public materials describe this as requiring, at a minimum,
that the applicant have filed a fee dispute petition with the State Bar's Attorney-Client
Assistance Program where the loss involves a fee. Applications are filed in writing with the
State Bar's office in Raleigh, addressed to the Client Security Fund Board.

## 6. Small claims or equivalent court

North Carolina's small claims court is the Magistrate's Court, a division of District Court, set
out in Article 19 of G.S. Chapter 7A. A magistrate has jurisdiction over a civil action for money
owed, for possession of personal property, or for summary ejectment where the amount in
controversy does not exceed $10,000, under G.S. 7A-210 and G.S. 7A-211. This $10,000 figure is
set as part of the statutory definition itself, and the statute does not describe a mechanism for
county-level variation the way some states have; this pack treats the limit as statewide, not
county-varying, on the strength of that statutory text. Some non-primary sources describe a lower
figure in some counties; that claim was not confirmed against any primary source and is not
repeated here.

Either party may appeal a magistrate's judgment, under G.S. 7A-228. The only route is a trial de
novo before a District Court judge or jury; the magistrate's judgment is not reviewed on the
record. A notice of appeal must be given, orally in open court when the judgment is announced or
in writing filed with the magistrate's clerk within 10 days of the judgment. The appeal is
perfected by paying the District Court civil filing fees, within 10 days after judgment for a
summary ejectment case or within 20 days after judgment for any other small claim; failing to pay
in time results in automatic dismissal of the appeal. An appellant who cannot pay may instead file
an affidavit of indigency within the same window, which extends the payment deadline by 5 more
days if the affidavit is later found insufficient. G.S. 7A-228(e) also states that a party is not
required to have a lawyer to take or pursue this appeal, so self-representation continues into
the District Court trial de novo.

North Carolina small claims filings are exempt from the general rule that a corporation or other
business entity must be represented by a licensed attorney. G.S. 7A-222(c) states that,
notwithstanding G.S. 84-4, a party in a small claim action is not required to obtain legal
representation, so a corporation can appear through a non-lawyer representative such as an
owner or employee in Magistrate's Court, even though it generally cannot do so in District or
Superior Court.

The magistrate-assigned filing fee for a small claim is $96, computed from three separate court
cost line items in G.S. 7A-305(a): a $12.00 facilities fee, a $4.00 telecommunications and data
connectivity fee, and an $80.00 General Court of Justice fee for a civil action assigned to a
magistrate. This is the fee the statute sets; a reader should still confirm the current total
with the Clerk of Superior Court in the county of filing, since court costs are amended by the
General Assembly from time to time.

No North Carolina rule specific to suing a lawyer in Magistrate's Court, as opposed to any other
defendant, was found.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A claim on a fee agreement is generally a contract claim. G.S. 1-52(1) sets a three-year
limitation period for an action upon a contract, whether express or implied, except one under
seal or one governed by another specific statute.

Legal malpractice has its own statute, G.S. 1-15(c). The general rule is that a cause of action
for malpractice arising out of the performance of, or failure to perform, professional services
accrues at the time of the last act giving rise to the claim, and suit must be brought within
three years of that act, subject to the sections that follow. Where the injury is not readily
apparent to the claimant at the time it occurs, and is discovered, or reasonably should have been
discovered, two or more years after the act, the claimant has one year from discovery to file, but
in no event may suit be brought more than four years after the last act of the defendant giving
rise to the claim. G.S. 1-15(c) also has a narrower exception, unlikely to apply to a fee or
billing dispute, allowing suit up to ten years after the act for damage caused by a foreign
object negligently left in a person's body during a medical procedure.

Whether a North Carolina legal malpractice claim sounds in tort, in contract, or is available
either way at the client's election, was not confirmed against a case holding read directly for
this pack; G.S. 1-15(c)'s own text applies broadly to "malpractice arising out of the performance
of or failure to perform professional services," without itself resolving that characterization
question. See the gap list.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

The Rules of Professional Conduct's own Scope section, at paragraph (g), states directly that a
violation of a rule "should not give rise itself to a cause of action against a lawyer nor should
it create any presumption in such a case that a legal duty has been breached." The same paragraph
adds that the rules are not designed to be a basis for civil liability, and that nothing in the
rules should be read to expand a lawyer's substantive legal duties or the non-disciplinary
consequences of violating a rule. This tracks the national baseline's description of the Model
Rules' own Scope section with no deviation found.

Whether a proven rule violation is nonetheless admissible as evidence of the standard of care in
a North Carolina legal malpractice case was not researched; this kit does not cover malpractice
strategy.

A client's actual money routes in North Carolina are: a contract claim on the fee agreement, the
mandatory-on-the-lawyer Fee Dispute Resolution Program described above, and the Client Security
Fund where the loss involves dishonest conduct rather than a billing disagreement. Discipline,
including the narrow reinstatement-conditions mechanism described in Section 4, is a separate
track aimed at the lawyer's license, not a client's money.

## Before you rely on this

Confirm the current text of Rule 1.5 and the Scope section directly at ncbar.gov before citing a
rule in a letter. Confirm the Fee Dispute Resolution Program's current forms and procedure
directly with the State Bar's Fee Dispute Resolution coordinator before filing. Confirm the
current small claims filing fee with the Clerk of Superior Court in your county, since court
costs change by legislative amendment. See [verification_nc.md](verification_nc.md) for every
claim's source.
