# Kentucky

This pack is for someone in Kentucky who has a dispute with their own lawyer over fees,
billing, communication, or getting their file back. It does not cover suing a lawyer for
malpractice. Retrieval date for the sourcing in this pack is 2026-08-20.

What is different in Kentucky:

- Kentucky's Rules of Professional Conduct are Supreme Court Rule 3.130, cited by the
  parenthetical form "SCR 3.130(1.5)," not the colon form some states use.
- Kentucky's fee-reasonableness rule, SCR 3.130(1.5)(a), tracks the ABA Model Rule's eight
  factors with no additions found. This kit did not find a Kentucky-specific writing threshold
  or extra factor.
- The Kentucky Bar Association runs a statewide Legal Fee Arbitration Panel under SCR 3.810,
  but it is voluntary on both sides. If your lawyer declines to arbitrate, the process ends
  there. This is different from states where the lawyer must arbitrate if the client asks.
- Kentucky's Clients' Security Fund, under SCR 3.820, caps reimbursement at $50,000 per claim
  and $150,000 total per lawyer, and requires a claim within two years of when the client knew
  or should have known of the dishonest conduct.
- Kentucky's one-year limitation period for claims arising from a lawyer's professional
  services, KRS 413.245, is broad. A dispute that looks like a plain fee disagreement can still
  fall inside this one-year period if a court treats it as arising from professional services.
  Read the statutes of limitation section below before assuming you have more time.
- Kentucky law recognizes a lawyer's lien on money or property recovered for a client, but this
  kit did not find a general right for a Kentucky lawyer to hold your entire file hostage for
  unpaid fees. The Kentucky Bar Association's own ethics guidance says the opposite for most of
  the file. See the closing note in section 2.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct, numbering and adoption

The Kentucky Supreme Court adopted the current Rules of Professional Conduct by order,
Kentucky Supreme Court Order 2009-05, entered April 16, 2009, effective July 15, 2009. The
rules are codified as Supreme Court Rule (SCR) 3.130, with each individual rule identified by
a parenthetical, for example SCR 3.130(1.5) for the fee rule, SCR 3.130(1.4) for communication,
SCR 3.130(1.15) for safekeeping property, and SCR 3.130(1.16) for terminating representation.
When you write a letter, cite the rule this way: "SCR 3.130(1.5)."

The rules are published on the Kentucky Court of Justice website as part of the Supreme Court
Rules. The 2009-05 order itself, which contains the full current text and comments, is
published by the Kentucky Court of Justice at
<https://www.kycourts.gov/Courts/Supreme-Court/Supreme%20Court%20Orders/200905.pdf>. This kit did not find a
Kentucky-specific rule-numbering scheme that would cause a reader looking for "Rule 1.5" or
"Rule 1.16" to fail to find the right SCR 3.130 subsection. The subsection numbers match the
ABA Model Rules' numbers directly (SCR 3.130(1.5) is the fee rule, SCR 3.130(1.16) is the
termination rule, and so on), only the parenthetical citation form differs from the model.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

Kentucky's SCR 3.130(1.5)(a) lists the same eight reasonableness factors as the ABA Model
Rule, in the same order, with no additional factors and no factor omitted, based on a direct
reading of the rule text in the 2009-05 order. This kit did not find a Kentucky-specific
writing threshold, such as a dollar amount above which a fee agreement must be in writing.
SCR 3.130(1.5)(b) states the scope-and-basis disclosure shall be communicated to the client,
preferably in writing, before or within a reasonable time after starting the representation,
matching the model rule's structure. SCR 3.130(1.5)(c) and (d) on contingent fees, including
the prohibitions on contingent fees in domestic relations matters and in criminal defense,
also track the model rule as read in the same order.

SCR 3.130(1.15)(e), on safekeeping client property, requires legal fees paid in advance to be
deposited into a client trust account and withdrawn only as they are earned. There is one
Kentucky-specific exception worth flagging: SCR 3.130(1.5)(f) lets a lawyer designate a fee as
a "non-refundable retainer," which is exempt from the trust-account rule, but only if the
retainer is set out in a writing signed by the client that states the dollar amount, its
application to the scope of representation, and the time frame the agreement covers. If your
fee agreement uses the words "non-refundable retainer" and meets those writing requirements,
the trust-account and refund rules described elsewhere in this section may not apply to that
money. Check your own fee agreement for that language before assuming an advance payment must
be refunded.

One nuance worth flagging directly. SCR 3.130(1.16)(d) requires a lawyer, on termination, to
take steps to protect the client's interests, including surrendering papers and property the
client is entitled to and refunding any unearned advance fee; the rule text adds that "the
lawyer may retain papers relating to the client to the extent permitted by other law." Read on
its own, that sentence sounds like it could authorize a lawyer to hold your file until you pay.
It does not, in Kentucky. Comment (10) to that same rule, under the heading "Assisting the
Client upon Withdrawal," is direct: "The lawyer may not condition return of the client's file,
papers, and property upon payment of a fee." It goes on to explain that KRS 376.460 gives a
lawyer the right to have fees secured by a judgment the client recovers, but that a lawyer
"may withhold uncompensated work product from the client's returned files ... unless the
client's interests will be substantially prejudiced without" it. In other words, Kentucky
secures a lawyer's fee against money the client recovers, and does not give the lawyer a
general lien over the whole file. Note that the rule and its comments do not use the terms
charging lien or retaining lien; those are the names lawyers give the two ideas. The Kentucky
Bar Association's own ethics guidance reaches the same conclusion: Ethics Opinion E-395 (1997) answered "Qualified No" to the
question "May a lawyer hold a client's file because of a fee dispute?", finding no Kentucky
statute or case creating a retaining lien and limiting a lawyer to withholding only unpaid-for
work product. Ethics Opinion E-424 (2005), on a departing lawyer's duties to clients, reaffirms
E-395's holding in its own discussion of client files. Source for the rule and comment:
<https://www.kycourts.gov/Courts/Supreme-Court/Supreme%20Court%20Orders/200905.pdf>; source for
the opinions: <https://kybar.org/For-Members/Rules-Ethics-Information/Ethics-Opinions> (opinion
PDFs fetched directly, see the verification file). If a former lawyer tells you they are
holding your whole file for nonpayment, this is the rule to raise.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

The Kentucky Bar Association (KBA) runs a statewide Legal Fee Arbitration Panel program under
SCR 3.810, administered through the KBA's Dispute Resolution process. Details and the request
form are published at <https://kybar.org/For-Public/Dispute-Resolution>.

Participation is voluntary for both the client and the lawyer. This kit did not find any
provision making it mandatory on the lawyer when the client requests it. The published process
is: the client (or lawyer) submits a petition, a summary of the dispute, and a signed agreement
to arbitrate to the KBA. The KBA's Executive Director reviews the submission, and if it is in
order, sends it to the other party. The other party then has 20 days to agree to binding
arbitration. If the other party does not agree within that window, the KBA process ends there
and does not proceed to a hearing.

If both sides agree, the panel size depends on the amount in dispute: disputes under $10,000
are heard by one attorney arbitrator; disputes over $10,000 are heard by a panel of two
attorneys and one non-lawyer, with the non-lawyer appointed by the chief circuit judge of the
county where the client resides. The published page words these as "less than" and "more than"
$10,000 and does not say which panel hears a dispute of exactly $10,000; ask the KBA if your
dispute lands on that number. This kit did not find a filing fee for the KBA fee
arbitration program; the published materials describe it as a free service. This kit also did
not find a published filing deadline or window (for example, a set number of days from the
final bill) for requesting KBA fee arbitration; treat this as unconfirmed and do not wait to
find out, since the underlying claim is still running against the statutes of limitation in
section 7.

Because both sides must agree, this program only helps if the lawyer is willing to
participate. Kentucky's fee arbitration is a genuinely voluntary, negotiated process. It is
not a forum you can force your lawyer into the way you can in states where arbitration is
mandatory on the lawyer. The KBA's Agreement to Arbitrate Fee Dispute form confirms the same
mutual-agreement structure and states that awards are enforceable under Kentucky's Uniform
Arbitration Act, KRS Chapter 417, once both sides have signed on.

The KBA separately runs a Legal Negligence Arbitration program under SCR 3.800 for claims that
sound in malpractice rather than fees. That program is outside the scope of this kit.

## 4. Bar complaint process

Complaints against a Kentucky lawyer go to the KBA's Office of Bar Counsel (OBC), which the
KBA describes as an agency of the Kentucky Supreme Court responsible for investigating
complaints of lawyer misconduct. The complaint page and a downloadable complaint form are at
<https://kybar.org/For-Members/Office-of-Bar-Counsel/File-a-Complaint>. A complaint must be submitted in writing (the KBA
provides a form for this) and is reviewed by the OBC to determine whether it states a possible
violation of the Rules of Professional Conduct. If the OBC finds a possible violation, the
matter can be investigated and, if the Inquiry Commission finds probable cause, formal
disciplinary charges can follow.

A person who files a complaint is a witness in the disciplinary process, not a party to it,
and the OBC does not act as the complainant's lawyer or represent the complainant's financial
interests. This kit did not find a published deadline for filing a bar complaint against a
Kentucky lawyer; treat that as unconfirmed and file promptly rather than waiting.

Discipline is not a refund. The bar complaint process can result in a private or public
sanction against the lawyer, up to suspension or disbarment in serious cases, but it is a
separate track from getting your money back. This kit did not find a Kentucky disciplinary
restitution mechanism that orders a lawyer to pay money back to a client as part of the
disciplinary process itself; if you want money back, use fee arbitration (section 3), a
contract claim, or the Clients' Security Fund (section 5) if the loss involved dishonest
conduct, not a bar complaint.

## 5. Client protection fund

Kentucky's Clients' Security Fund (CSF) is established by the Kentucky Supreme Court under SCR
3.820 and administered by the KBA. Information and a claim process are published at
<https://kybar.org/For-Public/Clients-Security-Fund-FAQs>.

The CSF reimburses clients for losses caused by a Kentucky lawyer's dishonest conduct,
described in the KBA's materials as dishonesty, fraud, or unethical conduct occurring within
an attorney-client relationship, such as theft or misappropriation of client funds. It does
not cover ordinary legal malpractice, and it does not cover a plain fee dispute over how much
was reasonably owed. Reimbursement is limited to the claimant's actual, out-of-pocket
pecuniary loss; consequential or indirect losses are not covered.

Caps: the fund's published maximum is $50,000 per claim and $150,000 total per lawyer. If
claims against one lawyer exceed the aggregate cap, payouts are reduced on a pro rata basis.
Claims must be filed no later than two years after the client knew or reasonably should have
known of the dishonest conduct. There is no cost to file a CSF claim, no lawyer is required,
and the fund's rules do not allow a lawyer to charge a fee for helping prosecute a claim. The
fund is paid for out of a portion of KBA member dues, not tax dollars, and claims are handled
confidentially until a reimbursement decision is made. A panel of Trustees, three lawyers and
two non-lawyers appointed by the KBA Board of Governors, decides claims and meets roughly
twice a year.

## 6. Small claims or equivalent court

Kentucky's small claims court is the Small Claims Division of District Court, created and
described in KRS 24A.200. Its jurisdictional limit is $2,500, exclusive of interest and court
costs, under KRS 24A.230. This kit did not find a county-by-county variation in that dollar
limit; it is a single statewide figure set by statute.

Individuals may represent themselves or hire a lawyer in Small Claims Division. A corporation
or partnership must appear through an officer, a managerial employee, or a lawyer; it cannot
appear through a non-managerial employee. There is no jury trial available in Small Claims
Division itself; if a party wants a jury trial and the amount in dispute (money or personal
property) exceeds $250, that party can request the case be transferred to the regular Civil
Division of District Court by notifying the circuit court clerk's office at least seven days
before the small claims hearing. A losing party can appeal a small claims judgment to Circuit
Court by filing a Notice of Appeal with the circuit court clerk and paying a filing fee; on
appeal, no new evidence is presented, and the Circuit Court judge reviews the existing case
file to decide if the law was applied correctly. Source for this section: the Kentucky
Administrative Office of the Courts' Small Claims Handbook (form P-6), and KRS 24A.200 and
24A.230 directly.

This kit did not find a specific dollar filing fee published in the handbook or the statutes
for Small Claims Division; confirm the current fee with your county circuit court clerk's
office before filing. This kit also did not find a Kentucky small claims rule specific to
suing a lawyer; ordinary small claims procedure applies.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

Two different periods can apply to a dispute with your own lawyer, and which one applies
depends on how the claim is framed.

KRS 413.245 sets a one-year limitation period for a civil action "arising out of the
rendering of, or failure to render, professional services" by certain licensed professionals,
including lawyers, "whether brought in tort or contract." The clock runs from the date of the
occurrence, or, under the statute's discovery language, from the date the cause of action was
or reasonably should have been discovered, whichever is later, subject to the statute's own
tolling provisions (for example, for a plaintiff under legal disability). KRS 413.243 defines
"professional services" for purposes of this chapter. Because the statute's own text reaches
claims "whether brought in tort or contract," a dispute framed as a fee disagreement can still
fall inside this one-year period if a court treats the claim as arising from the rendering of
professional services rather than as an ordinary debt or breach-of-contract claim. This kit did
not independently confirm, from a primary Kentucky Supreme Court opinion, exactly where courts
draw that line between an ordinary fee-collection dispute and a claim "arising from
professional services." Treat the one-year period in KRS 413.245 as the period to plan around
unless you have confirmed with a lawyer that a longer period clearly applies to your specific
claim.

If a claim is a plain contract claim not arising from professional services, Kentucky's general
contract limitation periods apply instead, and they differ by whether the contract is written
or oral and, for written contracts, by when the contract was signed. Under KRS 413.090, a
written contract executed on or before July 15, 2014 has a 15-year limitation period. Under
KRS 413.160, a written contract executed after July 15, 2014 has a 10-year limitation period.
Under KRS 413.120, an action on an oral contract has a 5-year limitation period. Most fee
agreements are written, so KRS 413.090 or KRS 413.160 would be the relevant statute depending
on when you signed the agreement, if the claim is not instead governed by the shorter
professional-services period in KRS 413.245.

This kit did not find a separate statute of repose specific to legal malpractice claims in
Kentucky, distinct from the one-year discovery-based period already described in KRS 413.245.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

The Scope section of Kentucky's Rules of Professional Conduct, published as part of the
2009-05 order, states that violating a rule should not itself give rise to a cause of action
against a lawyer, and should not create any presumption that a legal duty was breached. It
also states that because the rules do establish standards of lawyer conduct, a rule violation
may be used as evidence of a breach of the applicable standard of conduct in an otherwise
recognized claim, such as a malpractice action. This mirrors the ABA Model Rules' Scope
language described in the national baseline file.

In practical terms, a rule violation on its own is not a lawsuit. Your money routes in
Kentucky are the ones described above: a contract or fee claim under the statutes in section 7,
the voluntary KBA fee arbitration program in section 3, or a claim to the Clients' Security
Fund in section 5 if the loss involved dishonest conduct. A bar complaint (section 4) can get a
lawyer disciplined, and a documented rule violation can support a later malpractice claim if
you have one, but this kit does not cover building or filing a malpractice claim.
