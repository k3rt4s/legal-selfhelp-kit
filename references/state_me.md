# Maine

For a person in Maine who is in a dispute with their own lawyer over fees, billing,
communication, getting a file back, or an unearned advance. Retrieval date for the sourcing in
this file is 2026-08-20. Read [national_baseline.md](national_baseline.md) first.

What is different in Maine:

- Maine's own reasonableness factors run to eleven, not eight. It adds whether the client gave
  informed consent to the fee arrangement, whether the fee agreement is in writing, and any
  other risks or benefits allocated by the agreement.
- Fee arbitration is mandatory on the lawyer, not the client. If the client asks for it, the
  lawyer must submit to it. There is no filing fee and no dollar floor or ceiling found in the
  rule that runs the program.
- A lawyer who wants to sue a client for unpaid fees must first mail the client written notice
  of the right to arbitrate at least 30 days before filing suit. Skipping that notice blocks
  the lawyer from enforcing any resulting fee judgment.
- The general contract limitation period is six years, longer than most states. Legal
  malpractice claims run on a separate rule that starts the clock on the date of the lawyer's
  act or omission, not on discovery, with two narrow exceptions.
- Maine's small claims dollar limit rose to $10,000 effective January 1, 2026. A version of the
  statute setting the older $6,000 limit is still visible on the legislature's own site as the
  prior text, so check the effective date before relying on either figure.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct, numbering and adoption

The Maine Supreme Judicial Court adopted the Maine Rules of Professional Conduct (MRPC),
effective August 1, 2009. The current published text carries amendments effective September
28, 2023. It is published by the Maine Judicial Branch at
[courts.maine.gov/rules/text/mr_prof_conduct_only_2023-09-28.pdf](https://www.courts.maine.gov/rules/text/mr_prof_conduct_only_2023-09-28.pdf).

Cite it in a letter as "Maine Rule of Professional Conduct 1.5" or "MRPC 1.5." The numbering
tracks the ABA model directly: Rule 1.4, Rule 1.5, Rule 1.15, and Rule 1.16 are exactly where a
reader familiar with the model would expect them.

There is a superseded-numbering trap to know about. Before August 1, 2009, Maine lawyers were
governed by the Maine Code of Professional Responsibility, which was itself organized as Bar
Rule 3, with provisions cited as "M. Bar R. 3.4," "M. Bar R. 3.5," and so on. That code was
abrogated on the same date the MRPC took effect. Some older Board of Overseers ethics opinions
still in circulation, including one used in section 2 below, cite that old Bar Rule 3
numbering. That numbering is no longer current. Do not cite "Bar Rule 3" in a letter today.

A separate document, the Maine Bar Rules, governs the regulatory and procedural side: the
Board of Overseers of the Bar, Bar Counsel, the Fee Arbitration Commission (Rule 7), the
Professional Ethics Commission (Rule 8), and the Grievance Commission (Rule 9). The current
Maine Bar Rules carry amendments effective July 10, 2025, published at
[courts.maine.gov/rules/text/m_bar_r_only_2025-07-14.pdf](https://www.courts.maine.gov/rules/text/m_bar_r_only_2025-07-14.pdf).
Bar Rule 1 establishes the Board as the statewide regulator: nine members appointed by the
Maine Supreme Judicial Court, six lawyers and three public members, three-year terms.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

This is the most valuable section. Maine's Rule 1.5(a) keeps the model's eight factors and
adds three more, numbered 9 through 11 in the rule itself:

- Whether the client gave informed consent to the fee arrangement.
- Whether the fee agreement is in writing.
- Any other risks allocated by the fee agreement, or potential benefits of the agreement,
  judged as of the time the agreement was made.

A written fee agreement is not an absolute requirement in Maine's rule, but whether one exists
is now a factor a lawyer's fee can be judged against.

Maine has an explicit nonrefundable fee rule the model rules do not have. Rule 1.5(h) allows a
lawyer to charge a fee that is earned on receipt and therefore not refundable, but only if the
lawyer confirms in writing, before or within a reasonable time after starting the
representation, that the fee is nonrefundable and what the client gets in exchange for it. A
lawyer cannot ask a client to waive in advance the right to challenge whether a nonrefundable
fee was reasonable. Any later agreement settling a dispute over a nonrefundable fee's
reasonableness requires the client to be separately represented, or advised in writing to seek
independent counsel and given a real chance to do so. The fee itself must still be reasonable
under all the factors in Rule 1.5(a).

Rule 1.5(g) puts the fee arbitration duty directly in the fee rule itself: a lawyer practicing
in Maine "shall submit, upon the request of the client," a fee dispute to arbitration under the
court's fee arbitration rules. See section 3.

Contingent fees are prohibited in a longer list of matter types than the model rule's two.
Rule 1.5(d) bars a contingent fee in any initial divorce, annulment, judicial separation,
paternity or parentage, parental rights and responsibilities, emancipation, grandparent
visitation, guardianship, or child support action, or in any post-judgment proceeding to
modify one of those, in a criminal defense, and in administering an estate in probate for a
fee based on a percentage of the estate's value.

On whether a lawyer may hold a client's file for unpaid fees: Rule 1.15(f) itself does not
answer this cleanly. It says that upon termination of representation, a lawyer shall either
return to the client, or retain and safeguard in a retrievable format, all information and
data to which the client is entitled. That is written as a choice, not as an unconditional duty
to hand the file over on request. If the lawyer keeps it, the rule requires safeguarding it for
at least eight years unless the client agrees in writing to a different period.

The Board of Overseers' Professional Ethics Commission has answered the fee-withholding
question directly, twice, found via the Board's ethics opinion index at
[mebaroverseers.org](http://www.mebaroverseers.org/attorney_services/ethics_opinions.html).
Ethics Opinion #51 (Dec. 5, 1984), reaffirmed as an Enduring Ethics Opinion in May 2017, holds
that a lawyer may not condition delivery of a client's file, or of material in it whose cost
the lawyer advanced, on the client paying the lawyer's fees or disbursements. The opinion
construed the fee-and-expense-lien language in the old Bar Rule 3.7(c)(1), the predecessor to
today's MRPC 1.8(i), as not authorizing a lien on the file itself, and says the lawyer's
remedy for nonpayment is a civil suit, not withholding the file. Ethics Opinion #120 (Dec. 11,
1991), reaffirmed April 2022, cites Opinion #51 approvingly on this exact point while
addressing how a file must be delivered.

Ethics Opinion #187 (Nov. 5, 2004, reaffirmed March 2019), read at
[maine.gov/tools/whatsnew/attach.php?id=89446&an=1](https://www.maine.gov/tools/whatsnew/attach.php?id=89446&an=1),
separately addresses what counts as "information and data to which the client is entitled": a
lawyer should deliver client property and anything of value, including pleadings,
correspondence, research memoranda, and notes from client and witness interviews, and may
ordinarily keep billing records, internal staffing memos, and most drafts. Opinion #187 does
not itself address withholding for unpaid fees; Opinion #51 is the one that does. Opinion #187
was written under the old, abrogated Bar Rule 3 numbering described in section 1, even though
it remains published as current guidance.

These are Professional Ethics Commission opinions, not a rule provision or a court holding, and
I did not find a Maine Law Court case addressing a retaining lien on a client's file. Within
that limit, the Board's own current, twice-reaffirmed guidance is that a Maine lawyer may not
hold a client's file to force payment in a fee dispute.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

Maine runs one statewide program through the Fee Arbitration Commission, part of the Board of
Overseers of the Bar, under Maine Bar Rule 7, published at
[courts.maine.gov/rules/text/m_bar_r_only_2025-07-14.pdf](https://www.courts.maine.gov/rules/text/m_bar_r_only_2025-07-14.pdf).

It is mandatory on the lawyer and voluntary for the client. MRPC Rule 1.5(g) requires a lawyer
to submit to arbitration on the client's request. Nothing in Bar Rule 7 requires the client to
use it; the client files a petition and, as a condition of filing, must agree in the petition
to be bound by the panel's decision (Rule 7(d)(1)(B)).

The award binds the client who filed, because filing requires that agreement up front. For the
lawyer, the award is enforced under the Uniform Arbitration Act, 14 M.R.S. section 5927 et
seq. Bar Rule 7(g) makes the Act's provision on compelling or staying arbitration, section
5928, inapplicable, and allows a challenge to the award only on the limited grounds and by the
procedure set out in 14 M.R.S. section 5938.

I found no filing fee and no dollar floor or ceiling in the text of Bar Rule 7. That is a
confirmed absence from the operative rule, not something I failed to look for.

The filing deadline runs from the bill or the payment, not from the end of the
representation. Bar Rule 7(d)(3) lets the Board Clerk recommend dismissal if arbitration was
not commenced within six years from whichever came first: when the disputed bill was rendered,
or when the fee was paid in whole or in part.

Before a lawyer can sue a client to collect fees, Bar Rule 7(d)(6) requires the lawyer to mail
the client written notice of the right to arbitrate at least 30 days before filing or serving
the suit, using specific language the rule sets out. Rule 7(d)(7) bars the lawyer from
enforcing any fee judgment obtained without giving that notice first.

## 4. Bar complaint process

Complaints against Maine lawyers go to the Board of Overseers of the Bar's Central Intake
Office, established under Bar Rule 3, with information on filing at
[mebaroverseers.org/complaint/index.html](https://www.mebaroverseers.org/complaint/index.html).
Central Intake screens every complaint, can dismiss it under written guidelines with a right of
review, or forward it to Bar Counsel and the Grievance Commission (Bar Rule 3(a), Bar Rule 9).

There is no separate formal-versus-informal track in the rule text; Central Intake performs a
single screening step for every complaint. Bar Rule 11 says proceedings under the Bar Rules
are exempt from all statutes of limitations, so there is no confirmed filing deadline.

The process can result in private or public discipline against the lawyer. It cannot order the
lawyer to pay the client anything. I read the Bar Rules for a restitution-ordering mechanism
and did not find one; the only mention of restitution in the rules is that a complainant's
acceptance of restitution from the lawyer does not by itself require dismissing a disciplinary
complaint, which confirms the two tracks are kept separate rather than that discipline can
produce a refund. A billing disagreement alone belongs in fee arbitration, section 3, not in a
disciplinary complaint.

## 5. Client protection fund

The Maine Lawyers' Fund for Client Protection is administered by a Board of Trustees under the
supervision of the Maine Supreme Judicial Court, governed by the Maine Rules for Lawyers' Fund
for Client Protection (MRLFCP), amendments effective April 22, 2019, published at
[courts.maine.gov/rules/text/mrlfcp_2019-04-24.pdf](https://www.courts.maine.gov/rules/text/mrlfcp_2019-04-24.pdf).
The rules took effect July 1, 1997, and the Fund pays only for dishonest conduct occurring
after January 1, 1999 (Rule 2(d)).

Coverage is limited to losses caused by a Maine lawyer's dishonest conduct, defined in Rule
10(c) as theft, embezzlement, or wrongful taking or conversion of money or property. Fee
disputes and ordinary malpractice are excluded by that definition; they are not dishonest
conduct. Rule 10(d) also excludes losses to the lawyer's own family members, partners, and
employees, losses covered by a bond or insurance policy, losses to financial institutions or
insurance companies, losses to any government entity, and losses from a lawyer with no
substantial Maine practice contacts.

The caps are written directly into the rule text: $50,000 per claim and $200,000 per lawyer
(Rule 13(a)). This is not left to a separate administrative vote. Reimbursement does not
include interest or incidental expenses (Rule 13(b)).

The deadline is a discovery rule with a repose backstop: a claim must be filed no later than
three years after the claimant knew or should have known of the dishonest conduct, and never
later than ten years after the dishonest conduct itself (Rule 10(b)). I found no requirement
that a disciplinary complaint be on file before a Fund claim can be filed; Rule 12(j) only says
an approved claim generally should not be paid while a disciplinary proceeding on the same
conduct is still pending.

Awards are discretionary. The Trustees may deny or reduce a claim for unjust enrichment, lack
of hardship, or the claimant's own unreasonable contribution to the loss (Rule 10(f)), and
approving a claim takes at least four trustees' affirmative votes (Rule 12(h)).

## 6. Small claims court

Maine's small claims actions are heard in the District Court, with appeals to the Superior
Court, under the Maine Rules of Small Claims Procedure (MRSCP), amendments effective November
1, 2024, published at
[courts.maine.gov/rules/text/mrscp%202024-11-01.pdf](https://www.courts.maine.gov/rules/text/mrscp%202024-11-01.pdf).

The jurisdictional limit is set by statute, 14 M.R.S. section 7482, at
[legislature.maine.gov/statutes/14/title14sec7482.html](https://legislature.maine.gov/statutes/14/title14sec7482.html).
The statute carries two versions on the legislature's own site: a claim up to $6,000 was the
limit in text effective until January 1, 2026, and a claim up to $10,000, exclusive of interest
and costs, is the limit in text effective January 1, 2026 and after. As of this pack's
retrieval date, the $10,000 figure is the one in force. The Maine Judicial Branch's own 2026
small claims guide confirms the $10,000 figure in plain language.

A party may be represented by an attorney (MRSCP Rule 16(a)). A corporation, partnership, sole
proprietorship, or governmental entity may instead be represented by a non-lawyer officer or
employee (Rule 16(b)); that is a specific small claims accommodation, not the rule in ordinary
civil court.

Appeal rights differ depending on who appeals, under MRSCP Rule 11(d). A plaintiff who appeals
gets review on questions of law only, decided by the Superior Court on the record, without a
jury. A defendant who appeals may choose the same record review, or may instead demand a jury
trial de novo, but only by making a written demand on the notice of appeal and filing a
supporting affidavit meeting the standard of Maine Rule of Civil Procedure 56(e), showing a
genuine issue of material fact for a jury. A defendant who does not make that demand and file
that affidavit gets the same record-only review a plaintiff gets. The appeal window is 30 days
from entry of judgment, extendable up to 30 more days on a showing of excusable neglect.

The confirmed cost to appeal: the entry fee for a Civil Appeal to the Superior Court is
$175.00, per the Judicial Branch's current fee schedule, Administrative Order JB-05-26,
effective March 9, 2026, at
[courts.maine.gov/adminorders/jb-05-26.pdf](https://www.courts.maine.gov/adminorders/jb-05-26.pdf).
The appellant also pays for a transcript of the hearing if a recording was made, ordered on the
Transcript and Audio Order Form CV/CR-165, and the fee order does not fix that as a flat amount.

If you are the defendant and you demand a jury trial, add $300.00. The Judicial Branch's own
guide to small claims cases, "A Guide to Small Claims Cases," PUB-006, January 2026, at
[courts.maine.gov/help/guides/sc-guide-2026.pdf](https://www.courts.maine.gov/help/guides/sc-guide-2026.pdf),
says that a defendant appealing on questions of fact may request a jury trial in Superior Court
and that there is a fee for a jury trial, and it points the reader to Administrative Order
JB-05-26 for the amount. That order lists a "Jury Trial Fee in Superior Court" of $300.00. So
the jury fee does apply to a small claims appeal. The guide also says the notice of appeal fee,
the transcript fee, and the jury trial fee are all non-refundable.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

The general contract limitation period is six years. 14 M.R.S. section 752 says all civil
actions must be commenced within six years after the cause of action accrues, except where
another statute specially provides otherwise, at
[legislature.maine.gov/statutes/14/title14sec752.html](https://legislature.maine.gov/statutes/14/title14sec752.html).
This is Maine's general catch-all period and is longer than the contract period in many other
states. A separate, narrower 20-year period in 14 M.R.S. section 751 applies only to contracts
under seal, promissory notes signed before an attesting witness, and bank-issued debt
instruments, not to an ordinary fee agreement dispute.

Legal malpractice has its own accrual rule in 14 M.R.S. section 753-B, at
[legislature.maine.gov/statutes/14/title14sec753-B.html](https://legislature.maine.gov/statutes/14/title14sec753-B.html).
The statute covers professional negligence, malpractice, and breach of contract for legal
service under one rule, so the tort-versus-contract label does not change which accrual rule
applies. As a general rule, the clock starts on the date of the lawyer's act or omission that
caused the injury, not on the date the client discovers the problem: this is an occurrence
rule, not a discovery rule. There are two named exceptions: a claim about a real estate title
opinion uses a discovery rule but can never be brought more than 20 years after the act or
omission (a statute of repose), and a claim about drafting a will offered for probate uses a
discovery rule with no repose period stated in the text. Outside those two exceptions, I found
no general statute of repose for legal malpractice in Maine. A predecessor statute, 14 M.R.S.
section 753-A, also titled "Actions against attorneys," was repealed in 2001; do not cite it.

Section 753-B fixes when the clock starts, not how long it runs on its own. The Maine Law
Court has confirmed that the six-year catch-all in section 752 supplies the length. In
Packgen, Inc. v. Bernstein, Shur, Sawyer & Nelson, P.A., 2019 ME 90, the Law Court affirmed
dismissal of a legal malpractice claim as time-barred under "the six-year statutes of
limitations for civil actions, 14 M.R.S. section 752, and attorney malpractice actions, 14
M.R.S. section 753-B," and referred throughout to "the six-year limitation period created by
section 752." Read together, section 753-B sets the accrual date and section 752 supplies the
six-year length, confirmed by the Law Court's own opinion at
[courts.maine.gov/courts/sjc/lawcourt/2019/19me090re.pdf](https://www.courts.maine.gov/courts/sjc/lawcourt/2019/19me090re.pdf).

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

A violation of the Maine Rules of Professional Conduct does not by itself create a civil cause
of action. This is stated in the Preamble to the MRPC, in the numbered Scope discussion at
paragraph 20: violation of a rule "should not itself give rise to a cause of action against a
lawyer nor should it create any presumption in such a case that a legal duty has been
breached," and the rules "are not designed to be a basis for civil liability." This is the
Scope section of the professional conduct rules themselves, in the same mrpc.txt document
fetched from the same courts.maine.gov filename cited in section 1, not the separate judicial
conduct code Maine also publishes.

The same paragraph does allow a rule violation to be used as evidence: "since the Rules do
establish standards of conduct by lawyers, a lawyer's violation of a Rule may be evidence of
breach of the applicable standard of conduct." That leaves the door open for a violation to
support a malpractice claim's standard-of-care element, without creating a standalone claim on
its own.

The actual money routes for a client are the ones covered above: a contract claim on the fee
agreement, fee arbitration under section 3, and the client protection fund under section 5 if
the loss involves dishonest conduct rather than a fee dispute.
