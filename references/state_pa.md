# Pennsylvania

This pack is for a person in Pennsylvania who has a fee, billing, communication, or file-return
dispute with their own lawyer. It does not cover legal malpractice as a claim to run yourself.
Retrieved 2026-08-20.

What a Pennsylvania reader most needs to know, that a reader in another state does not:

- Pennsylvania has no statewide attorney fee arbitration program. Fee disputes are handled by
  local county bar Fee Dispute Committees, and every one of them is voluntary. Nothing forces
  your lawyer to show up. This is the opposite of a "mandatory on the lawyer" state.
- Rule 1.5(b) requires the fee basis to be in writing when the lawyer has not regularly
  represented the client. Pennsylvania's rule drops the word "preferably" that softens the ABA
  model rule, so this is closer to a real writing requirement than the national baseline
  describes.
- Pennsylvania's small claims equivalent is the magisterial district court almost everywhere,
  with a $12,000 limit, but Philadelphia uses a separate Municipal Court with its own $12,000
  civil limit. Neither court requires a lawyer, and a corporation can appear through a
  non-lawyer officer or authorized employee.
- The Pennsylvania Lawyers Fund for Client Security caps an individual award at $150,000 and
  caps total payouts for one dishonest lawyer at $1,000,000. It excludes both fee disputes and
  malpractice by name.
- Rule 1.16(d) lists four duties on termination, matching the ABA model list exactly. If your
  pack for another state describes more steps, that is that state's addition, not a
  Pennsylvania requirement.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct, numbering and adoption

Pennsylvania adopted the Rules of Professional Conduct by order of the Supreme Court of
Pennsylvania dated October 16, 1987, effective April 1, 1988. They are codified at 204 Pa.
Code, Chapter 81. Cite them as "Pa.R.P.C." followed by the rule number, for example
"Pa.R.P.C. 1.5." The rule numbers a client is most likely to need sit at the same numbers a
reader would expect from the model rules: Pa.R.P.C. 1.4 (communication), 1.5 (fees), 1.15
(safekeeping property), and 1.16 (declining or terminating representation).

The official publication point is the Pennsylvania Code and Bulletin at
<https://www.pacodeandbulletin.gov/Display/pacode?file=/secure/pacode/data/204/chapter81/chap81toc.html>,
maintained jointly with the Legislative Reference Bureau. That site states it reflects changes
through 56 Pa.B. 3438 (June 6, 2026). The Disciplinary Board of the Supreme Court of
Pennsylvania also publishes a consolidated PDF of the rules at padisciplinaryboard.org, dated
November 14, 2024 in its most recent posted revision as of this pack's retrieval, but that PDF
could not be read directly by this pack's tooling; the pacodeandbulletin.gov page was used
instead for every rule quoted in this pack. See
[verification_pa.md](verification_pa.md).

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

Pennsylvania's eight reasonableness factors at Pa.R.P.C. 1.5(a) track the ABA model list in
substance, with one visible difference: Pennsylvania lists "whether the fee is fixed or
contingent" first, as factor (1), where the model rule places the same factor last, as factor
(8). No factor is added, dropped, or reworded beyond that reordering. If you are comparing your
bill against the eight factors, treat them as the same eight questions in a different order,
not as a shorter or longer list.

Pa.R.P.C. 1.5(b) requires the basis or rate of the fee to be communicated to the client, in
writing, before or within a reasonable time after the representation begins, when the lawyer
has not regularly represented that client. The ABA model rule says only that this
communication is "preferably" in writing. Pennsylvania's version drops that word, so for a new
client the fee basis is supposed to be in writing, not merely explained. If you never got
anything in writing about how you would be charged, that is a Pa.R.P.C. 1.5(b) problem on its
own, separate from whether the amount later billed was reasonable.

Pa.R.P.C. 1.5(c) requires a contingent fee agreement to be in writing, signed, and to state the
method for determining the fee, including the percentages that apply at settlement, at trial,
and on appeal, and whether litigation expenses are deducted before or after the contingent fee
is calculated. At the end of the matter the lawyer must give the client a written statement
showing the outcome and how any amount paid to the client was calculated. No dollar cap,
percentage cap, or sliding scale for contingent fees appears in the rule.

Pa.R.P.C. 1.5(d) prohibits a contingent fee in a domestic relations matter where the fee or its
amount depends on securing a divorce or on the amount of alimony or support, and in
representing a defendant in a criminal case. The rule's own text, as published, does not extend
this domestic relations prohibition to a fee contingent on the amount of a property settlement
the way some other states' rules do; this pack states that only as a reading of the section
quoted, not as a holding of any court, and it should be checked against the current rule text
before you rely on it.

Comment 6 to Pa.R.P.C. 1.5 says a lawyer should conscientiously consider submitting to a fee
dispute procedure the bar has established, such as arbitration or mediation. That is
aspirational language in a Comment, not a command in the rule text itself. It is also the closest thing Pennsylvania's rules come
to requiring a lawyer to use fee arbitration, and it does not require anything. See Section 3.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

Pennsylvania has no statewide fee arbitration or mediation program. Fee dispute handling is run
entirely by county bar associations, and every program confirmed for this pack is voluntary for
both sides. The Disciplinary Board's own complaint-filing page,
<https://www.padisciplinaryboard.org/for-the-public/file-complaint>, says fee disputes are not
normally handled within the attorney disciplinary process, and directs a complainant to pursue
the dispute with the bar association in the county where the attorney practices instead.

Programs confirmed directly from each bar's own materials:

- Philadelphia Bar Association Fee Dispute Resolution Program
  (<https://philadelphiabar.org/?pg=FAQFeeDisputes>). Free. Voluntary: neither side
  can be forced to mediate or arbitrate, except that a lawyer who joined the Bar's Lawyer
  Referral and Information Service panel has already agreed in advance to participate if a
  referred client requests arbitration. A petition must be filed within four years of the last
  legal service performed or the last payment made, whichever is later. An arbitration award,
  once both sides agree to arbitrate, is final and binding and cannot be appealed to court,
  though it can be enforced there if the losing side does not pay.
- Allegheny County Bar Association Special Fee Determination Committee
  (<https://www.acba.org/docDownload/2321588>). Its own rules exclude
  disputes of $1,000 or less, or more than $25,000, from its jurisdiction, so it hears amounts
  above $1,000 and up to $25,000. The attorney must be an ACBA member.
- Bucks County Bar Association Fee Dispute Committee
  (<https://bucksbar.org/community-resources/fee-dispute/>). Free. Voluntary for both sides; each must
  agree before a hearing is held. If both submit to a decision, that decision is legally
  binding on both.
- Berks County Bar Association fee dispute process (<https://berksbar.org/?pg=feeresolution>).
  Voluntary. Binding only if both the client
  and the attorney agree to arbitration; either side can decline and keep the right to go to
  court.
- Chester County Bar Association Fee Dispute Program (<https://www.chescobar.org/?pg=FeeDispute>).
  Covers attorneys whose main offices are
  in Chester County, resolved through arbitration. Also covers professional-fee disputes
  between attorneys and physicians, stenographers, and other professionals if both sides agree
  to be bound.
- Montgomery County Bar Association Fee Dispute Committee
  (<https://www.montgomerybar.org/?pg=problem-with-your-attorney>). Requires both the client and the
  named attorney to agree to appear before an informal conference; agreeing to appear also
  means agreeing to be bound by the decision, with no appeal to court.
- Dauphin County Bar Association Fee Dispute Committee (<https://www.dcba-pa.org/fee-dispute-program/>).
  Free. Voluntary for both the client and
  the DCBA member, though the association "strongly encourages" its members to cooperate.

This list is drawn only from county bar programs whose own materials this pack could reach and
read; Pennsylvania has more counties than are listed here, and a county not listed may or may
not have its own program. Ask your county bar association directly.

No confirmed program made participation mandatory on the lawyer for a client-initiated dispute.
No confirmed program described a lawyer's duty to notify the client of a right to arbitrate
before suing for fees. Dollar ceilings and floors, filing deadlines, and filing fees were
confirmed only where stated above; where a program's page did not state one, this pack does not
guess at it. See the gap list in [verification_pa.md](verification_pa.md).

If a county program exists where you are, it is usually the cheapest and fastest way to test
whether a bill was reasonable, but only if your lawyer agrees to participate. If your lawyer
will not agree, or your county has no program, the realistic path is a written demand followed
by the magisterial district court or Philadelphia Municipal Court (Section 6).

## 4. Bar complaint process

The Disciplinary Board of the Supreme Court of Pennsylvania is Pennsylvania's disciplinary
body, operating through its Office of Disciplinary Counsel. A complaint can be filed online or
by mailing a form, both reached from
<https://www.padisciplinaryboard.org/for-the-public/file-complaint>, to
the district office where the attorney practices; forms are offered in nine languages. Filing
produces an acknowledgment, a file number, an investigation by the Office of Disciplinary
Counsel, and eventual notice of the outcome.

The Board's own file-a-complaint page says fee disputes are not normally handled within the
attorney disciplinary process, and tells a complainant to pursue a fee dispute with the bar
association in the county where the attorney practices (Section 3) instead of through
discipline.

No filing deadline for a disciplinary complaint was found on the Board's own complaint-filing
page. This pack does not state one either way; see the gap list.

Discipline is not a refund. It puts a lawyer's conduct on the record and can lead to sanctions,
but getting money back runs through a separate forum: fee arbitration where a county program
exists (Section 3), a contract claim in court (Section 6), or the Client Security Fund where the
lawyer's own conduct was dishonest (Section 5).

Pa.R.P.C. 1.15 requires client funds to be held separately from the lawyer's own funds and
governs Pennsylvania's IOLTA (Interest on Lawyers Trust Accounts) program in detail, including
monthly reconciliations and multi-year record retention. If a lawyer took a retainer, did
little or no work, and the money is not there to refund, that is a trust account and honesty
problem, not just a billing disagreement, and it belongs in front of the Disciplinary Board and
potentially the Client Security Fund rather than a fee dispute committee.

Pa.R.P.C. 1.16(d) requires a lawyer, on termination, to take four steps as far as reasonably
practicable: giving reasonable notice, allowing time to hire other counsel, surrendering papers
and property the client is entitled to, and refunding any advance payment of a fee or expense
that was not earned or incurred. That is the same four items the ABA model rule lists, in the
same substance. A written request for your file and a written request for a refund of unearned
fees can go in the same letter.

## 5. Client protection fund

The Pennsylvania Lawyers Fund for Client Security, administered by the Disciplinary Board under
204 Pa. Code, Chapter 83, Subchapter E, reimburses clients for losses caused by a Pennsylvania
lawyer's defalcation, meaning a conversion or misappropriation of money or property the lawyer
held for the client. It is not malpractice insurance and it is not a fee dispute forum, at
<https://palawfund.com>, phone 717-231-9510 (toll-free 800-962-4618), both listed at
<https://palawfund.com/contact-us/>.

What it covers: a claim that an attorney received money or property on the client's behalf, in
an attorney-client or related fiduciary relationship such as executor, guardian, trustee, or
escrow agent, and then converted it instead of delivering or accounting for it.

What it excludes, stated on the Fund's own site: claims alleging legal malpractice or
ineffective assistance of counsel, fee disputes as the Fund's own Board determines them, and
consequential or incidental damages such as lost interest or the cost of pursuing a
civil suit against the lawyer. Claims by the lawyer's spouse, close relatives, or employees are
also excluded.

The per-claimant maximum award is $150,000. The aggregate cap on payouts tied to one attorney is
$1,000,000, effective February 24, 2014, though the Board may ask the Supreme Court of
Pennsylvania to permit exceeding it. Both figures are stated on the Fund's own frequently asked
questions page, <https://palawfund.com/frequently-asked-questions/>, and in 204 Pa. Code Rule
514, <https://www.pacodeandbulletin.gov/Display/pacode?file=/secure/pacode/data/204/chapter83/s514.html>.
Awards are discretionary; the rule itself, 204 Pa. Code Rule 502,
<https://www.pacodeandbulletin.gov/Display/pacode?file=/secure/pacode/data/204/chapter83/s502.html>,
describes payments as "a matter of grace and not of right," and there is no appeal from the
Board's decision and no judicial review of it.

If a disciplinary complaint has not already been filed with the Disciplinary Board, a completed
Disciplinary Complaint Form must be submitted along with the Fund claim, unless the attorney is
deceased. No filing deadline, whether tied to the date of the loss or its discovery, was found
on the Fund's own published materials. This pack does not state one; see the gap list.

## 6. Small claims or equivalent court

Pennsylvania's small claims equivalent is the magisterial district court almost everywhere in
the state, designed for a non-lawyer to use without counsel. Its civil jurisdictional limit is
$12,000, exclusive of interest and costs, at 42 Pa.C.S. § 1515,
<https://www.legis.state.pa.us/WU01/LI/LI/CT/HTM/42/00.015.015.000..HTM>. A plaintiff whose
claim exceeds $12,000 may waive the excess to stay within that limit; the waiver is revoked
automatically if the case is later appealed.

Philadelphia does not use a magisterial district court. It has its own Philadelphia Municipal
Court, which exercises the equivalent small claims jurisdiction there, also up to $12,000,
exclusive of interest and costs, at 42 Pa.C.S. § 1123(a)(4),
<https://www.legis.state.pa.us/WU01/LI/LI/CT/HTM/42/00.011.023.000..HTM>. The statute itself
states this limit is meant to let litigants proceed without hiring counsel. If you are in
Philadelphia, "small claims" means Municipal Court, not a magisterial district court, and the
two are not interchangeable.

Under Pa.R.Civ.P.M.D.J. 207, a corporation or similar entity may appear in magisterial district
court through an attorney, through an officer, or through an employee or authorized agent who
has personal knowledge of the matter and written authorization from an officer to appear. A
corporation is not required to hire a lawyer at this level, which is unusual; in many courts a
corporate party must be represented by counsel.

Either side may appeal a magisterial district court judgment for money to the Court of Common
Pleas within 30 days, under Pa.R.C.P.M.D.J. 1002. The appeal is then heard as a trial de novo, a
full new hearing rather than a review for error, under Pa.R.C.P.M.D.J. 1007.

No filing fee figure could be confirmed against an official court fee schedule for this pack;
fees vary by the amount claimed and by county, and figures found during research came only from
non-official summaries. Confirm the current fee with the magisterial district court or Municipal
Court where you plan to file. No rule specific to suing a lawyer in either court was found.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A fee agreement dispute is a contract claim. Pennsylvania's statute of limitations for a
contract claim, whether the contract is written, an express contract not founded on a writing,
or a contract implied in law, is four years, at 42 Pa.C.S. § 5525(a). Pennsylvania does not
shorten the period for an oral or unwritten agreement the way some states do; written and
unwritten contract claims share the same four years.

Legal malpractice, a claim that the lawyer's own negligence or breach of the engagement caused
you harm, needs its own counsel rather than self-help. It is not covered further in this kit
beyond the deadline, included so you know a clock exists and can get counsel in time. Pennsylvania
case law, most often cited as Bailey v. Tucker, 533 Pa. 237, 621 A.2d 108 (1993), describes a
legal malpractice claim in Pennsylvania as available in trespass (a negligence theory) or in
assumpsit (a contract theory), with different limitation periods attached: two years for a
trespass claim under 42 Pa.C.S. § 5524, and four years for an assumpsit claim on an oral contract
under 42 Pa.C.S. § 5525, or the period stated in Section 5525 for a written one. This pack read
that opinion through a non-official case-law mirror, not the official appellate reporter or a
pacourts.us archive of a case that old, so treat the tort-versus-contract election as widely
cited rather than independently confirmed by this pack; see
[verification_pa.md](verification_pa.md). Which theory is available, and which period actually
applies to a given set of facts, is a question for a malpractice lawyer, not this kit.

Pennsylvania case law, most often cited as Fine v. Checcio, 870 A.2d 850 (Pa. 2005), describes a
discovery rule that can delay when either clock starts: the period does not begin until the
client neither knew nor reasonably should have known, using reasonable diligence, that an injury
occurred and what caused it. This pack read that opinion through a non-official case-law mirror
as well, so states the existence and shape of the rule without treating it as independently
confirmed against an official reporter, and does not attempt to apply it to any set of facts; see
[verification_pa.md](verification_pa.md) for how this claim was sourced. No statute of repose
specific to legal malpractice was found in this research; see the gap list.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

Pennsylvania's Rules of Professional Conduct are a disciplinary code, not a source of civil
liability by themselves. The Scope section at 204 Pa. Code § 81.2, paragraph 19, states that
"violation of a Rule should not itself give rise to a cause of action against a lawyer nor
should it create any presumption in such a case that a legal duty has been breached." This
pack's research did not locate language in that same section affirmatively permitting a proven
violation to be used as evidence of the standard of care in a malpractice case, the way the ABA
model rule's own Scope commentary does; whether Pennsylvania courts allow that use in practice
was not researched further, because this kit does not cover malpractice strategy. See the gap
list.

Your actual money route runs through the other forums in this pack: a contract claim in
magisterial district court, Philadelphia Municipal Court, or another court (Section 6), a county
fee dispute program where your lawyer will agree to use it (Section 3), or the Client Security
Fund where the lawyer's conduct was dishonest rather than merely a bad bill (Section 5). Getting
the lawyer disciplined (Section 4) is a separate goal from getting money back, and it runs
through a separate forum.
