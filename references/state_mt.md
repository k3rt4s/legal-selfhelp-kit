# Montana

This pack is for someone in a dispute with their own lawyer over fees, billing,
communication, getting their file back, or an unearned advance payment. It does not cover
suing a lawyer for malpractice. Retrieval date for this pack is 2026-08-20.

Read [national_baseline.md](national_baseline.md) first. This file covers only what Montana
does differently, plus the Montana-specific programs and deadlines.

What a reader in Montana most needs to know:

- Montana's fee rule requires a written fee-basis disclosure in every matter expected to cost
  more than $500. The ABA model only prefers this in writing. Montana has required it in
  writing since a 2004 rule revision.
- Montana's lawyer lien statute reaches money and property outcomes, such as a settlement,
  judgment, or arbitration award. It does not reach your file or papers. A narrower rule lets a
  lawyer hold papers tied to the specific unpaid matter, but only up to what is not "reasonably
  necessary to protect the client's interest" when the representation ends.
- Montana runs a statewide fee arbitration program through the State Bar. It works differently
  depending on who is accused of owing money: if the client files against the lawyer and the
  lawyer will not sign on, the case proceeds without the lawyer and the decision still becomes
  binding on the lawyer. If the lawyer files against the client and the client will not sign
  on, the matter is simply closed. That asymmetry favors a client who files first.
- Montana's small claims court lets attorneys appear only if every party in the case has one.
  One side cannot bring a lawyer unless the other side does too.
- Montana has a dedicated legal malpractice statute, separate from its general contract
  statutes: 3 years from discovery, with a 10-year outside limit from the act itself.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct, numbering and adoption

The Montana Supreme Court adopts the Montana Rules of Professional Conduct (MRPC) by court
order, docketed under an Administrative File (AF) number. The current rules were last amended
by order in AF 09-0688, with the last amendment dated October 29, 2020. Cite a rule in a letter
as "Montana Rule of Professional Conduct 1.5" or "MRPC 1.5."

Montana follows standard Model Rule numbering for the rules this kit covers: 1.4
(communication), 1.5 (fees), 1.8(i) (lien and file-retention provisions), 1.15 (safekeeping
property), and 1.16 (declining or terminating representation). A reader looking for those
numbers will find them in the same place as in the ABA model.

The current comprehensive numbering traces to a 2002-2003 review by the State Bar of Montana's
Ethics Committee and Board of Trustees, adopted by the Montana Supreme Court effective April 1,

2004. A further rule-by-rule comparison to the ABA Model Rules, filed with the Court in AF

09-0688, records that the 2004 revision is when Montana first made the Rule 1.5(b) fee-basis
disclosure mandatory in writing (see section 2).

Official text: the Montana Supreme Court's Law Library publishes the full current rules at
https://courts.mt.gov/external/rules/mtr_prof_cond.pdf. The State Bar of Montana also publishes
the rules at https://www.montanabar.org/For-Attorneys/Ethics-Resources/Professional-Conduct.
Both copies were read for this pack and
match on every provision checked.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

Montana Rule 1.5(a) lists the same eight reasonableness factors as the ABA model, in the same
order and substantially the same wording. No deviation was found in the factor list itself.

The real deviation is in Rule 1.5(b), and it is significant:

- Montana requires the scope of representation and the basis or rate of fees and expenses to be
  communicated to the client in writing, before or within a reasonable time after the
  representation begins. The ABA model only says this should "preferably" be in writing. Any
  later change in the scope or the fee basis must also be communicated in writing.
- The one exception: this paragraph does not apply to a matter in which it is reasonably
  foreseeable that the total cost to the client, including attorney fees, will be $500 or less.
  There is also a standing exception, matching the model rule, for a client the lawyer
  regularly represents on the same fee basis.
- The State Bar's own rule-comparison chart, filed as an attachment to AF 09-0688, states
  plainly that "Montana has mandated a written fee agreement since 2004." If your lawyer never
  gave you anything in writing about the fee basis for a matter over $500, that is itself a
  rule violation, separate from whether the amount charged was reasonable.

On the file-and-lien question, Montana's structure is layered across two provisions, and
neither the rule nor the statute uses the words "file" or "hold your papers hostage." Read
together:

- Montana's attorney lien statute, MCA 37-61-420(2), gives a lawyer a lien only on the outcome
  of the legal matter: a settlement, decree, verdict, arbitration award, report, decision,
  contractual consideration, or judgment received by the client. Subsection (3) extends this to
  property that is the subject of a transactional matter. This lien attaches to money and
  property outcomes. It does not attach to client papers, correspondence, or the file itself.
- Rule 1.8(i) confirms this directly: a charging lien may be acquired and asserted "only against
  causes of action or counterclaims in litigation" and only to the extent MCA 37-61-420(2)
  allows, and it states expressly that "such a charging lien does not extend to other client
  property, papers or materials in the lawyer's possession."
- Rule 1.8(i)(3) separately allows a narrower retaining lien: a lawyer may not assert a
  retaining lien against any client papers or materials except those related to the specific
  matter for which payment has not been made. Even then, at the end of the representation the
  lawyer must deliver to the client any papers or materials "reasonably necessary to protect the
  client's interest in the matter," as required by Rule 1.16(d).
- In plain terms: your lawyer cannot use unpaid fees on one matter to hold your file on an
  unrelated matter, cannot claim your settlement check or judgment funds beyond the statute's
  terms, and cannot withhold from you, at the end of a matter, whatever part of your file you
  reasonably need going forward, even if you owe money on that same matter.

No separate statutory contingent fee percentage cap or sliding scale outside the MRPC was
found; this is listed in the gap list below rather than asserted as "none exists." No free
itemized-accounting rule comparable to a state that has one, beyond the general duties in Rule
1.4 and Rule 1.15, was found in the rules read; this is also listed in the gap list.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

Montana runs one statewide fee arbitration program, the State Bar of Montana's Rules on
Arbitration of Fee Disputes, most recently revised by Montana Supreme Court order in AF 08-0323,
effective May 1, 2009. The rules, containing the operative text in full, are published at
https://courts.mt.gov/external/rules/arb_fee_disp.pdf. The State Bar's public guidance on
disputing a fee is at
https://www.montanabar.org/For-the-Public/Disputing-Attorney-Fees.

- Who can start it, and the amount: either the client or the lawyer can initiate arbitration.
  Any disputed amount of $500 or more may be arbitrated; if only part of a bill is disputed,
  that disputed portion must be at least $500. No dollar ceiling on the amount the program will
  hear was found in the rules text.
- The asymmetry that matters most: under Rule 5.5, if the lawyer is the party responding to a
  client's arbitration request and does not sign on, the arbitration proceeds without the
  lawyer's participation. Rule 8.5 then supplies the deadline: absent an arbitration agreement
  signed by both parties, the decision becomes binding on a non-participating attorney thirty
  days after it is mailed to the parties, unless the lawyer files a lawsuit on the fee dispute.
  Under Rule 5.6, if the client is the party responding to a lawyer's arbitration request and
  does not sign on, the State Bar simply notifies the lawyer and the matter is closed for
  arbitration purposes; Rule 8.5's thirty-day binding mechanism does not apply when it is the
  client who does not participate. In practice, a client who files can force a binding result
  even over the lawyer's objection; a lawyer who files cannot force a reluctant client into it.
- Binding effect where both sides sign on: full binding arbitration under the agreement's
  Option (a). No fee to file was found described in the rules text.
- Deadline: the rules read for this pack did not state a fee-arbitration-specific filing
  deadline separate from the general contract statute of limitations described in section 7.
  That absence is noted here rather than assumed to mean no deadline exists; if you are close to
  the contract limitation period, do not wait to find out.
- Panel and hearing: a three-member arbitration board hears the dispute, convened in the
  judicial district from which the board is chosen, generally within ninety days after the
  board is selected.
- Challenging an award: it depends on how the arbitration became binding. Where both sides
  signed on to binding arbitration up front, Rule 10.2 says the award may only be challenged
  under the Uniform Arbitration Act, and it says plainly that nothing in the rule lets those
  parties bring a lawsuit in court instead. Where the award became binding on a
  non-participating attorney under Rule 8.5, the escape hatch is filing a lawsuit on the fee
  dispute within the thirty days. There is no separate internal appeal inside the Bar.
- Enforcement, and this is the strongest teeth in any state pack in this kit: Rule 10.1 says
  that if sixty days pass from the mailing of the board's decision and the attorney has neither
  filed a lawsuit under Rule 8.5 nor complied with the decision, the Montana Supreme Court shall
  issue an order to show cause why the attorney should not be suspended from the practice of
  law, and shall suspend the attorney if good cause is not shown. Rule 10.3 lets a suspended
  attorney get that lifted by proving compliance. A Montana fee arbitration award is not a
  suggestion.

## 4. Bar complaint process

The Office of Disciplinary Counsel for the State of Montana (ODC) investigates complaints
against Montana lawyers, operating under the direct supervision of the Montana Supreme Court.
The Commission on Practice (COP), nine lawyers and five non-lawyers appointed by the Court,
hears and decides complaints and can recommend discipline to the Court. ODC's own filing
instructions are at https://www.montanaodc.org (Filing a Grievance page). The Montana judiciary
describes the ODC/COP structure at https://courts.mt.gov (Attorney Complaints page).

- To file: a grievance must be submitted in writing, with a signed Grievance Cover Sheet and a
  detailed written explanation, mailed to the Office of Disciplinary Counsel. ODC states it
  will investigate whether the facts presented show a violation of the Montana Rules of
  Professional Conduct.
- No separate informal, consumer-assistance intake track apart from the written grievance
  process was found on ODC's own page. ODC's page states directly that "this office does not
  pursue your legal remedies" and cannot give legal advice; it directs a person with legal
  questions to a private attorney or the Bar's lawyer referral service.
- Filing deadline: ODC's grievance page does not state a filing deadline. The Rules for Lawyer
  Disciplinary Enforcement (RLDE), Rule 11(A), set a general time limit: except as otherwise
  provided, a formal complaint must be filed within six years from the date of the alleged
  misconduct, or from when it was discovered or should have been discovered with due diligence.
  Rule 22(G) states that, except as otherwise provided, time limits under the RLDE are "directory
  and not jurisdictional," meaning a late filing is not automatically barred. RLDE full text (the
  2021 rules adopted under AF 06-0628), published by the Office of Disciplinary Counsel:
  https://img1.wsimg.com/blobby/go/0dfa798d-443a-4159-b66e-3a404e41e7a3/downloads/RLDE%202021%20(with%20Order).pdf.
  The Montana Supreme Court issued further orders in AF 06-0628 in July 2026; those later orders
  were not available as searchable text when this pack was written, so confirm current RLDE rule
  numbers before relying on one.
- What the process produces: discipline against the lawyer, ranging from a private admonition
  to disbarment, decided by the Commission on Practice or the Court. A grievance is not itself a
  lawsuit and does not by itself return money to you.
- Restitution: RLDE lists "requirement of restitution to persons financially injured" as one of
  the forms of discipline or condition an Adjudicatory Panel of the Commission on Practice can
  impose. This is a real, rule-based mechanism, not a routine outcome; it is imposed case by
  case as part of a disciplinary sanction, not awarded automatically to every complainant.

## 5. Client protection fund

Montana's fund is the Lawyers' Fund for Client Protection (LFCP), administered by the Trustees
of the State Bar of Montana. The Bar's program page, with the current LFCP Rules and
application form, is at
https://www.montanabar.org/For-the-Public/Client-Protection-Fund.

- What it covers: losses caused by the "Dishonest Conduct" of a Montana-licensed lawyer,
  meaning mishandling or embezzling client funds within an attorney-client relationship. An
  application may be filed once the accused lawyer has died, been judged mentally incompetent,
  been convicted of a crime arising from the dishonest conduct, relinquished practice, or been
  disbarred or suspended, and the claimant has made a reasonable attempt to recover the funds
  from the lawyer first.
- Exclusions stated on the Bar's page: losses from the lawyer's negligence or malpractice are
  not covered, and losses from a personal business relationship between lawyer and client
  outside the attorney-client relationship are not covered. Those situations belong in a
  disciplinary complaint or a malpractice suit instead.
- Cap: no per-claim or aggregate dollar cap was found, either in the LFCP Rules or on the Bar's
  program page. The rules instead describe a discretionary process: the Board considers the
  overall amount in the Fund at the time of the claim, and may make a partial award rather than
  full reimbursement so the Fund is not exhausted by one large claim. This pack did not find a
  published maximum of any kind, in the rule text or stated administratively, and does not
  assert one.
- Deadline: the LFCP Rules set one. An application must be filed no later than three years after
  the applicant knew, or through reasonable diligence should have known, of the lawyer's
  dishonest conduct. No requirement that a disciplinary complaint be filed first was found in the
  materials read; that absence is listed in the gap list below.
- Awards are discretionary. The Board can deny reimbursement if it finds the lawyer earned the
  fees or the claim is really a malpractice claim rather than theft, and issues a written
  explanation in that case.

## 6. Small claims or equivalent court

Montana's small claims process runs inside the justice's court, under Title 25, chapter 35 of
the Montana Code Annotated (MCA).

- Jurisdictional limit: claims for money or specific personal property up to $7,000, exclusive
  of costs, where the defendant can be served within the county where the action is filed. MCA
  25-35-502, at https://leg.mt.gov/bills/mca/title_0250/chapter_0350/part_0050/section_0020/.
- No county-by-county variation in that limit was found; it is set by the statewide statute.
- Attorneys: Montana's rule is unusual. Under MCA 25-35-505(2), a party may not be represented
  by an attorney in small claims court unless all parties are represented by an attorney. In
  other words, one side cannot bring a lawyer unless the other side also has one; it is an
  all-or-nothing rule, not a one-sided ban. Individuals represent themselves; organizations may
  be represented by a member or employee as described in the same statute.
- Appeal: either party dissatisfied with the judgment may appeal to the district court of the
  county where judgment was rendered. The appeal is commenced by written notice to the small
  claims court and service of that notice on the other party within 10 days after entry of
  judgment. MCA 25-35-803(1).
- Trial de novo: no. MCA 25-35-803(2) states directly that "there may not be a trial de novo in
  the district court," and the appeal is limited to questions of law.
- True cost of the appeal: the Montana district courts' official civil fee schedule lists a
  dedicated line item, "Appeal from Lower Court - Small Claims," at $10, distinct from the
  general non-small-claims lower-court appeal fee of $30 and the general district court civil
  filing fees. Montana District Court Fee Schedule-Civil, updated May 2024, retrieved from
  https://courts.mt.gov. No appeal bond requirement was found in the small claims appeal
  statutes read.
- A separate figure, the general per-defendant "appearance" fee, is listed at $60 in the current
  MCA text (MCA 25-1-201(1)(b)) but at $70 in the same district court fee schedule PDF (updated
  May 2024). This pack could not confirm whether that general appearance fee applies on top of
  the $10 small claims appeal line item, so it is not included in the true-cost figure above;
  see the gap list.
- No small claims rule specific to suing a lawyer was found; the general attorney-appearance
  rule above applies the same way it would to any other defendant.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

- Written contract, or any obligation founded on an instrument in writing: 6 years. MCA
  27-2-202(1). A written fee agreement dispute is usually governed by this period.
- Oral contract, account, or promise not founded on a writing: 5 years. MCA 27-2-202(2).
- An obligation or liability other than a contract, account, or promise, not founded on a
  writing: 3 years. MCA 27-2-202(3).
- Legal malpractice: Montana has a dedicated statute, separate from the general contract
  periods above. MCA 27-2-206 sets a 3-year period, running from when the client discovers, or
  through reasonable diligence should have discovered, the act, error, or omission, whichever is
  later. The same statute sets an outside limit: the action may not be commenced more than 10
  years from the date of the act, error, or omission itself, regardless of when it was
  discovered. Retrieved from https://leg.mt.gov/bills/mca/title_0270/chapter_0020/part_0020/
  section_0060/.
- This pack did not find, in the statute text read, a separate statement of whether a Montana
  malpractice claim sounds in tort, contract, or either at the plaintiff's election; MCA
  27-2-206 applies by its own terms to "an action" against the lawyer for the described conduct,
  without distinguishing the theory pled. That distinction is listed in the gap list below
  rather than asserted.

This section exists so you know a clock is running and can get your own counsel in time. This
kit does not cover how to bring a malpractice claim.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

The Montana Rules of Professional Conduct fold Scope-like language into the numbered Preamble
rather than using a separate "Scope" heading. Preamble paragraph (21) states, consistent with
the ABA model, that "violation of a Rule should not itself give rise to a cause of action
against a lawyer nor should it create any presumption in such a case that a legal duty has been
breached." Montana's paragraph (21) goes further than the ABA model text on this point: it adds
that the Rules "are not designed to be a basis for civil liability," that their purpose "can be
subverted when they are invoked by opposing parties as procedural weapons," and that a lawyer's
own compliance or discipline record under the Rules "does not imply that an opposing party or
lawyer has standing to seek enforcement of the Rules in a collateral proceeding or transaction
outside of the disciplinary process." Unlike the rules in some other states, the Montana text
read for this pack does not contain a sentence saying a rule violation may be used as evidence
of a breach of the standard of care in a malpractice case. Source: MRPC Preamble, paragraph
(21), in the full rules at https://courts.mt.gov/external/rules/mtr_prof_cond.pdf. This text is
part of the professional conduct rules themselves, not any judicial conduct code.

This pack did not independently verify, through Montana case law, whether or how Montana courts
allow a Rule violation to be used as evidence of the standard of care in a malpractice case
despite this no-standing language; that is a malpractice-strategy question outside this kit's
scope, and is listed in the gap list below rather than asserted.

Your actual money routes in Montana are: a contract claim on the fee agreement (section 7), the
fee arbitration program (section 3), and the Lawyers' Fund for Client Protection for dishonest
conduct such as theft or embezzlement of your money (section 5). Discipline, including the
restitution mechanism noted in section 4, is a separate track run by ODC and the Commission on
Practice, not a lawsuit you control.
