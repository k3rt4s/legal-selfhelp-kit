# California

This pack is for a person in California who has a fee, billing, communication, or file-return
dispute with their own lawyer. It does not cover legal malpractice as a claim to run yourself.
Retrieved 2026-08-20.

What a California reader most needs to know, that a reader in another state does not:

- California does not use the model rule's "unreasonable fee" test. Its rule bans an
  "unconscionable or illegal" fee, a different and separately defined standard, and lists
  thirteen factors for it, not eight.
- California runs one statewide fee arbitration program, the Mandatory Fee Arbitration Act
  (MFAA). It is mandatory on the lawyer if the client requests it, and the lawyer must notify
  the client of this right before suing for fees. Skipping that notice is grounds to dismiss
  the lawyer's own fee lawsuit.
- The Client Security Fund states in its own rule that an ordinary failure to refund a fee, or
  a dispute over the size of a bill, is outside its coverage. Only a fee taken for work the
  lawyer did none or almost none of qualifies, and even then the lawyer must already have been
  disciplined, resigned, or died before the Fund will pay.
- Small claims court has an unusual split limit: $12,500 for an individual plaintiff but only
  $6,250 for a business, and it can directly confirm or vacate a fee arbitration award, or hold
  a trial after arbitration, when no more than $6,250 is at stake.
- There is no deadline for a client to submit a State Bar complaint, but the State Bar's own
  prosecutors must file formal charges within five years of the conduct in most cases, so
  reporting years later can still fail even though the complaint form has no printed deadline.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct, numbering and adoption

California adopts the Rules of Professional Conduct through the Board of Trustees of the State
Bar of California, subject to approval by the Supreme Court of California, under Business and
Professions Code sections 6076 and 6077. Cite them as "California Rules of Professional
Conduct, rule 1.5" or "CRPC rule 1.5." The State Bar's own text uses a lowercase "rule."

The current rules took effect November 1, 2018, replacing an older numbering scheme sometimes
still called the "1992 Rules," where, for example, today's rule 1.5 was rule 4-200. If you are
looking at an older letter or filing that cites a rule number like "4-200," it is citing the
pre-2018 rules, not a typo. The official publication point is the State Bar of California, at
<https://www.calbar.ca.gov/legal-professionals/rules/rules-of-professional-conduct>, currently
distributing the "2026 Current Rules" edition of the rules PDF, retrieved 2026-08-20.

Numbering tracks the model rules for rule 1.4 (communication) and rule 1.5 (fees). It does not
for rule 1.16: the discharge and withdrawal duties that sit at 1.16(d) in most other states, and
in the model rules, sit at rule 1.16(e) in California. A reader searching this pack's own
citations for "1.16(d)" in the California rule itself will not find it there.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

California's biggest deviation is the standard itself. Rule 1.5(a) does not ask whether a fee
is unreasonable. It bans an agreement for, or the charging or collecting of, an "unconscionable
or illegal" fee. Unconscionability is judged on the facts existing when the fee agreement was
made, and rule 1.5(b) lists thirteen factors, not exclusive, for it:

1. Whether the lawyer engaged in fraud or overreaching in negotiating or setting the fee.
2. Whether the lawyer failed to disclose material facts.
3. The amount of the fee in proportion to the value of the services performed.
4. The relative sophistication of the lawyer and the client.
5. The novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, and the skill required.
6. The likelihood, if apparent to the client, that taking the case will preclude other
   employment by the lawyer.
7. The amount involved and the results obtained.
8. Time limitations imposed by the client or the circumstances.
9. The nature and length of the professional relationship with the client.
10. The experience, reputation, and ability of the lawyer performing the services.
11. Whether the fee is fixed or contingent.
12. The time and labor required.
13. Whether the client gave informed consent to the fee.

Factors 4 (relative sophistication of lawyer and client) and 13 (the client's informed consent
to the fee itself) are not in the ABA's eight-factor list. Neither is the direct focus in
factors 1 and 2 on fraud, overreaching, and undisclosed material facts, framed here as
questions about the fee itself rather than about the lawyer's general conduct.

Rule 1.5(c) directly prohibits two kinds of contingent fee: one in a family law matter where
payment or amount depends on securing a dissolution, or on the amount of spousal or child
support or a property settlement, and one for representing a defendant in a criminal case.

Rule 1.5(d) covers a fee labeled "non-refundable" or "earned on receipt." Such a label only
holds up if the fee is a true retainer, meaning it is paid solely to secure the lawyer's
availability rather than as payment for work, and the client agreed in writing after the lawyer
disclosed this. A fee called "non-refundable" that does not meet both conditions can still be
disputed as if it were an ordinary advance fee, refundable to the extent unearned.

Two statutes add California-specific writing requirements the model rules do not state as
numbered rules. Business and Professions Code section 6148 requires a written fee agreement
whenever it is reasonably foreseeable that total expense, including fees, will exceed $1,000,
with required contents including the basis of compensation and the scope of services. Section
6147 requires a written agreement for any contingency fee, regardless of amount, with its own
required contents including the contingency rate and how costs affect the client's recovery.
Under both statutes, failing to meet the requirement makes the agreement voidable at the
client's option, and the lawyer is then limited to collecting a reasonable fee rather than the
agreed one.

California also imposes a statutory sliding-scale cap the model rules do not have at all:
Business and Professions Code section 6146 caps a contingency fee against a health care
provider for professional negligence at 25 percent of the amount recovered if the case settles
before a complaint or arbitration demand is filed, and 33 percent if it settles, is arbitrated,
or goes to judgment after that filing, with a court able to approve a higher fee only on a
motion showing good cause.

Rule 1.5.1 adds a further fee-related rule not paralleled by a single numbered model rule: two
lawyers from different firms who divide a fee must have a written agreement between themselves,
the client's written consent to the division after disclosure of who is involved and the
division's terms, and the total fee charged to the client cannot increase solely because of the
division.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

California runs one statewide program, the Mandatory Fee Arbitration Act (MFAA), at Business
and Professions Code sections 6200 through 6206. The State Bar's Board of Trustees establishes
and administers the system and approves the rules under which local county bar associations run
their own arbitration programs; the State Bar itself arbitrates directly only where no local
bar program exists. Find your county's program through the State Bar's Mandatory Fee
Arbitration Program page, <https://www.calbar.ca.gov/legal-professionals/ethics-compliance-practice-resources/mandatory-fee-arbitration-program-resources>.

Participation is mandatory on the lawyer if the client commences it, and voluntary for the
client, unless the client agreed in writing in advance to be bound (section 6200(c)). This
asymmetry is the single most useful fact in this section: a lawyer cannot refuse to arbitrate a
fee dispute the client brings.

The award becomes binding automatically 30 days after service of notice of the award, unless a
party requests a trial after arbitration within that 30 days (section 6203(b)). Either party can
make that request. The one exception: a party who willfully failed to appear at the arbitration
hearing loses the right to a trial after arbitration, and that absent party carries the burden of
showing the failure to appear was not willful (section 6204(a)). Separately, a party who did not
appear at the hearing at all cannot recover attorney's fees or costs even if a later court
proceeding is what confirms, corrects, or vacates the award (section 6203(c)); that fee-shifting
rule is not the same question as whether the party can seek a trial after arbitration in the
first place.

The statute itself states no dollar ceiling or floor on what the program will arbitrate.
Separately, Code of Civil Procedure section 116.220(a)(4) lets small claims court confirm,
correct, or vacate a binding MFA award, or hold the trial after nonbinding arbitration, only
when $6,250 or less is in controversy; above that figure the same kind of court proceeding
belongs in the regular civil department instead (see Section 6).

The statute does not set an independent filing window for a client who wants to start
arbitration before the lawyer sues. It does say arbitration cannot be commenced if a civil
action for the same relief would already be time-barred (section 6206), so the practical
deadline is the underlying claim's own statute of limitations, ordinarily the four-year
contract period discussed in Section 7. Filing for arbitration then tolls that same statute
while the arbitration is pending. If the lawyer sues or serves notice of the right to arbitrate
first, the client must request arbitration before filing an answer or within 30 days of the
lawyer's notice, or the right is waived (section 6201).

The statute lets the Board of Trustees set a filing fee by rule and allows arbitrators to
allocate it between the parties (sections 6200(a), 6203(a)), but does not state a dollar amount.
No current fee schedule was located; see the gap list in [verification_ca.md](verification_ca.md).

The lawyer must give the client written notice of the right to arbitrate, on the State Bar's
prescribed form, before or when serving a summons or claim to sue the client for fees (section
6201(a)). Failure to give this notice is expressly a ground to dismiss the lawyer's own action.
If your lawyer sues you for unpaid fees and you never received that notice, this is worth
raising immediately, ideally with your own counsel.

## 4. Bar complaint process

The Office of Chief Trial Counsel, part of the State Bar of California, handles attorney
misconduct complaints. File online or by mail to the Intake Unit; there is no filing fee. The
contact center is 800-843-9053 within California, 213-765-1200 from outside it. Details at
<https://www.calbar.ca.gov/public/file-complaints-claims/file-attorney-complaint/how-file-complaint-against-attorney>.

California's own complaint page separates a fee dispute from a misconduct complaint and directs
a billing disagreement toward the Mandatory Fee Arbitration Program in Section 3 rather than
treating it as the primary discipline track. If the underlying problem looks like dishonesty,
rather than a disagreement about the size of a bill, a misconduct complaint is still the right
tool alongside, or instead of, fee arbitration.

No deadline governs when a client can submit a complaint. A separate rule constrains the State
Bar's own prosecutors instead: Rule of Procedure 5.21 requires the Office of Chief Trial Counsel
to file the formal Notice of Disciplinary Charges within the later of five years from the
violation or two years from when the first complaint about it was submitted, so long as that
complaint came in within the five years. Several things toll this period, including the
attorney's continued representation of the complainant, the attorney's concealment of the
facts, and a pending related civil or criminal proceeding. In practice, a complaint filed many
years after the conduct risks being outside the window in which formal charges can still be
filed, even though the complaint form itself states no deadline.

Discipline is not a refund. Outcomes range from a warning through disbarment. The one exception
is narrow: under section 6203(d), if a binding MFA award or judgment orders a refund and the
lawyer does not pay it, the State Bar can place the lawyer on involuntary inactive status until
the refund is paid, plus administrative penalties of up to 20 percent of the amount owed or
$1,000, whichever is greater. This enforces an arbitration award or judgment already obtained;
it does not turn an ordinary misconduct complaint into a payment order on its own.

## 5. Client protection fund

The Client Security Fund, administered by a Commission the Board of Trustees appoints under
Business and Professions Code section 6140.5, reimburses certain losses caused by a
California lawyer's dishonest conduct. Details, current rules, and the application are at
<https://www.calbar.ca.gov/public/file-complaints-claims/apply-reimbursement>.

What it covers, defined by the Fund's own Rule 3.431: theft, embezzlement, or wrongful
conversion of money or property; failure to refund an advance fee where the lawyer performed an
insignificant portion of the promised services or none at all, treated as a wrongful taking;
borrowing from a client with no intention or ability to repay; taking money for an investment
that was never actually made; and other acts of intentional dishonesty or deceit that caused
the loss.

What it excludes: the rule states plainly that "all other instances of an attorney's failure to
return an unearned fee or the disputed portion of a fee are outside the scope of this provision
and not reimbursable." An ordinary disagreement over whether a bill was fair, or whether a
refund is owed, does not qualify on its own. Losses from negligence, malpractice, or
incompetence are also excluded, as are interest, expenses, and other incidental costs.

Before the Fund will pay, Rule 3.432 requires that the lawyer already be disbarred, disciplined,
or voluntarily resigned from the State Bar, or have died, been adjudicated mentally incompetent,
or become a judgment debtor of the applicant in a contested proceeding or been convicted of a
crime because of the dishonest conduct. A CSF claim generally needs the underlying discipline or
legal process to have actually produced a result, not merely a complaint on file.

The cap, under Rule 3.434(A), is $100,000 per claim and per attorney, cumulatively, for a loss
occurring on or after January 1, 2009. For a loss before that date, the cap is $50,000. An
application must be filed no more than four years after the loss was discovered or reasonably
should have been discovered (Rule 3.440(E)). Filing a CSF application does not stop any other
statute of limitations from running on other remedies, by the Fund's own instructions.

Payments are entirely discretionary. The Fund's own rules and instructions describe
reimbursement as "solely within the discretion of the State Bar" and state that no person has a
right to it, so a claim that otherwise qualifies is still not guaranteed to be paid in full.

## 6. Small claims or equivalent court

California's small claims court sits within the superior court and has no separate name.

The jurisdictional limit is $12,500 for a claim by a natural person (Code of Civil Procedure
section 116.221) and $6,250 for most other plaintiffs, including a business (section
116.220(a)(1)). If your dispute is with your own lawyer and you are suing as an individual, the
higher figure applies.

Lawyers generally cannot represent a party in small claims court at all (section 116.530(a)),
with narrow exceptions for representing themselves, an all-lawyer partnership, or an all-lawyer
professional corporation. A lawyer can still advise a party beforehand, testify to facts within
personal knowledge, represent a party on an appeal to superior court, or represent a party in
enforcing a judgment (section 116.530(c)).

The appeal structure is asymmetric. A plaintiff has no right to appeal the judgment on their own
claim; only a defendant, or a plaintiff facing a defendant's countercomplaint, can appeal to the
superior court (section 116.710). That appeal is a full new hearing before a different judicial
officer, not a review of the small claims record (section 116.770). If you sue your lawyer in
small claims court and lose, that judgment is close to final for you specifically as the
original plaintiff.

One rule speaks directly to a lawyer fee dispute: section 116.220(a)(4) gives small claims court
jurisdiction to confirm, correct, or vacate a binding MFA arbitration award, or to hold the
trial after nonbinding arbitration, when the amount in controversy is $6,250 or less (see
Section 3). Above that amount, that same kind of court proceeding goes to the regular civil
department instead.

No statewide flat filing fee figure was located; fees vary with the size of the claim under a
separate statutory schedule. Confirm the current fee with the clerk in the county where you file.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A fee agreement dispute, such as a lawyer's failure to refund an unearned fee under a written
agreement, is generally a contract claim. California's period for an action on a contract
founded on a written instrument is four years, at Code of Civil Procedure section 337(a).

Legal malpractice needs its own counsel rather than self-help. It is included here only so you
know a clock exists and can get counsel in time. Code of Civil Procedure section 340.6(a) sets
the period at one year after the plaintiff discovers, or through reasonable diligence should
have discovered, the facts constituting the wrongful act or omission, or four years from the
date of the wrongful act or omission itself, whichever occurs first. Several things toll this
period, including that the plaintiff has not yet sustained actual injury, that the attorney
continues to represent the plaintiff on the same subject matter, that the attorney willfully
concealed the facts (which tolls only the four-year figure), that the plaintiff is under a
legal or physical disability, and, specific to this kit's subject, that a fee dispute between
the lawyer and client is pending resolution under the Mandatory Fee Arbitration Act described
in Section 3. Filing for fee arbitration therefore does double duty: it pursues the fee dispute
and pauses the malpractice clock while it is pending.

Whether section 340.6 applies regardless of whether a malpractice claim is framed in tort or in
contract was not independently confirmed against case law; the statute's own text covers "a
wrongful act or omission... arising in the performance of professional services" without
distinguishing by legal theory, but this pack does not confirm how California courts have
applied that language across theories. See the gap list in [verification_ca.md](verification_ca.md).

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

Rule 1.0(b)(3) states plainly that a violation of a rule "does not itself give rise to a cause
of action for damages caused by failure to comply with the rule," and that nothing in the rules
or their comments is meant to enlarge or restrict the existing law on a lawyer's liability to
others. There is no private right of action for a bare rules violation in California.

Comment 1 to rule 1.0 cites *Stanley v. Richmond* (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 1070, 1097, for the
same point, and then adds the useful qualifier: a rule violation "may be evidence of breach of
a lawyer's fiduciary or other substantive legal duty in a non-disciplinary context." The same
comment cites two examples of a rule mattering outside discipline: *Fletcher v. Davis* (2004)
33 Cal.4th 61, on enforcing an attorney's lien, and *Chambers v. Kay* (2002) 29 Cal.4th 142, on
enforcing a fee-sharing agreement.

Your actual money route runs through the rest of this pack: a contract claim in small claims or
another court (Section 6), the statewide Mandatory Fee Arbitration Act (Section 3), or the
Client Security Fund where the lawyer's conduct was dishonest rather than merely a disputed bill
(Section 5). A rules violation can support a bar complaint (Section 4) and can be evidence in
one of these other proceedings, but it is not, by itself, a claim for damages.
