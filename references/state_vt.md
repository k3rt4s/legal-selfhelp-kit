# Vermont

This pack is for someone in Vermont who has a dispute with their own lawyer over
fees, billing, communication, getting their file back, or an unearned advance. It
does not cover suing a lawyer for malpractice. Retrieved August 20, 2026.

What is different about Vermont:

- Vermont's Rules of Professional Conduct (V.R.P.C.) are a separate body of rules
  from Administrative Order 9 (A.O. 9), which governs the disciplinary process. Both
  are Vermont Supreme Court rules, but you need A.O. 9 to file or track a complaint.
- Vermont added its own nonrefundable-fee rule to Rule 1.5 in 2016. It is not in the
  ABA model rule. It sets specific disclosure conditions before a lawyer can call any
  part of a fee nonrefundable.
- The Vermont Bar Association's Fee Dispute Committee is voluntary for both sides.
  Your lawyer cannot be forced into it, and you cannot be forced into it either. If
  your lawyer will not agree to participate, this path is closed.
- Vermont's small claims appeal is decided on the existing case record, not a new
  trial. A different judge reviews what already happened; you do not get to present
  new evidence.
- The Client Security Fund only covers theft-like misconduct (the rule's own term is
  "Dishonest Conduct," defined as misappropriation, embezzlement, defalcation, or
  conversion). An ordinary billing dispute or a slow refund is not covered by itself.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct, numbering and adoption

The Vermont Rules of Professional Conduct are adopted by the Vermont Supreme Court
and cite the same way the ABA model rules do: "V.R.P.C. 1.5," or in plain writing,
"Vermont Rule of Professional Conduct 1.5." The numbering follows the ABA model
numbering, so Rule 1.4, 1.5, 1.15, and 1.16 are in the expected places. The rules took
effect September 1, 1999, and have been amended a number of times since, most
recently for Rule 1.5 in 2016 (the nonrefundable-fee provision) and in later years for
other rules; the official compiled text is published by the Vermont Judiciary at
<https://www.vtcourts.gov/sites/default/files/documents/VermontRulesofProfessionalConduct.pdf>
(the Judiciary's public-facing domain moved from vermontjudiciary.org to vtcourts.gov
during 2026; most old links redirect, but this specific dated filename did not, so the
current filename is cited directly here).
The Judiciary's promulgated-rules page, which lists every stamped amendment order as
it is adopted, is at <https://www.vermontjudiciary.org/attorneys/rules/promulgated>.

Discipline procedure is not in the V.R.P.C. It is in a separate Vermont Supreme Court
rule, Administrative Order 9, which creates the Professional Responsibility Program
(the Professional Responsibility Board and its disciplinary counsel). A reader who
only reads the V.R.P.C. will not find the complaint process; A.O. 9 is what governs
filing a complaint, screening, hearing panels, and sanctions.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

Vermont's Rule 1.5(a) reasonableness factors are the same eight ABA model factors,
word for word, with no Vermont-specific addition or subtraction. This was checked
directly against the current rule text. So the biggest deviation in this section is
not in the factors themselves.

The most valuable Vermont-specific rule is Rule 1.5(f) and (g), added in 2016 (V.R.P.C.
1.5, eff. May 9, 2016), which the ABA model rule does not have at all. It sets
conditions a lawyer must meet before charging any fee as "nonrefundable" or "earned on
receipt":

- The lawyer must confirm to the client in writing, before or shortly after starting
  the work, that the fee will not be refunded, and describe what the client gets for
  it.
- A lawyer cannot make a client sign away the right to challenge whether a
  nonrefundable fee was reasonable, except to settle an existing dispute, and only if
  the client is separately represented or was advised in writing to get independent
  counsel first.
- A fee that does not meet these conditions is not nonrefundable. It is an ordinary
  advance, and Rule 1.5(g) requires it to be held in the lawyer's trust account under
  Rule 1.15(c) until the lawyer actually earns it.

This matters directly for a billing dispute: if your fee agreement calls part of your
payment "nonrefundable" or "earned on receipt" but the lawyer never gave you the
written disclosure Rule 1.5(f) requires, that label may not hold up, and the money may
have had to sit in trust until earned.

On writing requirements generally, Rule 1.5(b) only says the fee basis should
"preferably" be in writing, the same as the model rule; Vermont does not require a
signed written fee agreement for ordinary hourly or flat-fee work, only for contingent
fees (Rule 1.5(c), which must be signed by the client) and for the nonrefundable-fee
arrangement above.

Rule 1.16(d) requires a lawyer, on ending the representation, to surrender "papers and
property to which the client is entitled" and refund any advance payment not earned.
It also says "the lawyer may retain papers relating to the client to the extent
permitted by other law." Vermont's "other law" here is common-law attorney's lien,
addressed in three Vermont Bar Association ethics advisory opinions rather than in a
court rule: Opinion 82-09, Opinion 91-03, and Opinion 99-07. Opinion 82-09 in
particular discusses Vermont case law on a lawyer's retaining lien. Under this line of
authority, a Vermont lawyer may hold a client's file to secure unpaid fees, but only
where doing so will not prejudice the client's ability to pursue an active legal
matter; a closed file with no pending deadlines can be withheld more freely than an
active file the client needs to move forward. Opinions 82-09 and 91-03 predate the
V.R.P.C. (they were decided under the old Code of Professional Responsibility), but
Opinion 99-07 expressly reconsiders the question under the V.R.P.C. and says its
analysis and result does not change. These opinions are advisory, not binding
rules, and are published by the Vermont Bar Association at
<https://www.vtbar.org/advisory_ethics/liens-attorney/> (an index page; the individual
opinions were retrieved as PDFs linked from that index).

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

Vermont runs one statewide program: the Fee Dispute Committee of the Vermont Bar
Association (the VBA), formerly called the Fee Arbitration Committee. It offers both
mediation and arbitration, both free, run out of the VBA's Montpelier office. Details
and the intake form are at
<https://www.vtbar.org/for-the-public/fee-dispute-information/>.

Participation is voluntary on both sides. The Committee Chair contacts both parties
after a request is filed and confirms that both agree to take part; if the lawyer will
not agree, the Committee does not proceed. This means, unlike some states where a
lawyer must submit to arbitration at the client's request, a Vermont client cannot
force their lawyer into this program.

If both sides agree, they choose mediation, arbitration, or both. Mediation is
expressly non-binding. Arbitration becomes binding only once both parties sign a
written arbitration agreement; the template agreement retrieved from the VBA states
that the parties "agree to submit their aforesaid fee dispute to binding arbitration"
and that the Vermont Arbitration Act, 12 V.S.A. section 5651 and following, governs
the proceeding. A signed award can only be challenged under that Act's own procedure,
which requires a motion to vacate within 30 days of the award (12 V.S.A. section
5677(c)). The arbitration panel decides only the fee amount; the info sheet states
plainly it "cannot and will not make decisions as to the behavior or ethics of an
attorney." A decision is due within 30 days of the hearing.

No published dollar floor or ceiling on what the Committee will hear was found in the
program's own information sheet or FAQ. No filing deadline specific to the Committee
itself (as opposed to the Arbitration Act's 30-day post-award window) was found
either; both are recorded as gaps below rather than stated as facts. Nothing found
requires a lawyer to notify a client of a right to arbitrate before suing for fees in
Vermont.

## 4. Bar complaint process

Discipline is handled by the Professional Responsibility Program, created by
Administrative Order 9, not by the Vermont Bar Association (a private membership
association that runs the fee dispute and Client Security Fund programs described
above, separately). A complaint against a lawyer is filed with the Office of
Disciplinary Counsel. The Judiciary's own complaint page, with the intake process and
a link to the complaint form, is at
<https://www.vermontjudiciary.org/about-vermont-judiciary/filing-complaint>.

A.O. 9 sets out screening, investigation, and a hearing panel process. Under A.O. 9,
Rule 8 ("Sanctions"), the available sanctions include disbarment, suspension,
immediate interim suspension, public reprimand, admonition, and probation. Rule
8.A.(7) also authorizes "reimbursement of retainers, fees, trust funds, or other
monies collected or received by the lawyer on a client's behalf," but only alongside
another sanction, never as reimbursement alone. This is a real, rule-based restitution
mechanism, though it can be ordered only once misconduct is found and only in
combination with a separate sanction such as reprimand or suspension; it is not a
guaranteed refund and it does not run on the complainant's timeline. This provision
was confirmed directly in the rule text of an archived, dated compilation of A.O. 9
(current through January 2016); the promulgated-amendments list published since then
identifies specific amended rule numbers (2, 5, 6, 12, 13D, 15A, 16B, 17C, 18, and
27A), none of which is Rule 8, so nothing found indicates this provision has changed,
though the current consolidated text was not independently re-fetched.

No time limit on filing a disciplinary complaint was found in the source reviewed. The
Judiciary's own complaint brochure, retrieved as an official Judiciary publication, is
explicit that discipline is not a substitute for getting your money back: it exists to
regulate lawyers, not to resolve a fee dispute or order a refund as its primary
purpose. A billing disagreement alone, with no dishonesty or ethics violation, is
unlikely to result in discipline and is better routed to the Fee Dispute Committee
(Section 3) or small claims (Section 6).

## 5. Client protection fund

Vermont's fund is the Client Security Fund, administered by the Vermont Bar
Association's Client Security Fund Committee under rules adopted in 1986 and revised
in 2003 and 2022. It reimburses losses caused by a lawyer's "Dishonest Conduct,"
defined narrowly in the Fund's own rules as "a misappropriation, embezzlement,
defalcation, or conversion of money, property, or other thing of value." A general
overpayment dispute, a fee that was simply too high, or a slow refund of an unearned
advance is not, by itself, Dishonest Conduct under this definition unless the lawyer
actually misappropriated the money. The Fund's rules are published by the VBA at
<https://www.vtbar.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/CSF-RULES-Rev-August-3-2022.pdf>.

The per-claimant cap is $75,000, and the aggregate cap for all claims against a single
lawyer arising from the same course of conduct is the lesser of $150,000 or 20 percent
of the Fund's total assets as of the decision date. Both figures are set in the Fund's
own rule text (Rule 12), not in a statute, and both were last revised in the 2003 and
2022 rule revisions, so they are set administratively by the VBA Board of Managers
rather than by the Vermont Supreme Court or the legislature.

Before the Fund will pay a claim, the lawyer must already have been suspended,
disbarred, or resigned during a pending disciplinary case, or have died or been
adjudged incompetent; a disciplinary complaint being merely filed is not enough, it
must have concluded in one of those outcomes. The filing deadline is two years from
whichever is later: when the claimant knew or should have known of the Dishonest
Conduct, or when the claimant exhausted other reasonable ways to recover the money.
Awards are discretionary and can be increased by the Committee in cases of extreme
hardship, but nothing in the rules commits the Fund to paying every approved claim in
full.

## 6. Small claims or equivalent court

Vermont's small claims process runs in the Civil Division of the Superior Court in
each county; there is no separate small claims court. The jurisdictional limit is
$10,000 for most claims, set by statute at 12 V.S.A. section 5531(a); a claim cannot
be split into smaller pieces to get around that limit (12 V.S.A. section 5531(b)). A
narrower $5,000 limit applies specifically to actions to collect consumer credit or
medical debt (12 V.S.A. section 5531(e)); a billing dispute with your own lawyer is
not a debt-collection action against you, so the general $10,000 limit applies. No
county-by-county variation in the dollar limit was found; the limit is set uniformly
by statute.

Lawyers are allowed to represent either party in small claims, though the Judiciary's
own self-help page notes many people represent themselves
(<https://www.vermontjudiciary.org/civil/suing-and-being-sued-small-claims>). The
filing fee is tiered by claim amount under the statewide court fee schedule (32
V.S.A. section 1431(c)(1)): $65 for a claim of $1,000 or less, $90 for a claim between
$1,001 and $10,000. The schedule is published at
<https://www.vermontjudiciary.org/fees>.

Either party may appeal a small claims judgment to a Superior judge (12 V.S.A. section
5538). This is confirmed by the statute itself to be an on-the-record review, not a
trial de novo: "The appeal shall be heard and decided, based on the record made in the
small claims procedure." No new evidence or new trial is available on appeal; a judge
who did not hear the original case reviews the existing record. The appeal must be
filed within 30 days of the judgment, using Form 100-00291, per the Judiciary's small
claims appeals page
(<https://www.vermontjudiciary.org/civil/small-claims-trials-and-appeals>). The appeal
fee is $120, set by 32 V.S.A. section 1431(d) and confirmed independently on the same
small claims appeals page. Both the statute-based fee schedule and the plain-language
appeals page agree on $120 as the fee to file the appeal itself, and no separate or
additional appeal-specific fee was found in either source. There is no appeal as of
right to the Vermont Supreme Court from a small claims appeal decision; a further
appeal there requires the Supreme Court's own permission (12 V.S.A. section 5538).

No rule specific to suing a lawyer in small claims, as distinct from suing anyone
else, was found.

## 7. Limitation periods

Vermont's general civil (contract) limitation period is six years from when the claim
accrues, set by 12 V.S.A. section 511, and this statute does not distinguish written
from oral contracts; both get six years unless another statute sets a shorter period
for a specific type of claim. A dispute over what your fee agreement required, or
whether an advance was earned, is ordinarily a contract claim and would fall under
this six-year period.

Vermont does not have a statute labeled specifically "legal malpractice." The general
personal-injury and negligence period is three years under 12 V.S.A. section 512(4),
which for "injuries to the person suffered by the act or default of another" includes
its own built-in discovery rule in the statute's own text: the claim "accrue[s] as of
the date of the discovery of the injury." Vermont does have a separate, explicit
medical malpractice statute (12 V.S.A. section 521, three years from the incident or
two years from discovery, whichever is later, with an outer seven-year repose period),
which shows the legislature writes a dedicated malpractice statute when it means to;
no equivalent dedicated statute for legal malpractice was found in this chapter. This
pack did not locate and confirm, from an official source, a specific court decision
stating whether a Vermont legal malpractice claim must be brought as a tort claim
under section 512, as a contract claim under section 511, or either at the client's
election; that question is recorded as a gap below rather than answered here. No
statute of repose specific to legal malpractice was found; the medical malpractice
statute of repose (7 years, section 521) does not apply to attorneys.

Whichever period applies, a legal malpractice claim is a separate, more serious claim
than a fee or billing dispute, and it has its own clock. If anything about your
lawyer's conduct raises malpractice, not just a fee disagreement, get another lawyer's
advice promptly rather than relying on this pack, which does not cover malpractice
claims.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

Vermont's Rules of Professional Conduct include a Scope note, numbered paragraph [20],
that directly addresses this question: "Whether violation of a rule gives rise to a
cause of action or creates a presumption that a legal duty has been breached is a
question of substantive law beyond the scope of the rules... They are not designed to
be a basis for civil liability... nothing in the rules should be deemed to augment or
diminish any substantive legal duty of lawyers." This is the same substantive language
used in the ABA model rules' own scope note; Vermont did not depart from it. In plain
terms, a violation of the V.R.P.C. by itself does not give a client a lawsuit. It also
states, in the same paragraph, that a rule violation being a basis for discipline does
not mean "an antagonist in a collateral proceeding" (which would include a client
suing for money) automatically "has standing to seek enforcement of the rule."

This pack did not locate and confirm an official source stating whether Vermont courts
treat a rules violation as admissible evidence of the standard of care in a
malpractice case; that is recorded as a gap below. Vermont's rules do not create a
private right of action on their own. The money routes covered in this pack are a
contract claim over the fee agreement (Section 7), the Fee Dispute Committee (Section
3), small claims court (Section 6), and, only for theft-like misconduct, the Client
Security Fund (Section 5). Fee forfeiture as a distinct remedy was not researched for
this pack and is not covered here.

(<https://www.vermontjudiciary.org/attorneys/rules>), confirm the Fee Dispute
Committee's current process directly with the Vermont Bar Association
(<https://www.vtbar.org/for-the-public/fee-dispute-information/>) before relying on

own current fee schedule (<https://www.vermontjudiciary.org/fees>) before filing,
