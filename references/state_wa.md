# Washington

This pack is for a person in Washington who has a fee, billing, communication, or file-return
dispute with their own lawyer. It does not cover legal malpractice as a claim to run yourself.
Retrieved 2026-08-20.

What a Washington reader most needs to know, that a reader in another state does not:

- Washington State Bar Association (WSBA) bylaws and its own cost-of-licensing documents
  confirm a Fee Arbitration Program exists, but no live WSBA page describing how to use it,
  whether it is mandatory, its dollar limits, or its deadlines could be found. The WSBA's
  current public guidance instead sends a fee-dispute complainant to Resolution Washington's
  community Dispute Resolution Centers, a general mediation network, not an attorney-specific
  arbitration forum. See Section 3; this changes the realistic path more than anything else in
  this pack.
- RPC 1.5(f) lets a lawyer treat a signed, written retainer or flat fee as the lawyer's own
  property on receipt, outside the trust account, something the model rule does not address at
  all. It also imposes its own duty: if a dispute arises over that retainer or flat fee, the
  lawyer must take reasonable and prompt action to resolve it.
- RPC 1.4(c) requires a lawyer without at least $100,000 per occurrence and $300,000 aggregate
  in malpractice insurance to disclose that fact to the client in writing and get informed
  written consent. Most states do not require this at all.
- The Lawyers' Fund for Client Protection application deadline is a flat 3 years from discovery
  of the loss, and a disciplinary grievance ordinarily has to be on file first. The per-claim
  cap is $150,000 right now. WSBA's own dated program materials say it rises to $250,000 on
  September 1, 2026, but the court's own posted Fund regulations still show $150,000 as of this
  pack's retrieval date. See Section 5. A claim over $5,000 is not paid all at once either way:
  $5,000 comes on approval and the rest waits for the Fund's fiscal year end.
- Washington's small claims department bars lawyers from appearing without the judge's consent,
  for either side, and the claim limit is $10,000 for a natural person but only $5,000 for
  everyone else.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct, numbering and adoption

The Washington Supreme Court adopts Washington's Rules of Professional Conduct (RPC) directly,
as their own standalone court rule set rather than as a numbered rule nested inside another
title. Cite them as "RPC" followed by the rule number, for example "RPC 1.5." Washington does
not renumber the rules a client is most likely to need: RPC 1.4 (communication), RPC 1.5
(fees), RPC 1.15A (safekeeping property), and RPC 1.16 (declining or terminating
representation) sit at the numbers a reader would expect from the model rules.

The official publication point is the Washington Courts website, at
<https://www.courts.wa.gov/court_rules/?fa=court_rules.list&group=ga&set=RPC>, which links each
rule as its own PDF. Washington's RPCs were originally adopted effective September 1, 1985, and
were amended effective September 1, 2006, with individual rules
amended many times since; RPC 1.5 was most recently amended effective April 29, 2025, and RPC
1.4 was amended effective September 1, 2021, the amendment that added the malpractice insurance
disclosure duty described below.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

Washington lists nine reasonableness factors, not the model's eight. The first eight track the
model list. The ninth does not:

9. The terms of the fee agreement between the lawyer and client, including whether the
   agreement or a confirming writing shows that the client received a reasonable and fair
   disclosure of the material elements of the fee agreement and of the lawyer's billing
   practices. RPC 1.5's own comment confirms this is a nine-item, non-exclusive list.

RPC 1.5(b) tracks the model rule on communicating scope and fee basis before or within a
reasonable time after the representation begins, with the same duty to communicate later
changes.

RPC 1.5(f) is Washington's most distinctive fee rule, and it has no model-rule counterpart at
all. Fees paid in advance normally must go into a trust account and be withdrawn only as
earned, but RPC 1.5(f) creates two exceptions:

- A retainer, a fee paid to keep the lawyer available during a period or on a matter, separate
  from payment for services actually performed. Agreed to in a signed writing, it is the
  lawyer's property on receipt and does not go into trust.
- A flat fee for specified services, paid in whole or in part in advance. If the client signs a
  writing meeting RPC 1.5(f)(2)'s disclosure requirements, including that the fee is the
  lawyer's property immediately, that the client can still terminate the relationship, and that
  a partial refund may be owed if the work is not finished, the flat fee is also the lawyer's
  property on receipt rather than trust-account money.

RPC 1.5(f)(3) then imposes its own duty: if a dispute arises over a retainer or flat fee taken
under this exception, the lawyer must take reasonable and prompt action to resolve it. A reader
whose lawyer took an advance fee "up front, nonrefundable" should ask whether that fee met
RPC 1.5(f)'s writing and disclosure requirements; if it did not, the money should have stayed in
trust until earned.

RPC 1.5(c) and (d) track the model rule on contingent fees: a signed writing stating how the
fee and expenses are calculated, a closing statement at the end of the matter, and a bar on
contingent fees in domestic relations matters and in criminal defense.

RPC 1.4(c), added effective September 1, 2021, is a Washington-specific rule with no model
counterpart. A lawyer who does not carry professional liability insurance of at least $100,000
per occurrence and $300,000 in the aggregate for all claims in a policy period must, before or
when starting the representation, notify the client in writing and obtain the client's written
informed consent. A lawyer whose coverage lapses mid-representation has 30 days to get a new
policy or give the same notice, or must withdraw.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

Washington does not have a publicly documented, statewide fee arbitration process a client can
currently use with confidence. What is confirmed and what is not needs to be kept separate.

Confirmed: the WSBA Bylaws list a "Fee Arbitration Program" among the alternative dispute
resolution programs the Bar may administer, at
<https://www.wsba.org/docs/default-source/about-wsba/governance/proposed-bylaw-amendments/current-wsba-bylaws0cc870f2f6d9654cb471ff1f00003f4f.pdf>,
and the WSBA's own 2024 submission on the total cost of practicing law lists "Fee Arbitration"
as a service funded out of member license fees, at
<https://www.wsba.org/docs/default-source/about-wsba/governance/bog-initiatives/total-cost-to-practice-law-aba-2024.pdf>.
Both confirm the program exists in some form.

Not confirmed, despite a thorough search: any live WSBA page describing how a client applies,
whether participation is mandatory on the lawyer once the client requests it, whether an award
binds one or both sides, any dollar ceiling or floor, any filing deadline, any filing fee, or
whether a lawyer must notify a client of arbitration rights before suing for fees. The search
covered the WSBA's full site map (over 6,700 URLs, none matching a current fee-arbitration
page), direct guesses at plausible URLs (each returned a 404), the WSBA's own site search (which
returns no results for "fee arbitration" and appears to require client-side rendering this
retrieval could not execute), and the Wayback Machine's index of wsba.org, which holds only
snapshots of a "fee-arbitration.htm" page from 2000 through 2003, too old to describe the
program as it runs today.

What the WSBA's current, dated public materials say instead: its Grievance FAQ (Office of
Disciplinary Counsel) states plainly that filing a grievance will not get you a fee refund, and
for a fee dispute it points a reader to Resolution Washington and to the WSBA's Alternative
Dispute Resolution Section directory of private providers. Resolution Washington is a statewide
network of community Dispute Resolution Centers offering general mediation services; it is not
an attorney-specific fee arbitration forum, and nothing on its own site describes attorney fee
disputes as a specialty.

If your county has its own bar association fee dispute committee, that was not surveyed for
this pack and may be a better first stop. Absent a confirmed statewide path, the realistic
route for a genuine billing disagreement is a written demand to the lawyer, informal help from
the WSBA in reaching the lawyer, general mediation through Resolution Washington, and if that
fails, small claims court (Section 6) or another civil action within the limitation period
(Section 7).

## 4. Bar complaint process

The Office of Disciplinary Counsel (ODC), operating under the WSBA, reviews complaints against
lawyers, called grievances, at <https://www.wsba.org/for-the-public/concerns-about-a-lawyer>.
Anyone can file. A grievance must be in writing, limited to 25 pages including attachments, in
English; there is no filing fee and no deadline, a grievance can be filed at any time.

The WSBA separates two tracks. A Request for Assistance is for a minor problem such as a lawyer
not returning calls or not returning a client's file; ODC will contact the lawyer informally.
A formal grievance, filed online through the E-Grievance system or by mailing a Grievance Form,
is for an accusation of unethical conduct. ODC's own materials list fee disputes, rude
behavior, errors in judgment, and other non-ethical complaints as matters disciplinary counsel
generally does not investigate, alongside a direct statement that a grievance will not produce
a fee refund. Filing a billing complaint as a formal grievance is unlikely to help; a Request
for Assistance or a direct demand letter is the better first move.

ODC aims to send an initial decision letter within about two months of filing, though it can
take longer. If a grievance is dismissed, the person who filed it can request review by a
Disciplinary Board review committee, in writing, within 45 days of the dismissal date; that
review decision cannot be appealed further. Under Rule 2.13 of the Rules for Enforcement of
Lawyer Conduct (ELC), a lawyer cannot bill a grievant for the time spent responding to a
grievance, and under ELC 2.12, statements made to disciplinary counsel are privileged, so the
lawyer cannot sue the grievant over what was said in the complaint.

Public discipline, when it happens, ranges from an admonition or reprimand up to a suspension
of as long as three years or disbarment; only the Washington Supreme Court can suspend or
disbar a lawyer. Discipline is not a refund. If money mishandling is involved, i.e. a lawyer
took money and did not do the work or cannot account for it, that is also a Client Protection
Fund question (Section 5), and a grievance ordinarily has to be filed first.

RPC 1.4 and RPC 1.16(d), covering communication duties and what a lawyer must do on discharge
or withdrawal, otherwise track the model rule closely; RPC 1.16(d) is phrased as a list of
examples (notice, time to find new counsel, surrendering papers and property, refunding unearned
fees) rather than a fixed numbered list, so Washington's rule does not add or remove a step
count the way some states do.

## 5. Client protection fund

The Washington Lawyers' Fund for Client Protection is created by Admission to Practice Rule
(APR) 15 and administered under Board of Governors-adopted procedural regulations, with
information and applications reachable through
<https://www.wsba.org/for-the-public/concerns-about-a-lawyer/client-protection-fund-board>. The
Fund is overseen by the WSBA Board of Governors as Trustees and a Client Protection Board of 11
lawyers or other licensed legal professionals plus two public members.

What it covers: a pecuniary loss from the dishonest taking of, or failure to account for, money
or property by a lawyer, LLLT, or LPO in connection with the practice of law or a related
fiduciary role. A pattern showing the lawyer knew or should have known at the time fees were
taken that the work could not be performed can also qualify as dishonest conduct even though it
looks like an unearned-fee dispute.

What it excludes: any loss from a lawyer's negligent performance of services, so malpractice is
not covered, and consequential damages such as lost interest or what it cost you to chase the
money. Those two exclusions are written down, the first in APR 15 itself and the second in the
Fund's separate Procedural Regulations. WSBA's own Application Procedures document also says
directly that the Fund generally cannot resolve a fee dispute between a lawyer and a client, a
plain disagreement about the size of a bill, as distinct from the dishonest-taking or
failure-to-account conduct the Fund does cover.

Deadlines and process: an applicant must ordinarily have a disciplinary grievance on file
against the lawyer, unless the lawyer resigned in lieu of discipline, was disbarred, or died,
and a Fund application normally waits on the outcome of that disciplinary process. The
application itself must be filed within 3 years of when the loss was discovered or reasonably
should have been discovered.

The per-application cap on a gift from the Fund is $150,000 under the court's own Procedural
Regulations, which the pack's sources found still in force with no amendment newer than
September 1, 2017. WSBA's own dated Application Procedures document, updated June 25, 2026,
says that as of September 1, 2026 the maximum rises to $250,000. Both statements come from
official sources; the increase is simply not yet reflected on the court's own posted
regulations as of this pack's retrieval date. If you are applying before September 1, 2026,
plan around $150,000; if you are applying after that date, confirm directly with WSBA whether
the increase has taken effect and whether the court's regulations have been updated to match.
No separate aggregate cap on what a single lawyer's misconduct can cost the Fund across
multiple applications was found under either figure; awards are gifts made at the Trustees'
discretion, not entitlements, and a lawyer whose conduct triggers a payment is personally
liable to repay the Fund.

A gift is not necessarily paid all at once. An application approved for $5,000 or less is paid
in full when approved. A larger award is paid $5,000 on approval, with the rest held until the
Fund's fiscal year end, when it may be prorated depending on the Fund's balance for that year.
A reader expecting the full approved amount right away should know it may arrive in two
payments, and the second one may be less than the full remaining balance.

## 6. Small claims or equivalent court

Washington's small claims equivalent is the small claims department of the district court,
created under RCW 12.40.010. The jurisdictional limit is $10,000 for a claim brought by a
natural person (a human being, as the statute defines the term) and $5,000 for a claim brought
by any other kind of claimant, such as a business.

Under RCW 12.40.080, no attorney and no legal paraprofessional, and no one besides the plaintiff
and defendant, may appear or take part in a small claims hearing without the consent of the
judicial officer hearing the case; this restriction applies to a corporate party too, except as
allowed under RCW 12.40.025. That section lets a defendant in a regular district court case
move it down into the small claims department when the claim is within the small claims limit.
Only the defendant can make that move. When it happens, two things are preserved for the
plaintiff: an assignee who held the claim when the case began does not lose standing under RCW
12.40.070, and a lawyer who was already the plaintiff's attorney of record when the case began
is not barred by the no-attorney rule. This is a materially tighter
rule than most states' small claims courts, which typically just discourage lawyers rather than
requiring the judge's consent for either side to have one.

RCW 12.40.120 bars an appeal from a judgment under $250 entirely, and bars an appeal by the
party who chose to bring the case in small claims if that party's own claim was under $1,000. A
default judgment can still be set aside under the regular district court civil rules for setting
aside judgments.

The filing fee is $35 under RCW 12.40.020, plus any surcharge authorized under RCW 7.75.035; the
same $35 fee applies to a counterclaim, cross-claim, or third-party claim filed in the case. No
rule specific to suing a lawyer in small claims was found.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A fee agreement dispute, such as a lawyer's failure to refund an unearned fee, is a contract
claim, and Washington splits the period by whether the agreement was written. Under RCW
4.16.040(1), an action on a written contract must be commenced within 6 years. Under RCW
4.16.080(3), an action on a contract or liability that is not in writing must be commenced
within 3 years. RCW 4.16.005 sets the general rule that a limitation period runs from when the
cause of action accrued, unless a more specific statute says otherwise.

Legal malpractice, a claim that the lawyer's own negligence caused you harm, needs its own
counsel rather than self-help. It is not covered further in this kit beyond the deadline,
included so you know a clock exists. Washington courts apply the same 3-year period, RCW
4.16.080(3), to a legal malpractice claim, and hold that the period begins to run when the
client discovered, or reasonably should have discovered, the facts giving rise to the claim,
sometimes called the discovery rule. No statute of repose for legal malpractice, comparable to
some other states' outer time limits regardless of discovery, was found in the statute text
reviewed for this pack.

The description of the malpractice deadline and its discovery-rule accrual rests on a 2026
Washington Court of Appeals, Division II decision, which is unpublished under GR 14.1 and so
carries no binding precedential weight, though it is an official appellate court document and it
cites two published Court of Appeals decisions, Huff v. Roach and Cawdrey v. Hanson Baker
Ludlow Drumheller, P.S., for the same points. Those two published decisions were not
independently read for this pack; see the flagged table in
[verification_wa.md](verification_wa.md).

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

The Rules of Professional Conduct are a disciplinary code. Washington's own Scope section,
comment 20, states directly that a violation of a Rule should not by itself create a cause of
action against a lawyer or any presumption that a legal duty was breached, and that the Rules
are not designed to be a basis for civil liability. Washington does not give a client a private
cause of action for a rules violation.

Your actual money route runs through the other forums in this pack instead: a contract claim in
small claims or another court (Section 6), general mediation or a local fee dispute program
where available (Section 3), or the Client Protection Fund where the conduct was dishonest
rather than merely a bad bill (Section 5). Getting the lawyer disciplined (Section 4) is a
separate goal from getting money back, and it runs through a separate forum.
