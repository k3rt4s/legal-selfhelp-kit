# Ohio

This pack is for a person in Ohio who has a fee, billing, communication, or file-return
dispute with their own lawyer. It does not cover legal malpractice as a claim to run
yourself. Retrieved 2026-08-20.

What an Ohio reader most needs to know, that a reader in another state does not:

- Ohio has no statewide fee arbitration program for a client's dispute with their own
  lawyer. Fee dispute resolution is run locally by certified grievance committees, and
  coverage varies a great deal by county. Where a program exists, such as Cleveland's, and
  the client consents, Ohio's Duty to Cooperate rule (Gov.Bar.R. V(9)(G)) turns a lawyer's
  refusal to participate into a disciplinary referral, even though the client was never
  required to use the program at all, which is the asymmetry to know about.
- Ohio uses "illegal or clearly excessive" as its fee standard, not "unreasonable." Ohio's
  own rule commentary says this was a deliberate choice to keep the older disciplinary
  standard rather than adopt the ABA's wording.
- Ohio adds a rule the model does not have at all: a lawyer cannot call a fee
  "nonrefundable" or "earned upon receipt" without telling the client in writing, at the
  same time, that a refund may still be owed based on the value of the work actually done.
- The Lawyers' Fund for Client Protection caps a claim at $100,000 and requires the claim
  be filed within five years of the loss or its discovery. An undated application form
  still in circulation states $75,000; the dated rule text controls and is higher.
- Ohio's small claims division has a single statewide limit, $6,000, with no county
  variation.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct, numbering and adoption

The Supreme Court of Ohio adopts the Ohio Rules of Professional Conduct. Cite them as
"Prof.Cond.R." followed by the rule number, for example "Prof.Cond.R. 1.5," the citation
form the rules themselves and Ohio court opinions use. The current rules took effect
February 1, 2007, with amendments in effect through January 1, 2026.

Ohio does not renumber the rules a client is most likely to need. Prof.Cond.R. 1.4
(Communication), 1.5 (Fees and Expenses), 1.15 (Safekeeping Funds and Property), and 1.16
(Declining or Terminating Representation) sit at the same numbers a reader would expect
from the ABA Model Rules. The official publication point is the Supreme Court of Ohio's
Legal Resources site, which publishes the full rule text along with an official "Comparison
to ABA Model Rules of Professional Conduct" note after each rule, a feature not every
state's official publication carries:
<https://www.supremecourt.ohio.gov/docs/LegalResources/Rules/ProfConduct/profConductRules.pdf>,
retrieved 2026-08-20.

Separately, the Rules for the Government of the Bar of Ohio (cited "Gov.Bar.R." plus a
rule number) govern the disciplinary and complaint process itself, distinct from the
conduct rules that set the substantive standard. Both are Supreme Court of Ohio rules,
published at the same site.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

Ohio's eight fee-reasonableness factors, at Prof.Cond.R. 1.5(a)(1) through (8), match the
ABA model list item for item. The deviation in Ohio is not in the factor list. It is in the
standard itself and in two rules the model does not have.

**The standard.** Prof.Cond.R. 1.5(a) prohibits an "illegal or clearly excessive" fee, not
an "unreasonable" fee. The rule's own official commentary explains this was a deliberate
choice: Ohio kept the wording of its former Disciplinary Rule 2-106(A) rather than adopt
the Model Rule's "unreasonable" language. A fee is "clearly excessive" when, after review
of the facts, a lawyer of ordinary prudence would be left with a definite and firm
conviction the fee exceeds a reasonable fee. In practice this reads close to the model
standard, but it is worth knowing the exact word Ohio uses if you are citing the rule in a
letter.

**The nonrefundable-fee rule.** Prof.Cond.R. 1.5(d)(3) has no counterpart in the ABA Model
Rules. A lawyer may not charge a fee denominated "earned upon receipt," "nonrefundable," or
similar terms unless the client is simultaneously told, in writing, that if the lawyer does
not complete the representation for any reason, the client may be entitled to a refund of
all or part of the fee based on the value of the work actually done. Ohio's rule commentary
ties this addition directly to a history of disciplinary cases involving "retainers" that
clients were told, incorrectly, they could never get back. If your fee agreement calls the
retainer nonrefundable and you were never given that written disclosure, that gap is itself
worth raising.

**The malpractice-insurance disclosure.** Prof.Cond.R. 1.4(c) has no Model Rule
counterpart either. Maintaining professional liability insurance is not mandatory for an
Ohio lawyer in solo or most practice settings, but a lawyer who does not carry a minimum
level of coverage must promptly tell a prospective or current client. Ohio's commentary
notes it is one of only a few states with a rule like this. It bears on fee disputes
indirectly: if a lawyer's uninsured status matters to how you approach a dispute, this is
the rule that would have required you to be told about it.

No Ohio-specific contingent fee percentage cap or sliding scale was found for the kind of
matter this pack covers. Contingent fees are prohibited in domestic relations matters where
payment depends on securing a divorce or on the amount of support or a property settlement,
and in criminal defense representation, matching the model rule's two prohibited
categories.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

Ohio has no statewide fee arbitration program that a client can invoke directly against
their own lawyer. What exists instead is enabling authority under Gov.Bar.R. V, Section
5(G), which lets a certified grievance committee, the local body that also handles bar
complaints, adopt and use Board-developed alternative dispute resolution procedures for
client dissatisfaction that does not rise to a disciplinary violation. A committee cannot
invent its own procedure; only ADR methods the Board of Professional Conduct has developed
may be used. Coverage depends entirely on whether the bar association in your county has
built a program on that authority.

The asymmetry Ohio does have is real, and the mechanism behind it is narrower than it first
looks. Gov.Bar.R. V, Section 9(G), the Duty to Cooperate, lets the Board, Disciplinary
Counsel, or a certified grievance committee's officers call on any attorney to assist in an
investigation or testify in a hearing "including mediation and alternative dispute
resolution procedures," and states that no attorney may refuse. That is a general
cooperation duty aimed at compelling testimony and assistance, not a rule written to force a
lawyer to become a respondent in a fee mediation. But the Cleveland Metropolitan Bar
Association's own fee dispute rules read it that way and act on it: when a client's petition
is received, the CMBA sends the attorney a copy of Rule V(9)(G) along with the request to
respond, and if the attorney will not sign the consent-to-arbitration form, the committee
does not force a hearing. It closes its file and refers the matter to the certified
grievance committee instead, for the attorney's "failure to cooperate with a procedure
governed by Gov. Bar R. V(9)(G)." In practical terms, a lawyer who refuses is not physically
compelled into a hearing room, but the refusal itself becomes a disciplinary referral. The
client, by contrast, is never required to use the program at all.

The clearest confirmed example of a local program is the Cleveland Metropolitan Bar
Association's Lawyer-Client Fee Dispute Resolution Committee. Its own rules, enacted
August 18, 2021 and still in force, provide: the client's request and consent trigger the
process, the attorney is given 14 days to respond and, if the client has consented,
arbitration is mandatory for the attorney (enforced through the referral described above
rather than by direct compulsion), there is no cost to file, the claim must be filed within
two years of the end of the representation or the final bill, whichever is later, the burden
is on the attorney to prove the fee was reasonable, and the panel's decision has the legal
effect of an account stated. CMBA's rules state that this makes the decision admissible in
court as a final, binding determination of the amount owed under Chapter 2711 of the Ohio
Revised Code, Ohio's arbitration statute; R.C. 2711.09 in fact lets a party apply to a court
of common pleas within a year of the award to confirm it and have judgment entered on it.
CMBA's rules and procedures are posted at
<https://www.clemetrobar.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Rules-and-Procedures.pdf>, and its
public-facing "Dispute with a Lawyer" page, including the no-cost statement, is at
<https://clemetrobar.org/dispute-with-a-lawyer/>, both retrieved 2026-08-20.

By contrast, the Cincinnati Bar Association's Fee Arbitration Committee is voluntary on both
sides: its own program page, at
<https://www.cincybar.org/About-Us/Services/Fee-Arbitration>, retrieved 2026-08-20, states
that, apart from a separate rule governing fee-splitting disputes between lawyers
(Prof.Cond.R. 1.5(f), which is mandatory but has nothing to do with a client's dispute with
their own lawyer), "arbitration is a voluntary process, and the parties to a fee dispute must
give their written consent before a matter can be accepted."

Columbus, Toledo, and Dayton bar associations were not confirmed one way or the other in
the time available for this pack. This list is almost certainly incomplete. If you are
outside Cleveland or Cincinnati, contact your county or city bar association directly and
ask whether it runs a fee dispute program, and if so, whether it is binding and whether
your lawyer can be made to participate.

If no local program covers you, the realistic path is a written demand followed by small
claims court (Section 6) for amounts within its limit, or a bar complaint (Section 4) if
the conduct looks dishonest rather than merely a bad bill.

## 4. Bar complaint process

Ohio splits disciplinary intake between the Office of Disciplinary Counsel, which handles
complaints statewide and against judges, and certified grievance committees, which are
local bodies, usually run by county or city bar associations and certified by the Board of
Professional Conduct, that handle complaints against lawyers in their area. A grievance can
go to either the Office of Disciplinary Counsel or the certified grievance committee for
the county where the lawyer practices. The Supreme Court of Ohio's own "About the
Disciplinary System" page describes all three bodies (Office of Disciplinary Counsel, Board
of Professional Conduct, and the Lawyers' Fund for Client Protection) and is at
<https://www.supremecourt.ohio.gov/disciplinary-system/ds/complaints/>, retrieved
2026-08-20. The grievance form itself, filed with the Office of Disciplinary Counsel or the
local certified grievance committee, is posted at
<https://www.supremecourt.ohio.gov/docs/DisciplinarySys/odc/odcform.pdf>, retrieved
2026-08-20.

Ohio's rules do not describe a separate informal "consumer assistance" intake track the way
some states do. What they do provide, at the local level, is the alternative dispute
resolution authority described in Section 3: a certified grievance committee may handle
"allegations of client dissatisfaction that do not constitute disciplinary violations"
through mediation or similar means, kept separate from the formal grievance process that
can lead to discipline. A billing disagreement that does not involve dishonesty is the kind
of complaint this softer track is built for. A formal grievance filed over a fee dispute
alone is more likely to be closed without discipline than to produce a refund, because
discipline and repayment are different remedies.

No statewide deadline for filing a grievance was located in the rules reviewed for this
pack; the gap list below records where that search stopped.

Discipline is not a refund. The Office of Disciplinary Counsel and certified grievance
committees can investigate, and a proven violation can lead to sanctions up to disbarment,
but getting money back runs through a separate forum: the local fee dispute committee where
one exists (Section 3), small claims or another court (Section 6), or the Lawyers' Fund for
Client Protection where the conduct was dishonest (Section 5).

## 5. Client protection fund

The Lawyers' Fund for Client Protection of the Supreme Court of Ohio, established under
Gov.Bar.R. VIII, reimburses losses caused by the dishonest conduct of an Ohio-admitted
attorney acting as an attorney or in a fiduciary capacity. "Dishonest conduct" is defined in
the rule as defalcation, embezzlement, or the wrongful taking or conversion of money,
property, or other things of value. The Fund's own page, with its eligibility rules, the
$100,000 maximum award figure, the five-year filing deadline, and the application itself, is
at <https://www.supremecourt.ohio.gov/courts/advisory/boards/client-protection/>, retrieved
2026-08-20.

What it excludes: the loss cannot be covered by any insurance, fidelity bond, or similar
fund, and a claim cannot be brought by the attorney's spouse, child, parent, grandparent,
sibling, partner, associate, employee, or employer, or by a business entity the attorney
controls, except where the Board finds extreme hardship or unusual circumstances. The rule
does not list an ordinary fee dispute as a covered loss; the fund exists for dishonesty, not
for a disagreement over how much work was done or what it was worth.

To be eligible, the attorney must, on or after the rule's effective date, have been
disbarred, suspended, or publicly reprimanded, have resigned, or have been convicted of
embezzlement or misappropriation, and the claim must be presented within five years of the
loss or its discovery. Taking affirmative action against the attorney within that five-year
period, such as filing a grievance or a lawsuit, tolls the deadline until that proceeding
ends. Where disciplinary or criminal proceedings cannot go forward because the attorney
cannot be located or is deceased, the Board may still consider a timely application.

The rule caps any single reimbursement at $100,000; no aggregate per-lawyer cap is stated in
the rule. The Board sets the actual amount within that $100,000 ceiling at its discretion,
and may adopt a sliding-scale percentage of loss to preserve the fund for other claimants.
Payment from the fund is expressly a matter of grace, not a right, and the Board's
determination is final. A separate, undated application form still circulated by the
Supreme Court of Ohio's own site states a $75,000 maximum. The rule itself, Gov.Bar.R. VIII,
Section 5, was last amended October 15, 2024, is dated, and controls; this pack uses the
rule's $100,000 figure. If you rely on this fund, confirm the current cap directly with the
Fund before you count on either number, because the form conflicts with the rule and is not
dated on its face.

## 6. Small claims or equivalent court

Ohio's small claims equivalent is the small claims division of a municipal or county court.
The statewide jurisdictional limit is $6,000, exclusive of interest and costs, set by
statute (R.C. 1925.02) and applied the same way in every county; no local variation was
found. A counterclaim or cross-claim that exceeds $6,000 does not itself defeat the small
claims division's jurisdiction, but triggers transfer to the regular docket. The party a
claim is brought against may also ask the court to transfer the case to the regular docket,
by motion with a supporting affidavit showing a good defense; whether to grant it is up to
the court, not automatic.

A corporation may bring or defend a small claims case through a lawyer, or, without one,
through a bona fide officer or salaried employee who may file and present the corporation's
claim or defense, but who may not cross-examine, argue, or otherwise act as counsel would
without an attorney present, under R.C. 1925.17.

No rule specific to suing a lawyer in small claims court was found; ordinary small claims
procedure applies. Confirm the current filing fee with the clerk of the court where you
plan to file, since filing fees are set locally and were not confirmed statewide for this
pack.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A fee agreement dispute, such as a lawyer's failure to refund an unearned fee, is a
contract claim. Ohio's limitation period for an action on an agreement, contract, or
promise in writing is six years from when the cause of action accrued, at R.C. 2305.06,
effective since June 16, 2021. A version of this statute amended by Senate Bill 219 takes
effect September 23, 2026. Its text, published by the Ohio Revised Code's own site, keeps
the six-year period unchanged; the amendment only adds a cross-reference to a new statute
of limitations for oil and gas lease actions (R.C. 2305.041(B)), a subject unrelated to a
fee dispute. Nothing about the contract limitation period for a fee dispute changes on that
date.

Legal malpractice, a claim that the lawyer's own negligence caused you harm, needs its own
counsel rather than self-help. It is included here only so you know a clock exists. Under
R.C. 2305.11(A), a legal malpractice claim must be brought within one year after it
accrues. The Ohio Supreme Court applies a discovery rule: the claim accrues, and the one
year starts, at the "cognizable event" when the client discovers, or should have
discovered, that an injury is related to the lawyer's act or omission and that pursuing a
remedy against the lawyer may be needed, or when the attorney-client relationship for that
matter ends, whichever is later. Separately, R.C. 2305.117(B), effective June 2, 2021, sets
a four-year statute of repose: no legal malpractice action may be brought more than four
years after the act or omission itself, regardless of when it was discovered, with limited
exceptions for minors and persons of unsound mind. The discovery rule can delay when the
clock starts; the repose period caps how long it can be delayed.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

Ohio's Rules of Professional Conduct are a disciplinary code, and their Scope section says
so directly: a violation of a rule should not itself create a cause of action against a
lawyer or a presumption that a legal duty was breached, and the rules are not designed to be
a basis for civil liability. The Scope section adds the same qualifier the ABA model
carries: because the rules do establish standards of conduct, a lawyer's violation of a rule
may be evidence of a breach of the applicable standard of conduct in a case that is
otherwise a recognized claim, such as malpractice.

Your actual money route runs through the other forums in this pack instead: a contract
claim in small claims or another court (Section 6), a local fee dispute program where one
covers you (Section 3), or the Lawyers' Fund for Client Protection where the conduct was
dishonest rather than merely a bad bill (Section 5). Getting the lawyer disciplined (Section
4) is a separate goal from getting money back, and it runs through a separate forum.
