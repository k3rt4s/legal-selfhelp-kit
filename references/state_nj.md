# New Jersey: your lawyer, your fee, and your options

This pack sits on top of [national_baseline.md](national_baseline.md). Read that file first.
This file covers only what New Jersey does differently, or specifically, on top of that model.
Sourcing for every claim below is in [verification_nj.md](verification_nj.md). Retrieval date
for this pack is 2026-08-20.

This is not legal advice, and it is not a substitute for talking to a New Jersey lawyer about
your own situation. It is a map of the forums that exist and what they can and cannot do.

What a New Jersey reader most needs to know, that a reader in another state does not:

- New Jersey runs one statewide Fee Arbitration Program. It is mandatory on the lawyer once
  you file correctly and within the window: the lawyer cannot refuse to participate, and if
  the lawyer does not respond, the hearing goes forward without them. It is not mandatory on
  you, and you can choose court instead if you would rather.
- Before a lawyer can sue you over a disputed fee, the lawyer must first tell you, in writing,
  that you can take the dispute to arbitration instead, and give you the District Fee
  Secretary's contact information. If the lawyer skips this and sues anyway, that is a defect
  in the lawyer's own case.
- The $50 fee arbitration filing fee has a waiver. If you cannot afford it, you can request a
  fee waiver from the Office of Attorney Ethics Fee Arbitration Unit instead of paying.
- If your retainer agreement has an arbitration clause, read it first. Under *Delaney v.
  Dickey*, a properly explained clause can route a fee dispute, and even a future malpractice
  claim, into private arbitration instead of this state program or court.
- New Jersey caps contingent fees in most tort cases on a sliding scale under Rule 1:21-7(c),
  and requires a written fee agreement under RPC 1.5(b) with only a narrow repeat-client
  exception.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct: numbering and adoption

New Jersey adopts its version of the ABA Model Rules through Rule 1:14 of the Rules Governing
the Courts of the State of New Jersey. New Jersey calls them the Rules of Professional Conduct,
abbreviated RPC, and keeps the same base numbering as the ABA model: RPC 1.4 covers
communication, RPC 1.5 covers fees, RPC 1.15 covers safekeeping property, and RPC 1.16 covers
declining or terminating representation. If you already read the national baseline, the rule
numbers you saw there are the same numbers New Jersey uses.

The Judiciary's own rule-publication page, <https://www.njcourts.gov/attorneys/rules-of-court>,
renders its content through a search tool rather than static rule text, so the text used to
write this section came from a commercial compilation instead, Gann Law Books' compiled Rules of
Professional Conduct at
<https://www.gannlaw.com/OnlineApp/dtSearch-Data/TextFiles/CourtRules/P1-02.pdf>. See
[verification_nj.md](verification_nj.md) for that caveat and the date the compilation's own
internal comments were current through.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

RPC 1.5(a) lists the same eight reasonableness factors as the ABA Model Rule. New Jersey did not
add a factor or reorder the list.

Where New Jersey diverges is the writing requirement. The Model Rule prefers, but does not
require, a written fee agreement. RPC 1.5(b) requires it: the basis or rate of the fee must be
communicated to the client in writing, before or within a reasonable time after the
representation starts. The one exception is a client the lawyer has regularly represented
before, on the same fee basis as before, who does not need a fresh writing each time. If your
New Jersey lawyer never gave you anything in writing about the fee, and you are a new client,
that alone is worth raising.

New Jersey also caps contingent fees in most tort cases on a sliding scale, under Rule
1:21-7(c). The cap does not apply to statutory fee-shifting claims like employment
discrimination, and it does not apply where the client is a subrogee. Where it applies, the
scale runs: 33-1/3% of the first $750,000 recovered, 30% of the next $750,000, 25% of the next
$750,000, 20% of the next $750,000, and anything above that requires the lawyer to apply to the
Assignment Judge for approval of a higher percentage. A lawyer can always charge less than the
cap allows. The cap is a ceiling, not a target, and the fee still has to be reasonable under RPC
1.5(a) even inside it. One caution on these figures specifically: the only source this kit could reach that actually states them is an archived 2015 capture of the Judiciary's own site. The current njcourts.gov does not serve the rule text to automated retrieval, so the kit has confirmed the 2015 wording and not today's. Confirm the percentages with the court or a lawyer before you rely on them. See verification_nj.md.

No New Jersey rule against nonrefundable fees, beyond the general reasonableness requirement and
the RPC 1.16(d) refund duty covered in Section 1, was found in the sourcing for this pack.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

This is the most useful forum in New Jersey for a straightforward fee dispute, and it works
differently from a state with no such program.

New Jersey runs one statewide Fee Arbitration Program, made up of 17 District Fee Arbitration
Committees, administered by the Office of Attorney Ethics. See NJ Courts, "File a Fee Dispute,"
<https://www.njcourts.gov/attorneys/attorney-ethics-and-discipline/file-fee-dispute>. This is
not a court case. The client chooses whether to invoke it. The lawyer does not get that choice:
once a district fee secretary accepts a properly filed request, the lawyer is required to
respond, and a lawyer who does not respond is simply barred from participating rather than
stopping the hearing. It goes forward and decides the fee without the lawyer's cooperation.

Before a lawyer can sue a client to collect a disputed fee, the lawyer must first give the
client written notice of the right to seek fee arbitration, including the name, address, and
phone number of the District Fee Secretary. This notice requirement runs on the lawyer, not the
client. If the lawyer skips it and sues anyway, the fee-dispute rule (sourced to a commercial
mirror in this pack, see [verification_nj.md](verification_nj.md)) provides that the complaint
can be dismissed for failing to allege the notice was given.

Once notice is given, the client has 30 days to file a Fee Arbitration Request Form or loses the
right to arbitrate. Filing the form, where a lawsuit over the fee is already pending, stays that
court action.

Fee arbitration is not available in every situation. It does not apply where the disputed fee
exceeds $100,000, where the client missed the 30-day window, where more than six years have
passed since the last services were rendered, where a court already set the fee, or where the
dispute raises substantial legal questions or involves other interested parties. Below $3,000,
a single Fee Committee member can decide the case. At $3,000 or more, it typically goes to a
three-member panel of two attorneys and one public member. The filing fee is $50, payable to
"Disciplinary Oversight Committee." If you cannot afford the $50, a Fee Waiver Request Form is
available; call the Office of Attorney Ethics Fee Arbitration Unit at 609-403-7800, ext. 34120,
and file the completed form together with your Fee Arbitration Request. That same number is the
one to call if you need an accommodation at any stage, and the earlier you ask the better.

A fee arbitration decision is final and binding on both sides. If it orders a refund, the lawyer
must pay within 30 days absent an appeal. If the lawyer does not pay, call the Office of Attorney
Ethics Fee Arbitration Unit at 609-403-7800, ext. 34120, and ask the OAE to file a motion to have
the lawyer suspended from practicing law until they comply with the decision. That is a real
enforcement route, and it is worth knowing it exists before you start. An appeal is narrow: it is available only
for procedural unfairness, fraud, a gross mistake of law, or a panel member's conflict of
interest, must be filed within 21 days of the written decision, and goes to the Disciplinary
Review Board.

A Fee Committee cannot award legal malpractice damages, though it may weigh the quality of the
lawyer's work in deciding whether the fee itself was reasonable. Filing for fee arbitration does
not bar a later malpractice suit, and what is said during arbitration is not admissible in one.

One further wrinkle: New Jersey's Supreme Court, in *Delaney v. Dickey*, held that a retainer
agreement can lawfully require the client to arbitrate future fee disputes and malpractice
claims in a private arbitration, instead of through this program or in court, so long as the
arbitration clause is explained to the client in plain, balanced language. If your retainer
agreement has an arbitration clause, read it before assuming this state program is your path.

## 4. Bar complaint process

New Jersey calls its disciplinary intake process an ethics grievance. It is filed with the
Office of Attorney Ethics, either online through the JEDS system or by mail. See NJ Courts,
"File an Ethics Grievance,"
<https://www.njcourts.gov/attorneys/attorney-ethics-and-discipline/file-ethics-grievance>.
Eighteen District Ethics Committees assist with review across the state.

Initial review takes up to 45 days and ends one of two ways: a declination letter, or a docket
number that opens an investigation. A grievance that is really a fee dispute will not be
investigated here. It gets redirected to the Fee Arbitration Program described in Section 3.

A docketed grievance is investigated either by a District Ethics Committee member, with a target
of six months or less, or by the OAE itself for complex matters, with a target of nine months or
less. If the investigation finds clear and convincing evidence of a violation, it leads to a
formal complaint, or, for minor matters, a nonpublic diversion program that corrects the conduct
without public discipline. A formal complaint gives the attorney 21 days to answer, followed by
a hearing before a panel, typically one public member and two attorneys, or a special ethics
master. A decision can be appealed to the Disciplinary Review Board within 21 days.

No specific deadline for filing the initial grievance was found in the sourcing for this pack.
See the gap list in [verification_nj.md](verification_nj.md).

## 5. Client protection fund

The New Jersey Lawyers' Fund for Client Protection reimburses clients for losses caused by a
lawyer's dishonest conduct. It is not a malpractice insurer and it is not a fee-dispute forum.
See the Fund's official publication CN 12229,
<https://www.njcourts.gov/sites/default/files/forms/12229_njlfcp.pdf>.

To recover, you generally have to show the lawyer received your money or property, improperly
took or used it, and caused you a loss through dishonest conduct. The Fund explicitly excludes
pure fee disputes, bad investment decisions, and professional negligence or malpractice. It pays
no interest and no consequential damages.

Before the Fund will investigate a claim, the lawyer generally must already be suspended,
disbarred, deceased, on disability inactive status, or convicted of embezzlement or theft. The
per-claimant cap is $400,000 for losses arising after January 1, 2007, and the aggregate cap
against any single lawyer is $1.5 million. There is no filing fee, and a claim generally must be
filed within one year of the date of the lawyer's first discipline or death, though the Fund's
Trustees may allow a late filing for good cause. The Fund is governed by Court Rule 1:28-3, which
gives its Board of Trustees sole discretion over how much, when, and under what conditions a
claim is paid. Anyone who helps you file a claim is barred from charging you a fee for that help.

## 6. Small claims or equivalent court

New Jersey's small claims forum is the Small Claims Section of the Special Civil Part, for
lawsuits of $5,000 or less. The filing fee is $35 for one defendant, plus $5 for each additional
defendant. Malpractice claims against a lawyer, doctor, dentist, or other professional cannot be
filed here.

For larger disputes, the Special Civil Part itself has a jurisdictional limit of $20,000. Filing
fees there are $50 to sue one defendant for $5,000 or less, $75 to sue one defendant for more
than $5,000, plus $5 for each additional defendant, a $10 mail-service fee per defendant, and
$100 for a jury demand.

No county-by-county variation in either limit was found; both appear to apply statewide. Whether
a corporate party needs its own lawyer in these courts, and whether either side can appeal or
get a new trial after a Small Claims or Special Civil Part judgment, were not confirmed in the
sourcing for this pack. See the gap list.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

New Jersey applies a single six-year period, under N.J.S.A. 2A:14-1, to both a claim on a
contract and a tortious injury claim not specifically covered by a shorter statute elsewhere in
the title. Legal malpractice claims fall under this six-year period.

New Jersey courts apply a discovery rule to legal malpractice claims. Under *Grunwald v.
Bronkesh*, the six-year clock does not start until the client has actually suffered damage and
has discovered, or reasonably should have discovered, the facts underlying the malpractice
claim. That means the period does not necessarily run from the date of the lawyer's mistake. It
runs from when a reasonable client would have realized something was wrong and been harmed by
it.

No New Jersey statute of repose specific to legal malpractice, setting an outer limit regardless
of discovery, was found in the sourcing for this pack.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

A violation of the RPCs, standing alone, does not create a private right to sue a lawyer for
money damages in New Jersey. The state Supreme Court confirmed this directly in *Baxt v.
Liloia*. A proven RPC violation can still matter in a legal malpractice case, where it may be
relevant and admissible as evidence that the lawyer breached the standard of care, but the
violation itself is not the claim. The claim has to be an otherwise-recognized cause of action,
like legal malpractice, with the RPC violation offered as evidence inside it.

This mirrors the national baseline's framing. New Jersey confirms it specifically through its
own Supreme Court, rather than leaving it to the Model Rules' own scope language.

## What actually changes here

New Jersey's fee arbitration program is structurally different from a state with no such
program. The pre-action notice requirement runs on the lawyer, not the client: before the lawyer
can even sue you over a disputed fee, the lawyer has to tell you, in writing, that you can take
it to arbitration instead. Once you file within the 30-day window, the result binds the lawyer
too, with only narrow grounds to appeal, and filing automatically stops any pending collection
suit. In a state without a comparable program, nothing stops the lawyer from suing you directly
over the fee and making you defend it in court on the lawyer's timeline. Check your retainer
agreement for an arbitration clause first, because under *Delaney v. Dickey* it can route you
somewhere else entirely.
