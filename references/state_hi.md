# Hawaii: handling a dispute with your own lawyer

This section is for a person in Hawaii who has a problem with their own lawyer over fees,
billing, communication, getting their file back, or an advance payment that was never
earned or never refunded. It does not cover legal malpractice claims or how to run one
yourself. Retrieved August 20, 2026.

What is different about Hawaii:

- The Hawaii State Bar Association runs a Fee Dispute Program (mediation, and arbitration
  only if both sides agree in writing) through its Attorney-Client Relations Committee, but
  its dedicated webpage and rules PDF are currently broken links on HSBA's own site. The
  program still exists and is reachable by phone; read section 3 for the number and what
  the published rules say.
- Hawaii bans calling any fee nonrefundable or "earned upon receipt." Advance fee payments
  are presumed unearned and must sit in a trust account until the lawyer actually earns
  them. Flat fees over $1,500 require a detailed written agreement. Read section 2.
- There is no right of appeal from a small claims judgment in Hawaii, by statute. The only
  route back into the same court is a motion to alter or set aside the judgment, filed
  within 10 days. Read section 6 carefully before you assume you can appeal a bad result.
- The rules of professional conduct use plain ABA-style numbering. Cite "Rule 1.5" in a
  letter, not a state-prefixed number.
- No Hawaii source was found addressing whether a lawyer may hold onto your case file until
  you pay. The only lien a lawyer is authorized to use attaches to money or a judgment in
  a case, not to your physical file. Read section 2's note on this before assuming either
  answer.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct: numbering and adoption

Hawaii's rules governing lawyers are the Hawaii Rules of Professional Conduct (HRPC),
adopted and promulgated by the Hawaii Supreme Court by order SCRU-11-0001047, adopted June
25, 2013, effective January 1, 2014, and amended since. The current compiled version
carries a last-amended date of January 1, 2026 on the Judiciary's own rules index.

The HRPC use plain ABA-style numbering, the same numbers you would expect from the
national baseline: Rule 1.5 is the fee rule, Rule 1.4 is communication, Rule 1.15 is
safekeeping property, Rule 1.16 is termination. There is no state prefix. Cite it in a
letter as "Rule 1.5," "Rule 1.4," and so on.

The official publication point is the Hawaii State Judiciary's Rules of Court page, which
links the current HRPC as "Exhibit A" to the Rules of the Supreme Court of Hawaii (RSCH):
<https://www.courts.state.hi.us/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/hrpcond_ada.pdf>.

Three separate bodies matter to a client in a dispute, and they are not the same office:

1. The Office of Disciplinary Counsel (ODC), an agency of the Hawaii Supreme Court, that
   investigates complaints against lawyers.
2. The Disciplinary Board of the Hawaii Supreme Court, which reviews investigations,
   conducts formal proceedings, and imposes or recommends discipline, operating under its
   own Rules of the Disciplinary Board (RDB), last amended October 23, 2025:
   <https://www.courts.state.hi.us/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/rdb_ada.pdf>.
3. The Lawyers' Fund for Client Protection, a separate fund administered by five trustees
   appointed by the Supreme Court, not by ODC or the Disciplinary Board, covered in section
   5 below. ODC provides investigative and administrative support to the Fund, but the Fund
   is its own body with its own trustees and its own rules.

The Rules of the Supreme Court of Hawaii (RSCH) is the broader rule set that creates and
governs ODC, the Disciplinary Board, and the Fund, most recently amended January 1, 2026
with a later single-rule amendment effective July 23, 2026:
<https://www.courts.state.hi.us/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/rsch_ada.pdf>.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

This is the section with the most practical value in this pack.

Rule 1.5(a) lists the same eight reasonableness factors as the ABA model rule, with one
addition folded into the eighth factor. Where the model rule simply asks whether the fee
is fixed or contingent, Hawaii's version of factor 8 adds, for contingency cases, "the risk
of no recovery and the conscionability of the fee in light of the net recovery to the
client." That gives a client in a contingency case a second, distinct argument beyond
whether the percentage itself was reasonable: whether what the client actually walked away
with, after the fee, was unconscionable.

Rule 1.5(b) requires the scope of representation and the fee basis to be communicated to
the client in writing before or within a reasonable time after the representation starts.
Hawaii adds a dollar carve-out the model rule does not have: no writing is required if it
is reasonably foreseeable that the total cost of representation, including fees, will be
$250 or less. Above that, and outside the "regularly represented client, same basis"
exception, the writing requirement applies.

The most important deviation is in the same subsection. Hawaii's Rule 1.5(b) states plainly
that fee payments received before legal services have been rendered "are presumed to be
unearned" and must be held in a trust account under Rule 1.15 until earned, and that "fee
agreements may not describe any fee as non-refundable or earned upon receipt." If your
lawyer's agreement calls your retainer nonrefundable, or says a flat fee is earned the
moment you pay it, that language does not match the rule that actually governs Hawaii
lawyers. This is the strongest, most direct tool in this pack for a Hawaii client trying to
get an unearned retainer back.

Rule 1.5(c) adds a dollar-triggered writing requirement the model rule does not have at
all. If a flat fee for the entire representation exceeds $1,500, the lawyer must use a
written, client-signed agreement stating: the nature and scope of the services; the total
fee and payment terms; how the flat fee is incrementally earned, by milestone or hourly
rate; that the fee is held in trust until earned; the client's right to an accounting on
request; and the client's right to a refund of the unearned portion if the engagement ends
early. If your lawyer charged a flat fee over $1,500 with no such written agreement, that
is itself a rule violation you can point to directly.

On the file-return and lien question: Hawaii Rule 1.8(i)(1) allows a lawyer to "acquire a
lien authorized by law to secure the lawyer's fee or expenses." The only such lien this
kit could find is Hawaii's attorney's charging lien statute, HRS section 507-81, which
attaches to a client's cause of action, judgment, or the proceeds recovered in a case, not
to the client's physical file. Separately, Rule 1.16(d) requires a lawyer, on termination,
to take reasonably practicable steps to protect the client, including "surrendering papers
and property to which the client is entitled." Neither the rule text nor its comments say
whether a lawyer may hold the file itself as leverage for unpaid fees. No Hawaii statute,
court rule, or published ethics opinion resolving that specific question was found within
the scope of this kit. Treat this as an open question rather than a settled right on either
side, and see the gap list in the verification file.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

The Hawaii State Bar Association (HSBA) does run a fee dispute program, through its
Attorney-Client Relations Committee. Published rules for it (11 rules, adopted September
2006, revised January 2007) offer non-binding mediation of a fee dispute by default, with
binding arbitration only if both you and the lawyer sign a separate written agreement to
arbitrate. The administrative fee is $100 for mediation or $150 for arbitration, paid by
the lawyer being complained about, not by you. This kit could not find these rules on
HSBA's current, redesigned website; the dedicated program page and the rules PDF are both
dead links there now. The rules were confirmed instead from a 2021 snapshot of that page
held by the Internet Archive's Wayback Machine. The program's continued existence today
was separately confirmed on HSBA's live FAQ page, which names a fee-dispute process and
gives the phone number 808-537-1868, the same number the 2021 snapshot used for this
committee: <https://hsba.org/faqs>. If you want to use this program, call that number and
ask HSBA directly for its current fee-dispute intake process, since the web path to it is
currently broken.

A second, weaker mechanism sits inside the disciplinary process itself. Under RSCH Rule
2.7(b)(3), if the Office of Disciplinary Counsel decides, in its sole discretion, that a
complaint involves only "minor misconduct," Counsel may reach an agreement with the lawyer
(called the "respondent" in the rule) to divert the matter to non-disciplinary proceedings,
which "may consist of fee arbitration, arbitration, mediation, lawyer practice assistance,
substance abuse recovery programs, psychological counseling, mentoring, or any other
non-disciplinary proceedings authorized by the supreme court." Read that carefully: this is
ODC's discretion to offer, not a client's right to demand it, and it requires the lawyer's
own agreement. There is no separate rule of procedure governing how this diversion track
actually runs, no dollar ceiling, no filing deadline, and no filing fee stated anywhere in
the rules this kit read; it is not a substitute for the HSBA program above.

If you want a forum with real teeth, your practical options are: the HSBA Fee Dispute
Program above, a complaint to ODC (see section 4, including its limited restitution power),
a small claims or civil court claim for money owed under a contract theory (section 6), or
a claim to the Lawyers' Fund if the conduct meets the Fund's dishonest-conduct definition
(section 5).

## 4. Bar complaint process

The Office of Disciplinary Counsel (ODC), an agency of the Hawaii Supreme Court, is where
you file a complaint against a Hawaii lawyer. The Disciplinary Board publishes the process at
https://dbhawaii.org/the-attorney-disciplinary-complaint-process/ . This kit did not find a separate informal or
consumer-assistance intake track distinct from the standard complaint process; RDB Rule 11
does describe a "Grievances" procedure, but that rule applies specifically to grievances
filed against Disciplinary Board members or Disciplinary Counsel themselves, not to
ordinary complaints against a lawyer in private practice.

There is no deadline for filing a complaint. The Board's own complaint-process page states
that there is no statute of limitations or time limit for filing an ethical complaint against
an attorney, though it notes that laches can apply where a delay actually prejudices someone.
It also says there is no standing requirement, so you do not have to have been harmed
yourself to file. That same page says that where a grievance comes out of a minor dispute or
misunderstanding between a lawyer and a client, ODC may try to resolve it informally rather
than open a full investigation. RDB Rule 12(d) sets internal complaint-processing time goals for ODC and the Board,
generally an eighteen-month total from opening a case to a formal petition or other
disposition, but that is a target for how long ODC's own process should take, not a
deadline for you to file.

Discipline is not a refund. Filing a complaint can result in a private admonition, a
reprimand, suspension, disbarment, or other sanctions, but the process exists to police the
lawyer, not to hand you your money back.

Hawaii does have a disciplinary restitution mechanism, which is not universal among states.
RSCH Rule 2.3(c) states that "restitution and/or payment of costs... may also be ordered by
the supreme court or by the Disciplinary Board" as part of a disciplinary outcome. This is
discretionary, tied to a disciplinary sanction, and not something you file for directly the
way you would file a fee arbitration claim; it is a possible outcome of a successful
complaint, not a guaranteed one.

## 5. Client protection fund

The Hawaii Lawyers' Fund for Client Protection was established by the Hawaii Supreme Court
in 1981. It is administered by five trustees, three lawyers and two nonlawyers, appointed
by the Supreme Court for staggered five-year terms under RSCH Rule 10.1, with investigative
and administrative help from ODC. Its own page:
<https://www.courts.state.hi.us/legal_references/attorneys/lawyers_fund_page>.

The Fund reimburses losses caused by a Hawaii lawyer's "dishonest conduct," a term RSCH
Rule 10.1(a) defines specifically to include: defalcation or embezzlement of money;
wrongful taking or conversion of money, property, or other things of value; borrowing
money from a client with no intention or reasonable ability to repay it; and, notably for
a fee dispute, "refusal to refund unearned fees received in advance where the attorney
performed no services or such an insignificant portion of the services that the refusal
constitutes a wrongful taking or conversion of money." An ordinary disagreement over
whether a bill was reasonable does not meet this definition. A lawyer who took a retainer,
did essentially nothing, and refuses to give it back may.

RSCH Rule 10.3(a) requires the loss to arise from an attorney-client or similar fiduciary
relationship, and requires that the lawyer have already reached a qualifying event: death,
bankruptcy, adjudicated incompetence or incapacity, disbarment, suspension, voluntary
resignation from practice, becoming a judgment debtor of the claimant based on the same
dishonest conduct, or a criminal conviction. In practice, a Fund claim usually follows some
other proceeding against the lawyer, not a standalone first step.

Excluded losses under Rule 10.3(b) include losses of the lawyer's own family, partners,
associates, employers, and employees; losses covered by a bond, surety, or insurance
contract; losses recoverable under a banker's blanket bond; and consequential or incidental
damages such as lost interest or the cost of seeking recovery. The trustees may, in cases
of extreme hardship or in the interests of justice, recognize an otherwise-excluded claim.

The caps, under Lawyers' Fund Rules and Regulations (LFRR) Rule 6.2, are $100,000 per
claimant and $300,000 in the aggregate for claims arising from any one attorney's conduct.
Awards are entirely discretionary; no claimant has a legal right to payment from the Fund.

The filing deadline, under LFRR Rule 6.1, is 5 years after the claimant knew or should have
known of the lawyer's dishonest conduct, or 2 years after the qualifying event described
above, whichever is later. The trustees may, in their discretion, consider a late claim for
good cause shown.

## 6. Small claims court

Hawaii's small claims division sits inside the district courts. Its jurisdiction, under
Hawaii Revised Statutes (HRS) section 633-27(a), is confined to cases for the recovery of
money only where the amount claimed does not exceed $5,000, exclusive of interest and
costs (with narrow exceptions for landlord-tenant security deposit cases and small
lease/rental property disputes, which do not apply to a fee dispute with your lawyer). This
kit found no county or circuit-level variation in that dollar figure; the $5,000 limit is
set once, statewide, by statute.

Under HRS section 633-28(b), any person may appear in small claims with the court's
approval, and licensed practitioners (lawyers) are not barred from appearing except in the
landlord-tenant security deposit carve-out. An unlicensed person appearing under this
section may not be paid for the representation.

Now the unusual part, and the reason this section needs to be read carefully. Hawaii has
no right of appeal from a small claims judgment, full stop. HRS section 633-28(a) states it
directly: "There shall be no appeal from a judgment of the small claims division." The
Rules of the Small Claims Division of the District Courts (RSCD), Rule 12(b), repeats it:
"A party may not appeal from a judgment of the small claims division." A 1978 Hawaii
Supreme Court case cited in the official annotations to section 633-28 describes the right
to appeal as "purely statutory" and confirms none exists here, and that mandamus cannot be
used as a substitute for an appeal.

The only post-judgment relief inside the small claims division itself is a motion to alter
or set aside the judgment under RSCD Rule 12(a), which must be filed within 10 days after
entry of judgment, or the court may grant relief for failure to receive prompt notice of
the judgment. This is not an appeal, not a trial de novo, and not a review by a higher
court. It is a request to the same small claims court to reconsider. If you lose in small
claims against your lawyer over a fee dispute, that loss is close to final; there is no
true cost or deadline to weigh for an "appeal" the way there would be in most other states,
because the option does not exist here.

The fee for filing, trial, judgment, and satisfaction in a small claims action is capped at
$35 by HRS section 633-29; other fees may be set by statute or court rule, and the judge
may waive prepayment or accrued costs on a sworn showing of inability to pay.

No small claims rule specific to suing a lawyer was found; a fee dispute with your lawyer
is litigated the same way as any other small claims case for money.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A fee agreement dispute is usually a contract claim. HRS section 657-1 sets a six-year
limitation period for "actions for the recovery of any debt founded upon any contract,
obligation, or liability," with no separate, shorter period stated for an oral contract as
opposed to a written one; the six-year period in section 657-1(1) applies either way, and
section 657-1(4) sets the same six years as a catch-all for personal actions not otherwise
covered.

Hawaii has no separate statute naming a legal malpractice limitation period. HRS section
657-7 sets a two-year period for "actions for the recovery of compensation for damage or
injury to persons or property," Hawaii's general tort limitation statute. The official case
notes attached to this statute describe a discovery rule for tort claims generally: the
period begins to run "the moment person discovers or should have discovered the negligent
act, the damage, and the causal connection." This kit did not independently confirm a
Hawaii court decision applying section 657-7, or that discovery language, specifically to a
legal malpractice claim, so treat the two-year tort period as the outer edge to plan
around, not a confirmed, malpractice-specific rule.

No statute of repose applicable to a general tort or contract claim against a lawyer was
found in HRS chapter 657; the chapter's one repose-style provision, section 657-8, is
written for claims about construction defects to real property, not legal services.

Whether a claim against your lawyer sounds in contract, in tort, or either at your
election, was not independently confirmed by this kit against Hawaii case law. Get advice
quickly if you suspect the underlying problem might be malpractice rather than a billing
disagreement, since that changes which clock applies and this kit does not cover running a
malpractice claim.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

Hawaii's Rules of Professional Conduct carry the same Scope-section language as the ABA
model rule. Paragraph [7] of the HRPC's Scope section (the note preceding the numbered
rules, not the separate, similarly titled section in Hawaii's Code of Judicial Conduct)
states that "violation of a Rule should not itself give rise to a cause of action against a
lawyer nor should it create any presumption in such a case that a legal duty has been
breached," and that the rules "are not designed to be a basis for civil liability." The
same paragraph adds that, because the rules do establish standards of conduct, "a lawyer's
violation of a Rule may be evidence of breach of the applicable standard of conduct" in a
case that is otherwise a recognized claim, such as malpractice.

In practical terms, a rule-violation complaint against your Hawaii lawyer does not by
itself put money in your hands. Your actual money routes are the ones covered above: a
contract claim over the fee itself under section 7, the Lawyers' Fund under section 5 if
the conduct meets its dishonest-conduct definition, the discretionary restitution power in
a disciplinary case under section 4, or a separate malpractice claim if that applies to
you, which is outside what this kit covers.

State Judiciary's Rules of Court page at courts.state.hi.us for the current HRPC, RSCH, RDB,
