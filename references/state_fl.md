# Florida

This pack is for a person in Florida who has a fee, billing, communication, or file-return
dispute with their own lawyer. It does not cover legal malpractice as a claim to run yourself.
Retrieved 2026-08-20.

What a Florida reader most needs to know, that a reader in another state does not:

- Florida runs a statewide Fee Arbitration Program through The Florida Bar, but it is entirely
  voluntary. Both the lawyer and the client must sign a written agreement to arbitrate. The Bar
  cannot force a lawyer into it just because you ask.
- Florida numbers its Rules of Professional Conduct with a "4-" prefix. The fee rule is Rule
  4-1.5, not Rule 1.5. Searching for the bare model rule number will not find it.
- Rule 4-1.5 splits its reasonableness test in two: eight factors for the fee itself, and a
  separate six-factor test for costs. The cost-specific test does not exist in the ABA model
  rule at all.
- Florida caps contingent fees by rule on a sliding scale in personal injury, wrongful death,
  and products liability matters, and by a separate provision of the Florida Constitution in
  medical liability matters. Both are numeric, both are checkable, and both are described in
  Section 2.
- Florida's small claims dollar limit ($8,000) is much lower than, and separate from, the
  general county court civil jurisdiction limit ($50,000). Do not confuse the two when deciding
  where to file.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct, numbering and adoption

Florida's Rules of Professional Conduct are Chapter 4 of the Rules Regulating The Florida Bar.
The Florida Supreme Court holds exclusive authority over admission and regulation of attorneys,
a judicial function assigned to that court by Fla. Stat. § 454.021, and the Court adopts and
amends these rules by its own orders, which appear at the end of each rule as amendment history
(for example, "Amended: Nov. 14, 1991 ... (593 So.2d 1035)" citing the Southern Reporter).

Cite a rule as "Rule 4-1.5" or, in a more formal citation, "R. Regulating Fla. Bar 4-1.5," not
"Model Rule 1.5." Florida renumbers every rule with a chapter-4 prefix: the fee rule a reader
would expect at 1.5 is 4-1.5, communication is 4-1.4, safekeeping property is 4-1.15, and
declining or terminating representation is 4-1.16. A reader searching for the bare model number
will not find Florida's version.

The official publication point is The Florida Bar's own compiled edition of the
court-adopted rules, at <https://www.floridabar.org/rules/rrtfb/>, which links to a separate
PDF for each chapter. The Chapter 4 PDF retrieved for this pack is stamped "RRTFB June 15, 2026,"
meaning it reflects amendments through that date. flcourts.gov itself is reachable, and its
opinions adopting and amending these rules were read directly for this pack, but some of its
own document-download links redirect to an internal court login page rather than the file, a
site quirk rather than a full block. Where that happened, the floridabar.org compiled edition,
or a Florida circuit court's own copy of the court-adopted rules, was used instead. See
[verification_fl.md](verification_fl.md).

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

Rule 4-1.5(b)(1) lists eight factors for a reasonable fee that track the ABA model list closely:
time and labor, novelty and difficulty, the skill required; whether taking the case precluded
other work; the customary local fee; the amount involved and results obtained; time limitations
imposed by the client or circumstances; the nature and length of the relationship; the lawyer's
experience, reputation, and ability; and whether the fee is fixed or contingent.

Florida then adds something the model rule does not have at all: a separate six-factor test for
costs, at Rule 4-1.5(b)(2). It asks about the disclosure made to the client about costs, any
specific agreement on how costs are calculated, the actual amount third-party providers charged
the lawyer, whether specific costs can be identified and allocated to the client, reasonable
charges for in-house services billed as costs, and the past course of dealing between lawyer
and client. If your dispute is really about costs charged on top of the fee, rather than the fee
itself, this is the test that applies, and a written cost agreement is presumed reasonable under
it.

Rule 4-1.5(e) requires the basis or rate of a fee to be communicated to the client, preferably
in writing, before or within a reasonable time after the representation begins. A fee that is
nonrefundable in any part must be confirmed in writing, explaining the intent of the parties as
to the nature and amount of that nonrefundable portion.

Contingent fees have their own set of Florida-specific rules that go well beyond the model rule:

- Every contingent fee agreement must be in writing, must state the percentage the lawyer
  receives at settlement, trial, and appeal, and must state whether costs are deducted before
  or after the fee is calculated. At the end of the matter the lawyer must give the client a
  written closing statement showing the outcome and how any remittance was calculated. Rule
  4-1.5(f)(1).
- In a personal injury, property damage, wrongful death, or products liability matter, Rule
  4-1.5(f)(4)(B)(i) sets a sliding-scale cap tied to when the fee was earned in the litigation:
  before an answer is filed, up to 33 1/3% of the first $1 million, declining above that; after
  an answer is filed, up to 40% of the first $1 million, declining above that; an additional 5%
  is allowed if the matter goes to appeal. A fee above these percentages is presumed clearly
  excessive unless a court approves it in advance.
- In a medical liability matter, Article I, Section 26 of the Florida Constitution guarantees
  the client no less than 70% of the first $250,000 recovered and 90% of anything above that,
  net of costs, in a contingent fee case. A client can waive this right, but only in writing,
  under oath, after specific written and oral disclosures the lawyer must make under Rule
  4-1.5(f)(4)(B)(iii). If you signed a contingent fee agreement in a medical liability case and
  never saw a waiver like this, the constitutional percentages likely still apply.
- The lawyer must keep the written fee contract and closing statement for six years after the
  closing statement is signed, and must make them available for the client's inspection at
  reasonable times. Rule 4-1.5(f)(5). If you never received a closing statement, you can ask for
  one and for a copy under this record-keeping duty.

Rule 4-1.5(i) separately requires that if a lawyer's own fee agreement contains a clause making
arbitration of fee disputes mandatory, the lawyer must first advise the prospective client in
writing to consider independent legal advice, and the agreement must carry a bold-print notice
that signing gives up the right to go to court over a fee dispute. This is not the same thing as
the Bar's own Fee Arbitration Program in Section 3, which is voluntary regardless of what your
fee agreement says; it only applies to a mandatory arbitration clause the lawyer put in your
contract.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

Florida runs one statewide program: the Fee Arbitration Program administered by The Florida
Bar's standing committee on Grievance Mediation and Fee Arbitration, out of the Bar's
Tallahassee office, under Chapter 14 of the Rules Regulating The Florida Bar and a set of Fee
Arbitration Procedural Rules the committee maintains. There is no separate local or county-level
program; this is the only one.

Participation is voluntary on both sides. The program has jurisdiction only where the parties
have agreed to arbitrate, by a written contract, by a signed request for arbitration, or by
court or disciplinary order. A party who has consented may withdraw that consent unilaterally
at any time before the Bar receives the other side's written consent; once both sides have
consented, withdrawal requires mutual agreement. There is no mechanism that lets a client force
an unwilling lawyer into the program, and no mechanism that lets a lawyer force an unwilling
client into it either. This is the single most important fact in this section: unlike some
states, Florida does not make arbitration mandatory on the lawyer just because the client asks.

Jurisdiction does not turn on a dollar ceiling or floor. It turns on there being no genuine
factual dispute other than the amount of, or entitlement to, fees or costs, and on all parties
estimating the evidence can be heard in eight hours or less. A $15,000 threshold appears in the
rules, but only to decide whether a single arbitrator or a three-member panel (at least one
lawyer, one non-lawyer) hears the case, not whether the program will take it at all.

No filing deadline was found in Chapter 14 or the Procedural Rules. There is no fee to file; the
Bar's own consumer guide states arbitrator services carry no charge. The hearing must be held
within 60 days of the arbitrator's or panel's assignment, and an award issued within 10 days
after the hearing closes.

The award is binding on both sides once both have agreed to arbitrate, and it may be confirmed,
modified, or set aside only as Florida's arbitration statute, chapter 682, Florida Statutes,
allows. There is no automatic right to a trial afterward; by agreeing to arbitrate you generally
give that up. If a lawyer fails to pay an award within 30 days without just cause, that lawyer
becomes delinquent and ineligible to practice.

No rule was found requiring a lawyer to notify a client of the right to use this program before
suing the client for fees. Do not confuse this with Rule 4-1.5(i) in Section 2, which is a
narrower notice tied only to a lawyer's own mandatory-arbitration clause in a fee agreement, not
a general precondition to a fee lawsuit.

Separately, The Florida Bar's Attorney Consumer Assistance Program (ACAP) offers Grievance
Mediation as another free, voluntary option, using a neutral mediator instead of a binding
arbitrator. ACAP frames both arbitration and mediation as alternatives to filing a formal
disciplinary complaint for a billing disagreement; see Section 4 for how that choice works and
why it matters.

## 4. Bar complaint process

The Florida Bar's Attorney Consumer Assistance Program (ACAP) is the intake point for a client
with a problem, at 866-352-0707. ACAP's own page lists a lack of communication and a failure to
return client papers or property as examples of what it can help with, and says the Grievance
Mediation and Fee Arbitration programs may be able to resolve a fee dispute or other issue with
a lawyer without filing a disciplinary complaint. ACAP is the place to call before deciding
whether to file a formal complaint, use fee arbitration, or use grievance mediation
(850-561-3166 for the Fee Arbitration Program's assistant).

Filing a formal disciplinary complaint is a separate track, handled through the Bar's grievance
process. If a disciplinary file involves only a fee or cost dispute and the parties sign an
agreement to arbitrate, the disciplinary file is closed without any finding of misconduct. Rule
14-5.2(a). In practice, a pure billing disagreement routed into the formal complaint process
tends to end up redirected into arbitration or closed, not turned into a refund.

No deadline for filing a formal disciplinary complaint was confirmed for this pack; see the gap
list in [verification_fl.md](verification_fl.md).

Discipline is not a refund. It is a separate process aimed at the lawyer's fitness to practice,
not at returning your money. Getting money back runs through the other forums in this pack: a
contract claim, fee arbitration, or the Clients' Security Fund (Section 5).

Rule 4-1.4 requires a lawyer to promptly inform the client of decisions needing the client's
consent, reasonably consult about how the client's objectives will be pursued, keep the client
reasonably informed of status, promptly comply with reasonable requests for information, and
explain a matter enough for the client to make informed decisions. Many complaints that feel
like a billing problem start here: no returned calls, no status updates, then a surprise
invoice.

Rule 4-1.16(d) requires a lawyer, on discharge or termination, to take steps reasonably
practicable to protect the client's interests: giving reasonable notice, allowing time to find
other counsel, surrendering papers and property the client is entitled to, and refunding any
advance payment of a fee or expense that was not earned or incurred. If a lawyer will not return
your file or refund an unearned advance fee after you have discharged them, this rule is what to
cite in your written request, before escalating to ACAP.

## 5. Client protection fund

The Clients' Security Fund of The Florida Bar, administered under Chapter 7 of the Rules
Regulating The Florida Bar, reimburses certain losses caused by a Florida lawyer's dishonest
conduct. Details and a claim form are at <https://www.floridabar.org/public/csf/>. It is not a
fee dispute forum and not malpractice insurance.

What it covers, called a "reimbursable loss": misappropriation, embezzlement, or other wrongful
taking or conversion of money or property by a Florida Bar member acting as a lawyer, in a
fiduciary role tied to the representation, or by a colleague in the same firm or a nonlawyer
employee. A "fee claim," where fees were paid and no useful services were provided, is treated
as its own category, capped at $5,000 per claim. A "misappropriation claim" is capped at
$250,000 per claim.

What it excludes: the fund's own definition of a reimbursable loss requires dishonest taking,
not simple negligence or an ordinary disagreement over whether a bill was fair. An ordinary fee
dispute, where the lawyer did the work and the argument is about the amount, is not what this
fund is for.

Payment is discretionary. There is no right to payment, and a partial or full payment on one
claim creates no right for another claimant. Rule 7-2.3(a), (c).

Prerequisites: the lawyer generally must no longer be a member in good standing, meaning
disbarred, revoked, deceased, or under a rehabilitative suspension, unless the theft was
committed by a nonlawyer employee, in which case a claim can proceed even if the lawyer remains
in good standing. Rule 7-2.4(a). Filing a grievance complaint against the lawyer may be required
as a prerequisite to the Fund even considering your claim. Rule 7-2.4(b). Claimants are expected
to reasonably exhaust other remedies first, such as bonds, malpractice insurance, or a law
firm's other partners, before the Fund will pay. Rule 7-2.4(d).

Deadline: a claim will not be considered unless filed within 3 years of the date the disciplinary
action against the lawyer becomes final, or within 3 years of the lawyer's death if the claim is
against a deceased lawyer. Rule 7-2.4(c). File the grievance complaint promptly if one is
required, since the 3-year clock does not wait for that process to finish.

## 6. Small claims or equivalent court

Florida's small claims equivalent is the small claims division of county court, governed by the
Florida Small Claims Rules ("Fla. Sm. Cl. R."), designed for a simple, speedy, and inexpensive
trial without a lawyer if you choose to go without one.

The small claims jurisdictional limit is $8,000, exclusive of costs, interest, and attorney's
fees, under Rule 7.010(b), raised from $5,000 effective January 1, 2020. This is separate from,
and much lower than, the general county court civil jurisdiction limit of $50,000 (effective
January 1, 2023) under Fla. Stat. § 34.01; a claim above the small claims limit still goes to
county court, just under regular civil procedure rather than the simplified small claims
process. No county-level variation in the $8,000 small claims figure was found.

Either side may be represented by a lawyer, or may appear without one. A corporation or other
business entity is not required to hire a lawyer for small claims: it may be represented by an
officer, a principal with authority to bind the entity, or an employee authorized in writing by
one. Rule 7.050(a)(2). This is an exception to Florida's general rule that a corporation must
appear through counsel, and it was confirmed against the current, in-force text of the rule;
see [verification_fl.md](verification_fl.md).

An unsuccessful party may appeal a small claims judgment to circuit court. Whether that appeal
is on the existing record or a new trial was not confirmed against a primary source for this
pack; see the gap list. No filing fee figure for small claims was confirmed as a statewide
number; confirm the current fee with the clerk in the county where you file, since it is set
per claim amount and by county. No rule specific to suing a lawyer in small claims was found.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A fee agreement dispute, such as a lawyer's failure to refund an unearned fee, is a contract
claim. Florida's limitation period is five years for an action on a written contract, Fla. Stat.
§ 95.11(2)(b), and four years for an action on a contract not founded on a written instrument,
Fla. Stat. § 95.11(3)(j). Under Fla. Stat. § 95.031, the clock generally starts when the last
element of the claim occurs, which for a contract claim is ordinarily the breach itself, not
when you discovered it.

Legal malpractice, a claim that the lawyer's own negligence caused you harm, needs its own
counsel rather than self-help. It is included here only so you know a clock exists and can get
counsel in time. Florida sets a single two-year period at Fla. Stat. § 95.11(5)(b), running from
when the malpractice was discovered or reasonably should have been discovered with due
diligence, and it applies the same way "whether founded on contract or tort," meaning how you
characterize the claim does not change the two-year clock. The statute limits this period to
persons in privity with the lawyer. No statute of repose specific to legal malpractice was found
in § 95.11 or § 95.031. Florida sets a repose period for medical malpractice, but none was
located for legal malpractice. That is what the search found, not a finding that no outer limit
exists, so ask a lawyer rather than reading it as extra time.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

The Rules of Professional Conduct are a disciplinary code, not a source of civil claims. The
Chapter 4 preamble says so directly. A violation of a rule should not by itself give rise to a
cause of action against a lawyer, should not create any presumption that a legal duty was
breached, and the rules are not designed to be a basis for civil liability. The preamble adds
one qualification worth knowing: a lawyer's violation of a rule may be evidence of a breach of
the applicable standard of conduct. A rule violation cannot be your claim by itself, but it is
not irrelevant either.

Your actual money route runs through the other forums in this pack: a contract claim in small
claims or county court (Section 6), the Fee Arbitration Program (Section 3), or the Clients'
Security Fund where the conduct was dishonest rather than merely a bad bill (Section 5). Getting
the lawyer disciplined (Section 4) is a separate goal from getting money back, and it runs
through a separate process.
