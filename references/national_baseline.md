# The national baseline: ABA Model Rules of Professional Conduct

Every state pack in this kit sits on top of this file. Read this first, then read your
state's pack. Retrieval date for the sourcing in this file is 2026-08-20, except the
contingent-fee dispute-practice guidance in section 4 and the lawyer-unavailable guidance in
section 10, both added 2026-08-21.

Read [verification_national.md](verification_national.md) before quoting any of this in a
letter. Confirm the current text of any rule you rely on against your own state's official
rules, because your state is what actually governs your lawyer, not this file.

## 1. What the Model Rules are, and what they are not

The American Bar Association adopted the Model Rules of Professional Conduct through its
House of Delegates in 1983. They are a model. The ABA is a private professional association,
not a legislature, a court, or a regulator. The Model Rules are not law anywhere by
themselves.

A rule becomes law for a lawyer only when that lawyer's own state adopts it, usually through
that state's supreme court or an equivalent rule-making body. Nearly every state has adopted
some version of the Model Rules, but each state writes its own version, numbers it its own
way, and departs from the model text wherever it chooses. Two states can share the same rule
number and mean different things by it.

That is why this kit does not stop here. This file describes the model shape. Your state pack
describes what your state actually did with it. If you cite "Rule 1.5" in a letter to your
lawyer, cite your own state's version, not this one.

## 2. Model Rule 1.5(a): the reasonableness requirement

The rule says a lawyer shall not make an agreement for, charge, or collect an unreasonable
fee or an unreasonable amount for expenses. Read that verb list again: agreement, charge, and
collect are three separate acts, and any one of them can violate the rule on its own. A fee
that looked reasonable when quoted can still become a problem in how it was billed or how it
was taken from a retainer. The reasonableness requirement follows the money the whole way,
not just at the moment the client signed.

The Model Rule lists eight factors relevant to whether a fee is reasonable. They are not
exclusive, and not every factor applies to every case. In your own words, they are:

1. The work the matter actually required: the time, the labor, how novel or difficult the
   legal questions were, and the skill needed to handle them properly.
2. Whether taking this case visibly cost the lawyer other work they could have taken instead.
3. What lawyers in the area customarily charge for similar work.
4. How much was at stake and what the lawyer actually achieved.
5. Any time pressure the client or the circumstances put on the matter.
6. How long and how the lawyer and client have worked together.
7. The lawyer's experience, reputation, and ability.
8. Whether the fee is a flat or hourly charge, or contingent on the outcome.

Read together, these factors ask one practical question: does the bill match what actually
happened in the case. A bill can be resisted on any of the eight grounds. A generic
"reasonable in my judgment" from the lawyer is not an answer to a factor-by-factor challenge.

Many states add factors beyond these eight, or reword them, or add a writing requirement that
the model list does not have. That is exactly what your state pack's deviations section
covers.

Not every state kept the model's "unreasonable" wording for this standard. A substantial
number of states instead prohibit a "clearly excessive" fee, which reads as a materially
higher bar for a client to clear: the fee has to cross the line by more than a fair margin,
not merely sit on the high side of reasonable. This kit confirmed that wording, used for the
general fee standard rather than some narrower rule, in Alabama, Massachusetts, Michigan,
North Carolina, Ohio, and Oregon. Michigan, Ohio and Oregon go further and define the standard: a
fee is "clearly excessive" only if a lawyer of ordinary prudence, after review of the facts,
would be left with a definite and firm conviction that the fee is in excess of a reasonable
fee.

This is not a list of every state that uses this wording; it is only the states this kit
checked and confirmed. Read section 2 of your own state pack before you assume the
"unreasonable" standard above is the one that actually governs your lawyer.

## 3. Model Rule 1.5(b): communicating scope and fee basis

The scope of the representation and the basis or rate of the fee and expenses the client will
owe must be communicated to the client. The Model Rule prefers this in writing, and requires
it before the representation begins or within a reasonable time after it starts. There is one
exception: a client the lawyer has regularly represented before, being charged on the same
basis as before, does not need a fresh disclosure each time.

Any later change in the basis or rate of the fee or expenses must also be communicated to the
client. A rate increase you were never told about is not just a billing surprise. It is a
separate communication failure under this rule, on top of whatever reasonableness question it
raises under 1.5(a).

## 4. Model Rule 1.5(c) and (d): contingent fees

A contingent fee, one that depends on the outcome of the matter, is allowed except where
paragraph (d) or other law prohibits it. Where it is allowed, the agreement must be in
writing and signed by the client, and it must state:

1. How the fee is calculated, including the percentage or percentages that apply at
   settlement, at trial, and on appeal.
2. Which litigation and other expenses are deducted from the recovery.
3. Whether those expenses are deducted before or after the contingent fee itself is
   calculated.

The agreement must also clearly tell the client what expenses the client owes regardless of
who wins. At the end of the matter, the lawyer must give the client a written statement
showing the outcome and, if there was a recovery, the amount remitted to the client and how
that amount was calculated. If you never received that closing statement, you can ask for it
in writing. It is required.

Contingent fees are prohibited outright in two situations under the Model Rule:

1. Domestic relations matters, where the fee or its amount depends on securing a divorce, or
   on the amount of alimony, support, or a property settlement.
2. Representing a defendant in a criminal case.

Some states widen or narrow these two categories, and some states add dollar caps or sliding
scales for contingent fees in particular kinds of cases. That belongs in your state pack, not
here.

The reasonableness requirement in Model Rule 1.5(a) does not exempt a contingent fee. The
eight factors listed in section 2 above apply to a contingent fee the same as they apply to
any other, and the last of those eight factors names contingent fees directly: whether the
fee is fixed or contingent is itself one of the things weighed in judging whether it is
reasonable. Agreeing to a percentage up front does not put that percentage beyond challenge
later.

In practice, a contingent fee dispute usually does not look like an hourly one. There are no
time entries to argue over, so the fight is rarely about hours worked. It is almost always
about the arithmetic on the settlement or closing statement: which expenses were deducted,
whether they were deducted before or after the fee was taken out, whether every deduction is
documented, and whether the percentage applied matches what the agreement actually specifies
for that stage of the matter. Ask for the itemized calculation and documentation for each
expense listed, not just the total remitted to you. This kit has a template for the closing
statement itself, `templates/10_contingent_closing_statement_request.md`, for when you never
received one.

## 5. Model Rule 1.4: communication duties

A lawyer must, as a matter of course:

1. Promptly tell the client about any decision or circumstance that needs the client's
   informed consent.
2. Reasonably consult with the client about how to accomplish the client's objectives.
3. Keep the client reasonably informed about the status of the matter.
4. Promptly comply with reasonable requests for information.
5. Consult with the client about any limitation on what the lawyer can do, when the lawyer
   knows the client is expecting something the rules do not allow.

The lawyer must also explain a matter well enough that the client can actually make informed
decisions about it. In practice, most complaints that feel like a fee dispute start as a
communication problem: unanswered calls, no status updates, and then a surprise invoice. That
is one story, not three, and this rule is why it matters as its own claim, separate from
whether the fee itself was reasonable.

## 6. Model Rule 1.15: safekeeping property

Client funds and property that the lawyer holds in connection with a representation must be
kept separate from the lawyer's own funds and property. Fees and expenses a client pays in
advance go into a trust account and can only be withdrawn as they are actually earned or
incurred. When a lawyer receives funds or property a client has an interest in, the lawyer
must promptly notify the client and, on request, account for it.

This is a different and more serious problem than a billing disagreement. If a lawyer took a
retainer, did little or no work, and the money is not there to refund, that is a trust
account problem, and it belongs in front of your state's disciplinary regulator, not just in
a negotiation over the bill.

## 7. Model Rule 1.16(d): duties on discharge or withdrawal

You can fire your lawyer. When a representation ends, whether the lawyer withdraws or the
client discharges them, the lawyer must take reasonably practicable steps to protect the
client's interests. Under the Model Rule, that includes giving reasonable notice, allowing
time to hire new counsel, surrendering the papers and property the client is entitled to, and
refunding any advance payment of a fee or expense that was not earned or incurred.

States enumerate this differently. Some list more steps than the Model Rule does, and some
split "surrender the file" from "surrender other work product" as two separate duties. Count
the steps in your own state's version rather than relying on a count from this file. Whatever
the count, the two requests you can make in the same letter are the same everywhere: give me
my file, and refund what you did not earn.

## 8. What these rules are not

The Model Rules are a disciplinary code. Their own Scope section says so directly: a
violation of a rule should not by itself give rise to a cause of action against a lawyer, and
should not create any presumption that a legal duty was breached. The Scope section adds one
important qualifier: since the rules do establish standards of lawyer conduct, a violation
may be used as evidence of a breach of the applicable standard of conduct in a case that is
otherwise a recognized claim, such as a malpractice action.

In practice that means two separate things:

1. A rule violation supports a complaint to your state's disciplinary regulator, and it can
   inform a fee dispute or a fee arbitration. It is not, by itself, a claim for money damages.
2. States differ on whether a proven rule violation can be used as evidence of the standard
   of care in a legal malpractice case. This file does not count them, because that count was
   not sourced. Whether your own state allows it, and how, is a question for your state pack.
   This kit does not cover malpractice claims or strategy.

Getting money back is a contract question, a fee arbitration question, or a claim to your
state's client protection fund if the loss involves dishonest conduct. Getting the lawyer
disciplined is your regulator's question. Keep the two goals separate, because the forums
that handle them are separate.

## 9. How to use this file with your state pack

This file gives the shape every state built from. Your state pack gives the operative law:
your state's actual rule numbers, its own reasonableness factors, its fee arbitration
program, its bar complaint process, its client protection fund, its small claims court, and
its own statutes of limitation. Where the state pack and this file conflict, the state pack
wins, because it describes the version of the rule that actually applies to your lawyer.

If your state pack says a section "tracks the model rule with no deviation," that means the
outline above is a reasonable guide to what your state requires, subject to the sourcing that
pack cites. If it says otherwise, follow the state pack.

## 10. When the lawyer is gone: death, suspension, disbarment, or closed practice

Everything in section 7 above still applies when the representation ended because the lawyer
died, was suspended or disbarred, or otherwise stopped practicing, rather than because the
lawyer withdrew or the client fired them. Model Rule 1.16(d) is not conditioned on the reason
the representation ended. Its duties, taking reasonably practicable steps to protect the
client, including surrendering the client's papers and property and refunding any advance
payment of a fee or expense that was not earned, apply on termination however it happened. The
lawyer being gone does not make the file or the unearned money disappear with them; it moves
the duty to whoever now stands in the lawyer's place.

Model Rule 1.15 is why there should be something to recover. Client funds, including fees and
expenses paid in advance that have not yet been earned, must be kept in an account separate
from the lawyer's own funds, not treated as the lawyer's money once received. If that
separation was actually observed, a client's unearned advance is not part of the lawyer's own
estate or the assets a disciplinary proceeding reaches, and identifying it should not depend on
the lawyer personally being available to write a check.

The Model Rules address the case of a lawyer who plans ahead for their own absence, but only in
a limited way, and only for a sole practitioner. A comment to Model Rule 1.3 (the diligence
rule) says that to prevent client matters from being neglected if a sole practitioner dies or
becomes disabled, the duty of diligence may call for that lawyer to have arranged in advance
for another lawyer to review client files, notify clients, and decide whether any file needs
immediate protective action. That comment also points to a separate ABA model rule for lawyer
disciplinary enforcement that provides for a court to appoint a lawyer to inventory a missing or
incapacitated lawyer's files when no such arrangement was made. Whether your own state adopted
either of these, and what it actually does when a lawyer leaves practice with no succession plan
in place, is a question for your state pack, not this file. Model Rule 1.17 separately lets a
lawyer sell a law practice while notifying affected clients and preserving each client's right
to take their file elsewhere instead, but that rule governs a lawyer's voluntary sale of a
practice, not a death, suspension, or disbarment, and this kit does not rely on it for anything
beyond that background.

What this means practically is that you are very likely not dealing with your former lawyer
directly anymore. Depending on what happened, the file and any client funds could sit with a
successor lawyer, the lawyer's estate, a court-appointed custodian, or the state's disciplinary
or bar authority. The Model Rules do not set a procedure for sorting out which one it is; that
is left to each state, and it is exactly what section 4 of your state pack, the bar complaint
and disciplinary process, is for. Start there and ask who currently has custody of the file.

If the money truly cannot be recovered from the lawyer, whether because the lawyer is dead,
disbarred, or simply gone, look at section 5 of your state pack, the client protection fund.
These funds exist for exactly this situation: money a client is owed that cannot be collected
from the lawyer. A lawyer being dead or disciplined does not by itself qualify a claim; each
fund sets its own eligibility rules, and those rules belong in your state pack, not here.

The letter templates in this kit still work once you know who to send them to. `templates/03_file_request.md`
and `templates/02_unearned_fee_refund.md` can both be addressed to a successor lawyer, an
estate representative, or a court-appointed custodian instead of the original lawyer; the
underlying Model Rule 1.16(d) and 1.15 obligations described above do not depend on who is
currently holding the file. This kit does not cover probate procedure or how to make a claim
against a deceased lawyer's estate. If the file or funds are held by an estate and the
custodian will not respond to a letter, that is a probate question, and you may need your own
lawyer for it.
