# Nebraska

This pack is for someone in Nebraska in a dispute with their own lawyer over fees, billing,
communication, getting a file back, or an unearned advance. It does not cover malpractice
strategy. Retrieval date for everything in this pack is 2026-08-20. Read
[national_baseline.md](national_baseline.md) first. See
[verification_ne.md](verification_ne.md) for the source and retrieval date behind every claim
below.

What is different about Nebraska:

- Nebraska's rules use a chapter-article-rule numbering scheme, not "Rule 1.5." The fee rule is
  cited as Neb. Ct. R. section 3-501.5, not "Rule 1.5." See section 1.
- Nebraska adds a real deviation on fees: on a client's request, a lawyer must provide, without
  charge, an accounting of the fees and costs charged in the matter. See section 2.
- Whether a lawyer can hold your file for unpaid fees depends on Nebraska's retaining-lien
  statute, not the conduct rule alone. See section 2.
- The Nebraska State Bar Association runs a statewide fee arbitration program, but it is
  voluntary on both sides, not mandatory on the lawyer. Its jurisdiction runs from $500 to
  $25,000, and this kit did not find a filing deadline in the program's own rules. See
  section 3.
- Nebraska's small claims court, the Small Claims Division of county court, bars attorney
  representation of either party and caps a plaintiff at two filings a week and ten filings a
  year. An appeal costs more than the district court docket fee alone: a separate appeal bond
  is required on top of it. The appeal is not a new trial; the district court reviews the
  small claims record for error, it does not rehear the case from scratch. See section 6.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct, numbering and adoption

The Nebraska Supreme Court adopts and amends the state's lawyer conduct rules by court rule.
They are published as Chapter 3 of the Nebraska Court Rules, titled "Attorneys and the
Practice of Law." The Nebraska Rules of Professional Conduct sit in Article 5 of that chapter.

Nebraska does not cite its fee rule as "Rule 1.5." It uses a chapter-article.rule numbering
scheme: Chapter 3, Article 5's rule tracking Model Rule 1.5 is cited as section 3-501.5, and in
full citation form as "Neb. Ct. R. section 3-501.5." The pattern holds across Article 5:
Model Rule 1.1 is section 3-501.1, Model Rule 1.15 is section 3-501.15, Model Rule 1.16 is
section 3-501.16, Model Rule 2.1 is section 3-502.1, and Model Rule 8.1 is section 3-508.1. If
you search for "Rule 1.5" on a Nebraska court or bar site and find nothing, this numbering
scheme is why: search for "3-501.5" or "section 3-501.5" instead.

Caution: Nebraska's chapter-article-rule scheme is its own state's convention and is not the
plain "1.5" numbering used in some other states, and at least one other state in this kit
renumbered its rules outright in 2021. Do not assume a rule number carries over between
states. Confirm the current Nebraska rule number for anything you cite by checking the
Nebraska Supreme Court's own rules pages before you rely on it.

The official publication point is the Nebraska Supreme Court's own rules site:
<https://supremecourt.nebraska.gov/supreme-court-rules/chapter-3-attorneys-and-practice-law>,
with the Rules of Professional Conduct under its Article 5 subpage. This kit did not find a
posted "effective as of" or "last amended" date on the live rule pages for section 3-501.5,
unlike some states that post an effective-date banner. Confirm you are reading the current
text by checking the site directly; do not rely on a date this pack does not have.

Nebraska has one disciplinary body and a separate client-protection body. The Counsel for
Discipline of the Nebraska Supreme Court investigates and prosecutes conduct-rule violations.
The Client Assistance Fund, administered by the Nebraska State Bar Association subject to the
Nebraska Supreme Court's authority, is the body that can pay a client money for a lawyer's
dishonest conduct. These are two different offices with two different jobs; see sections 4
and 5.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

Nebraska's rule 3-501.5 tracks the Model Rule 1.5(a) reasonableness standard and its eight
factors as written; this kit did not find Nebraska adding, deleting, or materially rewording
any of the eight factors, based on a direct read of the current rule text.

Nebraska does add one fee-related duty the Model Rules do not have at all: on a client's
"reasonable and timely request," the lawyer must provide, without charge, an accounting for
fees and costs claimed or previously collected. This is section 3-501.5(f). The rule spells out
what the accounting must include at a minimum: an itemization of all hourly charges, costs,
interest assessments, and past-due balances, and, for hourly-rate charges, a description of the
services performed and who performed them, in enough detail to generally let the client
understand the work done. It gives a client a direct, rule-based right to ask for an itemized
accounting, rather than relying only on the general communication duties in Model Rule 1.4. If
your lawyer's bills have been vague, this is the specific rule to cite when you ask for an
itemization. The rule does not state how quickly the lawyer must respond once you ask; "timely"
is not defined with a number of days.

Nebraska's version of Rule 1.5(b) and 1.5(c), governing communicating the fee basis and
contingent fee agreements, tracks the model structure; this kit did not find a Nebraska-added
dollar threshold that triggers a written-fee-agreement requirement beyond what the model rule
already requires for contingent fees, and did not find a Nebraska statutory cap or sliding
scale on contingent fee percentages in personal injury or other private civil matters. Both of
those are gaps, not confirmed absences; see the gap list in verification_ne.md.

Can a Nebraska lawyer hold your file for unpaid fees. Rule 3-501.16(d), tracking Model Rule
1.16(d), requires a lawyer on termination to take steps to protect the client, including
surrendering papers and property the client is entitled to, "to the extent permitted by other
law." Nebraska's "other law" on this point is its attorney's lien statute, Neb. Rev. Stat.
section 7-108. The statute itself is short: it gives an attorney "a lien for a general balance
of compensation upon any papers of his client which have come into his possession in the
course of his professional employment," and a separate lien on money of the client's in the
attorney's own hands or in the hands of the adverse party once notice of the lien is given. A
1900 Nebraska Supreme Court decision construing this statute, Cones v. Brooks, 60 Neb. 698, 84
N.W. 85 (1900), describes it as giving a retaining lien on "all papers, books, documents and
money of client" that come into the attorney's possession, plus a charging lien on money held
by an adverse party. This kit did not find any statute or case stating that the lien is
released once the client posts security or furnishes a bond for the papers; that specific
mechanism, sometimes found in other states, is not confirmed for Nebraska and is not stated in
the pack. In practice this means a Nebraska lawyer can lawfully hold your papers for unpaid
fees, but the statute itself says nothing about how or whether a client can force an early
release before paying the balance, and this kit did not find a Nebraska ethics opinion or
appellate decision limiting the lien where withholding the file would prejudice the client's
active case, the way some other states' rules do. A client who needs the file back before the
fee dispute is resolved should move the fee dispute forward quickly through arbitration or the
bar's process rather than assume any specific release mechanism is available.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

The Nebraska State Bar Association runs a single statewide voluntary fee arbitration program,
described at <https://www.nebar.com/page/FeeArbitration>. It is not a court, and it is not run
by the Nebraska Supreme Court. It runs under the NSBA's own 2025 Fee Arbitration Rules, most
recently approved by the NSBA House of Delegates October 10, 2025, and under the procedural
framework of the Nebraska Uniform Arbitration Act, Neb. Rev. Stat. section 25-2601 et seq.

Participation is voluntary on both the client and the lawyer. This kit did not find a
provision making the program mandatory on the lawyer once the client requests it, unlike some
states that force the lawyer into the program while leaving the client free to skip it. Both
sides have to agree to arbitrate before the program has jurisdiction.

The program's jurisdiction runs from $500 to $25,000 in dispute. A fee dispute below $500 or
above $25,000 falls outside what this program will hear. There is a $10.00 filing fee, payable
to the NSBA, which the rules state is refunded if the lawyer (the respondent) does not consent
to arbitrate. This kit read the program's own rules of procedure document, not just the
summary page, and did not find a stated filing deadline or limitations window in those rules,
and did not find a requirement that the lawyer notify the client of the right to arbitrate
before suing for fees. Both of those are treated as confirmed absences in the rules text this
kit read, not as gaps; see verification_ne.md.

Once both sides consent, the award is binding, and it is enforceable and reviewable the way
any Nebraska arbitration award is: through the Nebraska Uniform Arbitration Act's own
vacate-or-confirm process, Neb. Rev. Stat. section 25-2613, not through an ordinary appeal.
There is no automatic right to a full trial after an award both sides agreed to be bound by.

## 4. Bar complaint process

Nebraska's disciplinary body is the Counsel for Discipline of the Nebraska Supreme Court. A
complaint can be filed as described at
<https://nebraskajudicial.gov/administration/professional-ethics/attorney-discipline-ethics/file>.
This kit did not find
Nebraska splitting an informal consumer-assistance intake from a formal complaint process the
way some states do; the page it read describes a single grievance process for reporting lawyer
misconduct.

This kit did not find a stated deadline for filing a grievance on this page.

What the process will and will not produce: the Counsel for Discipline investigates and can
pursue discipline against a lawyer, up to suspension or disbarment. It does not have the
authority to order a lawyer to refund money to a client; discipline is not a refund. This kit
did not find a restitution mechanism attached to the Nebraska disciplinary process itself.
Money-back routes in Nebraska run through fee arbitration (section 3), the Client Assistance
Fund for dishonest-conduct losses (section 5), or a direct legal claim.

## 5. Client protection fund

Nebraska's client protection fund is the Client Assistance Fund of the Nebraska State Bar
Association, established subject to the authority of the Nebraska Supreme Court. Its rules are
published by the NSBA; this kit read the Fund's own rules document rather than only the
summary page at <https://www.nebar.com/page/CAF>.

The Fund covers losses caused by the dishonest conduct of a Nebraska lawyer acting in a
professional capacity, meaning conduct such as theft or conversion of a client's money or
property. It excludes ordinary fee disputes and excludes losses from a lawyer's negligence
alone; a lawyer doing incompetent or negligent work, without dishonesty, is not what this fund
is for. It also excludes claims arising out of an employer-employee relationship, as opposed to
a lawyer-client relationship, and requires that the claimant have no other reasonable source of
reimbursement for the loss.

The Fund's rules set a cap of $100,000 for losses caused by dishonest acts of an individual
lawyer. This kit read the cap as written as a single combined ceiling tied to "dishonest act or
acts by an attorney," rather than as separate, independently stated per-claim and aggregate
figures the way some states structure their caps; this kit did not find a further-separated
per-claimant maximum distinct from that figure. If your loss is large, do not assume the full
$100,000 is available to you alone; confirm current board practice with the NSBA before relying
on any specific dollar recovery.

The Fund's rules set a one-year period from when the claimant discovered, or reasonably should
have discovered, the loss, to file a claim. The rules let the Board extend that one-year period
at its own discretion in unusual circumstances for good cause shown, so a late claim is not
automatically barred. This kit did not find a requirement that a disciplinary complaint be
pending or resolved first as a precondition to filing a Fund claim, though a Fund claim and a
disciplinary complaint commonly proceed together in practice. Awards under the Fund's rules are
discretionary, made from a fund of limited size, and are not a guarantee of full or even
partial payment.

## 6. Small claims or equivalent court

Nebraska's small claims court is the Small Claims Division of the County Court. The
jurisdictional limit is $7,500, set by Neb. Rev. Stat. section 25-2802.

Attorneys may not represent either party in a small claims proceeding in Nebraska; both the
plaintiff and the defendant must appear for themselves, under Neb. Rev. Stat. section
25-2803(2). This is a real restriction that a reader used to hiring counsel for a small case
should plan around: it applies to you and to the lawyer you are suing. The bar lifts on appeal;
either side may be represented by an attorney once the case reaches district court, under
section 25-2807.

A plaintiff is capped in how often they can use small claims court: no more than two claims a
week and no more than ten claims a year, under Neb. Rev. Stat. section 25-2803(6). This is a
per-plaintiff limit set at the state level, not something that varies case by case, though this
kit did not check whether individual counties have adopted differing local small claims
practices beyond the statute itself.

Either side can appeal a small claims judgment to the district court, under Neb. Rev. Stat.
section 25-2807 and the general county-court appeal procedure in sections 25-2728 through
25-2738. This is not a new trial. Section 25-2733 states that "the district court shall review
the case for error appearing on the record made in the county court," and a 2024 Nebraska
Supreme Court decision, Sedighi v. Schnackel Engineers, 317 Neb. 890, 12 N.W.3d 507 (2024),
confirms the district court reviews a small claims judgment on this same error-on-the-record
standard, not as a full rehearing. Do not expect to retry the facts or call new witnesses on
appeal. The full cost of that appeal is more than one number: a $42 district court docket fee
under Neb. Rev. Stat. section 33-106, plus a separate $50 appeal bond specifically required for
small claims appeals under Neb. Rev. Stat. section 25-2729(4). Total cost to appeal is at least
$92, not $42 alone, and the appeal must be filed within 30 days of the small claims judgment
under section 25-2729. Section 33-106 also references a separate $15 records management fee in
some cases; this kit could not confirm from the statute text alone whether that fee applies to
a small claims appeal, so confirm the exact total with the district court clerk before you
file. Confirm the current docket fee amount before you rely on it, because court fee schedules
change; these figures were read directly from the statutes at retrieval.

This kit did not find a Nebraska small claims rule written specifically for suing a lawyer,
beyond the general attorney-representation bar above; the general small claims process and
limits apply to a fee dispute the same as any other civil claim within the dollar limit.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

Nebraska's general written contract limitation period is five years, under Neb. Rev. Stat.
section 25-205. Nebraska's oral contract limitation period is four years, under Neb. Rev. Stat.
section 25-206. A fee agreement dispute is ordinarily a contract claim, so which period applies
depends on whether your fee agreement was written or oral.

Nebraska's professional negligence limitation period is two years from the act or omission, or,
if the claim is not and could not reasonably have been discovered within that two-year window,
one year from actual discovery or from discovery of facts that would reasonably lead to
discovery, whichever of those two discovery-related dates comes first. That structure works out
in practice to whichever period runs longer, under Neb. Rev. Stat. section 25-222. The statute
does not name attorneys by title; it applies by its own terms to "professional negligence" and
"failure to render professional services" generally. This kit confirmed, from the statute's own
official annotations, that Nebraska courts apply this section to legal malpractice claims,
citing Nichols v. Ach, 233 Neb. 634, 447 N.W.2d 220 (1989), so the coverage of attorneys rests
on judicial construction of the general professional-negligence language, not on a
lawyer-specific statute. Section 25-222 also states its own outer limit: "in no event may any
action be commenced... more than ten years after the date of rendering or failure to render
such professional service." Nebraska case law confirms this ten-year repose period is valid;
the statute's official annotations cite Williams v. Kingery Constr. Co., 225 Neb. 235, 404
N.W.2d 32 (1987), stating "the ten-year period of repose contained in this section is
constitutional." So there is a repose period, and it is ten years from the act or omission,
regardless of when the problem is discovered.

This kit did not find a confirmed answer, from a primary Nebraska source, on whether a legal
malpractice claim in Nebraska sounds in tort, in contract, or at the plaintiff's election. This
is left out of the pack rather than guessed. If this distinction matters to you, an actual
malpractice claim is outside what this kit covers; talk to a lawyer about which limitation
period and which theory applies to your situation.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

Nebraska's Rules of Professional Conduct carry a Scope section, confirmed as belonging to
Chapter 3, Article 5, the professional conduct rules, not the state's separate judicial conduct
code for judges. This kit read that Scope section directly.

The Scope section states that a violation of a rule does not itself give rise to a cause of
action, and does not create a presumption that a legal duty has been breached. It adds that
because the rules set standards for lawyer conduct, a rule violation may be evidence of a
breach of the applicable standard of conduct in an otherwise recognized claim, such as a
malpractice action. This tracks the Model Rules Scope language described in
national_baseline.md; this kit did not find Nebraska rewording or narrowing that language.

So a rules violation in Nebraska is not a lawsuit by itself. The money routes available to a
Nebraska client instead are: a contract claim over the fee itself, the NSBA's voluntary fee
arbitration program (section 3), the Client Assistance Fund for a loss caused by dishonest
conduct (section 5), and, where a court finds it appropriate in a fee-related proceeding,
denial or reduction of an unreasonable fee. This kit did not find a Nebraska-specific
fee-forfeiture doctrine beyond ordinary fee-reasonableness review under section 3-501.5, and
that is a gap, not a confirmed absence.
