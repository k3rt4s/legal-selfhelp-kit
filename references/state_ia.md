# Iowa: handling a dispute with your own lawyer

This section is for a person in Iowa who has a problem with their own lawyer over fees,
billing, communication, getting their file back, or an advance payment that was never
earned or never refunded. It does not cover legal malpractice claims or how to run one
yourself. Retrieved August 20, 2026.

What is different about Iowa:

- Iowa numbers its rules of professional conduct inside chapter 32 of the Iowa Court
  Rules, cited "Rule 32:1.5," with a colon between the chapter and the rule number, the
  same style Wisconsin uses but with a different chapter number. Read section 1 below.
- Iowa's client protection fund and its attorney discipline system are run by two
  different bodies: the Client Security Commission handles the fund, and the Attorney
  Disciplinary Board and Grievance Commission handle discipline. All three sit under the
  Iowa judicial branch's Office of Professional Regulation, but they are separate
  entities with separate rules. Read sections 4 and 5.
- Iowa has no organized statewide fee arbitration program. The closest thing is a
  referral to county bar association arbitration committees, and only one county's
  program, Polk County's, could be confirmed with published rules. Read section 3 before
  you assume a state program exists.
- Iowa's trust account rules, not its fee rule, are where the state departs from the
  model on advance fees: a lawyer may not label a special retainer nonrefundable. Read
  section 2.
- Iowa's client protection fund publishes real dollar caps: $100,000 per claimant and
  $300,000 total per dishonest attorney. Read section 5.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct: numbering and adoption

Iowa's rules of professional conduct for lawyers are organized as chapter 32 of the Iowa
Court Rules, adopted and amended by the Iowa Supreme Court. Cite them as "Rule 32:1.5,"
"Rule 32:1.4," and so on, with a colon separating the chapter number from the rule
number. That format looks unusual next to a bare "Rule 1.5," and it is worth getting
right, because a citation that drops the "32:" does not point to anything in Iowa's own
numbering. The chapter also opens with a Preamble and a Scope note that comes before
Rule 32:1.0, covered in section 8 below.

Numbering itself tracks the ABA model. A reader looking for 1.5, 1.4, 1.15, or 1.16 will
find them at those same numbers inside chapter 32.

The official publication point is the Iowa Legislature's own site, which hosts the
current Iowa Court Rules, including chapter 32, as a dated PDF. The version checked for
this pack is the one currently linked from the Iowa Court Rules listings page, carrying
a July 31, 2026 posting date. Start at
<https://www.legis.iowa.gov/law/courtRules/courtRulesListings> and follow the current
chapter 32 link rather than guessing at a URL, because this kit found at least one other
chapter (34, covered in section 4) where an older, un-dated link on the same site served
a stale version.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

Rule 32:1.5(a) lists the same eight reasonableness factors as the ABA model rule: time
and labor, the difficulty of the matter, the customary local fee, the amount involved and
results obtained, time limits imposed by the client or circumstances, the length of the
relationship, the lawyer's experience and reputation, and whether the fee is fixed or
contingent. Iowa does not add to or subtract from that list, and the rule text uses the
same "unreasonable" standard as the model rule, not a "clearly excessive" standard.
Separately, the Attorney Disciplinary Board's own public complaint brochure describes
the rule in looser terms, saying a lawyer's fee "must not be clearly excessive." That
phrase does not appear in Rule 32:1.5(a) itself. This kit did not find a court decision
resolving whether that is a real difference in what counts as a violation or just looser
language in a consumer brochure, so treat the rule text, "unreasonable," as controlling
and treat "clearly excessive" as the regulator's own paraphrase.

Iowa's real deviation from the model is not in Rule 32:1.5 at all. It is in a separate
set of rules, the Iowa Court Rules Client Trust Account Rules, chapter 45. Rule 45.9
defines several kinds of fee arrangements a lawyer can use, including a "special
retainer" (an advance payment for specific work, held in trust and drawn down as earned)
and a "general retainer" (a payment for availability, not tied to specific work, which
belongs to the lawyer once paid and does not go into trust). Rule 45.9(2) says directly
that a special retainer must not be labeled or treated as nonrefundable. If your written
fee agreement calls an advance payment a nonrefundable retainer, and that payment is
really a special retainer for specific work rather than a true general retainer for
availability, that label may itself violate Rule 45.9(2), separate from any question
about whether the amount charged was reasonable.

Chapter 45 also states the baseline rule most states carry in their conduct rules
directly: advance fees and expense payments that have not yet been earned or incurred
must be held in a client trust account, not the lawyer's own account, until they are
earned. Iowa places this trust-accounting mechanic in chapter 45 rather than folding it
into Rule 32:1.5 or Rule 32:1.15 the way some states do, so do not assume Rule 32:1.5
alone tells you everything Iowa requires about how an advance fee must be held.

The current chapter 45 text is published the same way as chapter 32, through the Iowa
Court Rules listings page, at
<https://www.legis.iowa.gov/law/courtRules/courtRulesListings>.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

Iowa does not run a statewide, organized attorney fee arbitration program. This kit
searched the Iowa judicial branch's Office of Professional Regulation pages, the Iowa
State Bar Association's public pages, and the Iowa Court Rules for a statewide program
and did not find one. The Iowa judicial branch's own Client Security Trust Fund page
tells a person with a dispute over the amount of a fee, as opposed to a loss from
dishonest conduct, that the disagreement "should be referred to the Iowa State Bar
Association for arbitration," and gives a phone number for the Iowa State Bar
Association's Fee Arbitration Service: (515) 243-3179. That is a referral line, not a
description of a single, published, statewide procedure, and this kit could not locate
a published set of statewide arbitration rules to go with it.

What this kit did confirm is a county-level program: the Polk County Bar Association
runs an Attorney Fee Arbitration committee with its own published procedures and rules.
It is voluntary for both the client and the lawyer. There is no fee to file. A panel of
three to five committee members hears the dispute informally, without a court reporter
or strict rules of evidence. The panel's decision becomes binding only once both sides
sign a stipulation agreeing to be bound by it; neither side is forced into that
stipulation. The committee will not take a case if a lawsuit over the same fee is
already pending in court, though it can take the case if that lawsuit is later dismissed
without prejudice. Polk County's own published procedures state the committee will hear
disputes involving parties from outside Polk County if both sides agree to that. An
arbitration award under this program is not itself a court judgment; collecting on it
requires reducing it to a judgment under Iowa's general arbitration statute, Iowa Code
chapter 679A.

Polk County's own rules for this program set a filing deadline of ten years from a
written fee agreement or five years from an oral one, tied to a discovery-style trigger
similar to a normal statute of limitations, rather than a short, program-specific
window. This kit did not confirm whether other county bar associations in Iowa run a
similar program with the same or different terms; the Polk County program is the one
this kit could confirm with published rules, and this list may be incomplete.

Because there is no statewide, mandatory program, do not assume you can force your
lawyer into fee arbitration in Iowa the way a reader in some other states can. If your
lawyer will not agree to arbitrate voluntarily, whether through a county bar committee
or otherwise, your money route runs through small claims or district court for the
amount in dispute, covered in section 6, or through the client protection fund in
section 5 if the lawyer's conduct went beyond a billing disagreement into dishonesty.

The Iowa judicial branch's Client Security Trust Fund page, which is the source for the
Iowa State Bar Association referral above, is at
<https://www.iowacourts.gov/opr/attorneys/attorney-discipline/client-security-trust-fund-claims>.

## 4. Bar complaint process

Iowa's attorney discipline system runs through the Attorney Disciplinary Board, which
investigates and prosecutes complaints, and the Grievance Commission, which functions
as the body that hears formal disciplinary charges, both created and governed under the
Iowa Court Rules and both operating within the Iowa judicial branch's Office of
Professional Regulation. The Board is created by Iowa Court Rule 34.6.

A person starts the process by filing a complaint with the Board, online or by mail; the
Board's own consumer brochure walks through the process and is the source for most of
this section. The Board can investigate, and where it finds cause, can pursue a public
disciplinary case that can result in a private admonition, a public reprimand, license
suspension, or license revocation. This kit did not find a published time limit on when a
complaint must be filed, on either the Board's complaint page or in its consumer
brochure; treat that as unconfirmed rather than assuming there is none.

The Board's own brochure states plainly what the process cannot do for you: it cannot
require a lawyer to refund money, it cannot resolve a dispute over the amount of a fee,
and it cannot sue a lawyer for careless work on your behalf. Filing a complaint is free,
and a person who files one in good faith is immune from a lawsuit over having filed it.
Say this plainly: discipline is usually not a refund. A complaint can result in a
finding of misconduct and a sanction against the lawyer's license, but getting your
money back is a separate track, covered in sections 3, 5, and 6.

There is a partial exception, and it is narrower than it may sound. Iowa Court Rule
34.25(1)(e) requires an attorney applying for reinstatement after a suspension of more
than 60 days to show that the Client Security Trust Fund of the Bar of Iowa has been
repaid in full for any claims the fund already paid out because of that attorney's
conduct, or that the client security commission has approved a repayment plan. Iowa
Court Rule 34.27(2)(e) states the same condition for an attorney seeking readmission
after revocation or disbarment. That is a possible condition of getting the lawyer's
license back, decided inside the reinstatement process, not a separate order requiring
the lawyer to pay you directly, and it only reaches money the client protection fund
covered in section 5 has already paid out, not a fee dispute or an unrefunded advance
you have not yet pursued through that fund. It also depends on the case reaching a
suspension of more than 60 days or a revocation; Iowa Court Rule 34.24, which covers
reinstatement from a shorter suspension, lets the client security commission object to
the reinstatement but does not list this fund-repayment condition by name.

The Board's own consumer brochure describing how to file, what the process can and
cannot do, and the immunity for filing in good faith, dated current as of February 2019,
is published at
<https://www.iowacourts.gov/static/media/cms/Ethics_Brochure_Feb_2019_EE4D5F9EEF18F.pdf>.

## 5. Client protection fund

Iowa's fund is administered by the Client Security Commission, a body separate from the
Attorney Disciplinary Board, governed by Iowa Court Rules chapters 39 and 40, and
operating within the same Office of Professional Regulation. The Commission has five
lawyer members and two non-lawyer Iowa resident members, each serving four-year terms
with a maximum of two terms.

The fund reimburses losses caused by a lawyer's dishonest conduct in the practice of
law, not by ordinary negligence, malpractice, or a disagreement over the amount of a
fee. A plain fee dispute, where the lawyer did the work and you disagree about how much
it should have cost, is not what this fund is for.

Chapter 40 states real dollar figures: a maximum of $100,000 per claimant and a maximum
of $300,000 total against any one dishonest attorney, both figures applying to claims
arising from attorney conduct occurring on or after January 1, 2014, following a
November 26, 2013 amendment. This kit found and read that regulation text directly,
rather than relying on a secondary description of it.

The Commission's own page describes the fund as covering losses without a fee to file
and without needing a lawyer to bring the claim. This kit did not find a published
deadline running from the date of the loss itself, or a stated discovery rule for when
that deadline would begin, on either the Commission's page or in chapter 40's text as
read for this pack; treat the filing timeline as unconfirmed rather than assuming there
is none. Awards are discretionary, not a matter of right, and this kit did not confirm
whether Iowa requires a disciplinary complaint to be on file first as a precondition to
a fund claim.

The Client Security Commission's own page, with a phone number and an email address for
questions, is published at
<https://www.iowacourts.gov/opr/about-opr/client-security-commission>.

## 6. Small claims court

Iowa's small claims cases are heard in the small claims division of Iowa district court,
under Iowa Code chapter 631, rather than in a separately named court.

The jurisdictional dollar limit for a small claims money-judgment action is $6,500 for
claims arising on or after July 1, 2018, and $5,000 for claims arising before that date,
under Iowa Code section 631.1. Unlike some states, Iowa does not set a separate, lower
dollar limit for a personal injury or tort claim; the same figure applies to a claim for
money owed, whatever its legal basis, subject to the specific categories chapter 631.1
lists (which include a few claim types, such as a challenge to a mechanic's lien filing
or a dispute over ownership of pawned goods, that carry no dollar cap at all).

A defendant, and by extension either party, may appear in person or through an attorney;
Iowa Code section 631.5 does not bar a lawyer from appearing in a small claims case. This
kit did not confirm whether a corporate party must appear through counsel specifically,
as opposed to through a non-lawyer representative, and did not find county-level
variation in the dollar limit itself; the limit in section 631.1 is a statewide figure.

Appeal from a small claims judgment is not a new trial. Under Iowa Code section 631.13,
a party has 20 days to file a notice of appeal and pay the required docket fee, and the
appeal is decided on the existing record by a different judge, not heard fresh. The
filing fee for a small claims action itself is $95 under Iowa Code section 631.6, plus a
$20 postage charge and any fee for serving a nonresident defendant; a small claims
appeal carries its own $195 fee under Iowa Code section 602.8105. This kit did not find
a rule specific to suing a lawyer in small claims, as opposed to any other defendant.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A dispute over what you owed your lawyer, or what your lawyer owed you back, under a fee
agreement is a contract claim. Iowa Code section 614.1(4) sets a five-year period for
actions on unwritten contracts and for other actions not otherwise provided for. Iowa
Code section 614.1(5) sets a ten-year period for actions on written contracts. Which
period applies to your own fee agreement depends on whether that agreement was written
or oral.

A legal malpractice claim is different from a fee dispute, and this kit does not cover
running one. For reference only: Iowa courts have held that a legal malpractice claim,
which sounds in negligence rather than contract, is also governed by the five-year
period in Iowa Code section 614.1(4), since the statute does not name attorney
malpractice in one of its own specific categories the way it names certain other
professions. This kit confirmed that period, and the discovery rule described next,
directly against an Iowa Court of Appeals opinion, not just a secondary summary. Under
that discovery rule, the five-year clock does not necessarily start at the moment of the
lawyer's error; it starts when the client discovered, or through reasonable diligence
should have discovered, the fact of the injury and its cause. This kit did not find a
separate statute of repose specific to legal malpractice claims in Iowa; chapter 614's
own repose provisions apply to product liability and improvements to real property, not
to legal malpractice, so no outer limit beyond the discovery-tolled five-year period is
stated here.

Do not treat any of this as legal advice about your own deadline. If you suspect
malpractice rather than a billing or communication problem, that is outside what this
kit covers, and you should get your own lawyer's advice quickly given how these periods
run.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

Iowa's rules of professional conduct carry a Preamble and Scope note before the numbered
rules begin, and that note says directly that a violation of a rule should not itself
give rise to a cause of action against a lawyer, and should not create a presumption
that a legal duty was breached. The note adds that the rules are not designed to be a
basis for civil liability, and that their purpose can be undermined if an opposing party
tries to use them as a procedural weapon in unrelated litigation. At the same time, the
same note says that because the rules do establish standards of conduct, a violation may
still be used as evidence of a breach of the applicable standard of conduct, which can
matter in a malpractice case even though the rule itself creates no separate claim.

In practical terms, a rule violation on its own does not give you a lawsuit against your
lawyer in Iowa. Your actual routes to money are the ones covered above: a contract claim
over the fee itself, a county bar arbitration committee if one will take the case, the
client protection fund if the conduct was dishonest in the sense described in section 5,
or a separate malpractice claim if that applies and you get advice on it, which is
outside this kit.
