# New York

This pack is for a person in New York who has a fee, billing, communication, or file-return
dispute with their own lawyer. It does not cover legal malpractice as a claim to run yourself.
Retrieved 2026-08-20. See [verification_ny.md](verification_ny.md) for the source and
retrieval date behind every claim below.

What a New York reader most needs to know, that a reader in another state does not:

- New York runs a genuine statewide fee arbitration program, 22 NYCRR Part 137. Arbitration is
  mandatory for the lawyer once the client requests it, for disputes between $1,000 and
  $100,000. The award becomes final and binding unless either side sues on the merits within
  30 days.
- If a lawyer wants to sue you for fees, the lawyer must first send you a formal Notice of
  Client's Right to Arbitrate. If you do not request arbitration within 30 days of that notice,
  you lose the right to arbitrate and the lawyer can sue. If the lawyer sues without ever
  sending that notice, the lawyer's own complaint has to say so, and that is a real defect in
  the lawyer's case.
- New York's disciplinary system is not one statewide office. It is split across four Appellate
  Division judicial departments, and the Second Department alone splits further into three
  separate grievance committees by county group. Which committee is yours depends on where your
  lawyer's law office is, not where you live.
- For any matter where the lawyer expects to charge $3,000 or more, New York requires a written
  letter of engagement or retainer agreement, and that letter must tell you about your Part 137
  arbitration right. If you never got one, that is itself worth raising.
- The Lawyers' Fund for Client Protection has a strict two-year clock from the loss or its
  discovery. Its own published materials say a claimant should also file a written complaint
  with the attorney grievance committee and the district attorney where the lawyer's office
  was, and that losses are not generally reimbursed until the lawyer has been disbarred. It
  flatly excludes fee disputes, malpractice, and neglect, no matter how the loss happened.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct, numbering and adoption

New York's Rules of Professional Conduct are adopted by the Appellate Division of the New York
State Supreme Court and published as Part 1200 of the Joint Rules of the Appellate Division, 22
NYCRR Part 1200. They became effective April 1, 2009. Cite them as "Rule" followed by the
number, for example "Rule 1.5," the same numbering a reader would expect from the ABA model
rules. New York did not renumber the rules a client is most likely to need: Rule 1.4
(communication), Rule 1.5 (fees), Rule 1.15 (safekeeping property), and Rule 1.16 (declining or
terminating representation) sit at the same numbers as the model rules.

The Appellate Division adopted the rule text itself but did not adopt the Preamble, Scope, and
Comments. Those are published by the New York State Bar Association as guidance, not as binding
rule text. This pack cites the NYSBA's own current edition of the compiled rules for both the
rule text and the Scope commentary.

The official publication point is the New York State Unified Court System, and PDF copies are
also posted by each Appellate Division department, for example
<https://www.nycourts.gov/ad3/agc/rules/22NYCRR-Part-1200.pdf>. The version checked is the New
York State Bar Association's compiled edition of the Rules of Professional Conduct, marked
"(2025)" on its cover.

Attorney discipline itself is governed by a separate part, the Rules for Attorney Disciplinary
Matters, 22 NYCRR Part 1240. That is the rule that defines what counts as professional
misconduct and how a grievance committee investigates it; Part 1200 defines the conduct
standard, Part 1240 defines the process.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

Rule 1.5(a) lists eight factors for whether a fee is excessive, and they track the ABA model
rule's eight factors without adding or dropping any. New York's departure from the model is not
in that factor list. It is in the rest of Rule 1.5, which carries several requirements the
model rule does not have:

Rule 1.5(b) requires the scope of representation and the basis or rate of the fee to be
communicated to the client before or within a reasonable time after the representation begins,
in writing where a statute or court rule requires it. Any later change in scope or rate must
also be communicated. That "in writing where required" cross-references the letter of
engagement rule below.

Rule 1.5(c) requires a contingent fee agreement to be in writing, stating the method for
determining the fee, the percentages on settlement, at trial, and on appeal, which expenses are
deducted from the recovery, and whether those expenses come out before or after the fee is
calculated. At the end of the matter the lawyer must give the client a writing showing the
outcome and, if there was a recovery, how the remittance to the client was calculated.

Rule 1.5(d) prohibits four things the model rule does not list together this way: a fee based
on fraudulent billing, and a nonrefundable retainer fee (a lawyer may still use a reasonable
minimum fee clause if it plainly defines when the fee is triggered and how it is calculated).
It also sets out detailed conditions for a contingent fee in a domestic relations matter: such a
fee is barred outright if payment or amount is tied to securing a divorce, custody, or
visitation, or is set by reference to maintenance, support, or property division; and even
where a contingent domestic relations fee is otherwise allowed, it requires a signed written
retainer agreement, and any security interest, confession of judgment, or lien in that
agreement needs advance client notice and a court's approval.

Rule 1.5(e) requires a lawyer in a domestic relations matter to give the client a Statement of
Client's Rights and Responsibilities at the initial conference, before the retainer is signed.

Rule 1.5(f) is the piece that does not exist in the model rules at all: it requires a lawyer to
resolve fee disputes by arbitration at the client's election, under the fee arbitration program
described in Section 3.

Separately, 22 NYCRR Part 1215 requires a written letter of engagement, or a signed retainer
agreement covering the same ground, in any matter where the lawyer expects to charge $3,000 or
more, effective March 4, 2002. It is not required when the lawyer will charge a regularly
represented client on the same basis as before for the same general kind of work, in a domestic
relations matter governed by its own Part 1400 rules, or where the lawyer is admitted elsewhere,
keeps no New York office, and does no material part of the work in New York. The letter must
cover the scope of services, the fee and billing practices, and the client's right to arbitrate
a fee dispute under Part 137.

New York also has a statutory sliding-scale cap on contingent fees in medical, dental, or
podiatric malpractice actions, Judiciary Law Section 474-a: 30 percent of the first $250,000
recovered, 25 percent of the next $250,000, 20 percent of the next $500,000, 15 percent of the
next $250,000, and 10 percent of anything above $1,250,000, computed on the net recovery after
deducting expert and investigative expenses. Judiciary Law Section 474 itself, which governs
attorney compensation generally, leaves the fee to agreement between lawyer and client and does
not restrain it by law, apart from a specific rule requiring court approval of a contingent fee
involving an infant's guardian. There is no comparable statutory percentage cap for an ordinary,
non-malpractice contingent fee such as a personal injury case; the commonly quoted one-third
figure for those cases is customary practice, not a rule.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

New York runs a genuine statewide program, the Fee Dispute Resolution Program under 22 NYCRR
Part 137, administered by the Chief Administrator of the Courts through local arbitral bodies
in each judicial district. It is not a patchwork of unrelated local committees; it is one set
of statewide rules carried out locally. Details:

- Coverage and dollar range. The program applies to a lawyer admitted in New York, in a civil
  matter where representation began on or after January 1, 2002. It does not apply where the
  amount in dispute is less than $1,000 or more than $100,000, unless the parties consent to
  arbitrate a larger or smaller amount.
- Mandatory on the lawyer. Once the client requests arbitration, it is mandatory for the
  attorney. A lawyer who does not participate without good cause can be referred to the
  grievance committee.
- Binding effect and de novo review. An award is final and binding unless a party aggrieved by
  it commences an action on the merits of the fee dispute in court within 30 days after the
  award is mailed. If no such action is filed within that window, the award becomes final.
  Parties can also agree in advance, on a board-prescribed form, to make the award final and
  binding with no right to that later court action.
- Filing deadline. The rule that matters most: if the lawyer wants to sue the client for a fee,
  the lawyer must first send the client a written Notice of Client's Right to Arbitrate, by
  certified mail or personal service. That notice must tell the client of the right to
  arbitrate and that the client has 30 days from receiving it to request arbitration. If the
  client does not request arbitration within those 30 days, the right lapses and the lawyer may
  sue. A lawyer who sues must allege in the complaint either that the client got this notice
  and did not act on it in time, or that the dispute otherwise falls outside the program.
- Notice as a precondition to the lawyer's own suit. This is the single most useful fact in this
  section. If a lawyer sues you for fees without ever sending the Notice of Client's Right to
  Arbitrate, and the dispute is one the program covers, that is a real problem with the
  lawyer's own case, not a technicality to ignore.
- Filing fee. An arbitral body may charge a filing fee, but the rule requires it to be
  reasonably related to the actual cost of running the program and not set high enough to
  discourage people from using it. No statewide dollar figure was confirmed; it is set locally.
- What falls outside the program entirely. Criminal representation; disputes under $1,000 or
  over $100,000 absent consent; claims involving substantial legal questions, including
  malpractice or misconduct; claims for damages beyond a fee adjustment; fees already fixed by
  statute, rule, or court order; disputes where no services have been rendered for more than two
  years, or more than twelve months have passed since the client's last payment, whichever is
  later; out-of-state lawyers with no New York office and no meaningful New York services; and
  requests filed by someone who is not the client or the client's legal representative.

Because this is a real statewide program with a mandatory-on-the-lawyer feature and a notice
precondition to the lawyer's own suit, it is usually the strongest and cheapest first move for a
covered New York fee dispute, ahead of both small claims court and a bar complaint.

## 4. Bar complaint process

New York's discipline system runs through the Appellate Division, split into four judicial
departments, and further split into eight separate grievance committees by county group; the
Second Department alone has three (for Richmond, Kings, and Queens counties; for Westchester,
Dutchess, Orange, Rockland, and Putnam counties; and for Nassau and Suffolk counties). You file
with the committee for the county where your lawyer's law office is located, not where you
live. If the lawyer has no New York office, the complaint may go to any committee and will be
transferred to the department where the lawyer was admitted. Each department publishes its own
address and contact information; the Unified Court System's public Directory of Registered
Attorneys lists each lawyer's business address and admitting department if you are unsure which
committee is yours.

You file by completing a Complaint Form and submitting it by mail or, in some departments,
electronically. A signature is required; unsigned complaints are not processed. Initial
processing typically takes about sixty days.

A committee's authority is to determine whether the lawyer violated the Rules of Professional
Conduct (22 NYCRR Part 1200), under the process set by the Rules for Attorney Disciplinary
Matters (22 NYCRR Part 1240). At least one department's own instructions say plainly that the
committee has limited authority over rude behavior, attorney-client disagreements, or a belief
that the lawyer did a poor job, and that its staff cannot give legal advice or help you pursue a
fee claim. Discipline is not a refund. It can result in sanctions against the lawyer, but
getting money back runs through a separate forum: fee arbitration (Section 3), the Client
Protection Fund where the conduct was dishonest (Section 5), or a contract claim in court
(Section 6).

No published statewide deadline for filing a disciplinary complaint was confirmed one way or
the other; see the gap list in verification_ny.md.

## 5. Client protection fund

New York's fund is the Lawyers' Fund for Client Protection, a state agency, at
<https://www.nylawfund.org>, phone (800) 442-3863 or (518) 434-1935. It reimburses losses
caused by a New York lawyer's dishonest conduct in the practice of law, up to a maximum of
$450,000 per client loss.

What it covers: dishonest conduct, meaning the misappropriation or willful misapplication of
money, securities, or property in the practice of law, and unlawful acts such as theft,
larceny, embezzlement, fraud, or conversion. Typical examples include theft from an estate or
trust and misapplied escrow funds in a real property closing.

What it excludes: by law, the Fund cannot consider claims alleging legal malpractice, neglect,
or fee disputes, and it cannot consider claims for out-of-pocket or consequential damages. A
bad bill or a slow, careless lawyer is not what this fund is for, even if it feels like your
money is simply gone.

Deadline: clients must apply within two years after the loss or after discovering the loss.

There is no application fee, and you do not need a lawyer to apply. The Fund's own FAQ says a
claimant should also file a written complaint about the same conduct with the attorney grievance
committee and the district attorney where the lawyer's office was, on top of the Fund
application itself. The FAQ also says the Fund does not generally reimburse a loss until the
lawyer has been disbarred, and cooperating with the official investigation matters. That can
mean a real wait between filing and payment. No published aggregate cap per individual lawyer,
separate from the $450,000 per-claim maximum, was confirmed; see the gap list.

## 6. Small claims or equivalent court

Within New York City, the relevant court is the New York City Civil Court's Small Claims Part.
Outside the city, the equivalent forums are City Court small claims parts and Town and Village
Justice Courts.

The New York City Civil Court Act sets the small claims jurisdictional limit at $10,000,
exclusive of interest and costs (CCA Section 1801). The filing fee is $15 for a claim of $1,000
or less and $20 for a claim over $1,000; a defendant filing a counterclaim pays $5 plus mailing
costs (CCA Section 1803). Dollar limits and fees outside New York City, in City Courts and Town
and Village Courts under the Uniform City Court Act and Uniform Justice Court Act, were not
independently confirmed for this pack and should be checked with the specific court; see the
gap list.

Either side may bring an attorney, but it is not required. A lawyer sued as a business entity,
for example through a law firm organized as a corporation, PLLC, or PC, may appear by an
attorney or by an authorized officer, director, or employee with authority to bind the entity in
a settlement or at trial (CCA Section 1809); the court can inquire into that person's authority.

Filing a small claims case waives the broader right to appeal that exists in an ordinary civil
action. A limited appeal survives: either party may still appeal on the ground that substantial
justice was not done between the parties according to the rules and principles of substantive
law (CCA Section 1807). There is no trial de novo on appeal.

No rule specific to suing a lawyer in small claims court was found beyond the general
entity-representation rule above.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A fee agreement dispute, such as a lawyer's failure to refund an unearned fee or a disagreement
over what a written agreement required, is generally a contract claim. New York's default
limitations period for a contract action, express or implied, is six years, at CPLR Section
213(2). The statute lists several carved-out exceptions for particular kinds of contracts
(residential rent overcharge claims, sales-of-goods claims under the UCC, and certain General
Business Law claims) that do not apply to an ordinary attorney fee agreement. The clock
generally runs from the breach.

Legal malpractice, a claim that the lawyer's own negligence caused you harm, needs its own
counsel rather than self-help. It is not covered further in this kit beyond the deadline,
included so you know a clock exists and can get counsel in time. New York applies a single
three-year period to malpractice claims other than medical, dental, or podiatric malpractice,
regardless of whether the claim is framed in contract or in tort, at CPLR Section 214(6). A
malpractice claim generally accrues, and the clock starts, when the malpractice is committed,
not when the client discovers it. New York's Court of Appeals recognizes a continuous
representation doctrine that tolls the clock only where there is a mutual understanding that
the same lawyer will keep working on the same specific matter the claim arises from, not merely
while a general lawyer-client relationship continues (McCoy v. Feinman, 99 N.Y.2d 295 (2002)).

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

New York's Rules of Professional Conduct are a disciplinary code. Comment 12 to the Scope
section states plainly that violation of a rule should not itself give rise to a cause of
action against a lawyer, and should not create any presumption that a legal duty was breached;
the rules are designed to structure discipline, not to provide a basis for civil liability. That
comment also states the flip side: because the rules do establish standards of conduct, a
lawyer's violation of a rule may be evidence of a breach of the applicable standard of conduct,
which matters if you ever do pursue a malpractice claim with your own counsel.

Note what Section 1 said about that source. The Appellate Division adopted the rule text but
not the Preamble, Scope, and Comments, so Comment 12 is the bar association's published
guidance rather than adopted rule text. This pack did not locate a New York statute or opinion
stating the same point in as many words, and does not claim one. See the gap list in
[verification_ny.md](verification_ny.md).

Your actual money route runs through the other forums in this pack instead: Part 137 fee
arbitration where the dispute qualifies (Section 3), a contract claim in small claims or another
court (Section 6), or the Client Protection Fund where the lawyer's conduct was dishonest rather
than merely a bad bill (Section 5). Getting the lawyer disciplined (Section 4) is a separate goal
from getting money back, and it runs through a separate forum.
