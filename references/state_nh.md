# New Hampshire

This pack is for a person in New Hampshire who has a fee, billing, communication, or
file-return dispute with their own lawyer. It does not cover legal malpractice as a claim to
run yourself. Retrieved 2026-08-20; see [verification_nh.md](verification_nh.md) for the
source and date behind every claim.

What a New Hampshire reader most needs to know, that a reader in another state does not:

- New Hampshire's Rules of Professional Conduct are published with two layers of commentary
  attached to each rule, an NHBA Ethics Committee Comment describing how New Hampshire departs
  from the ABA model, and a separate ABA Model Rule Comment. Both are part of the official
  published rule page.
- New Hampshire's attorney fee arbitration program, run by the NHBA Dispute Resolution
  Committee, is voluntary on both sides. A lawyer cannot be forced into binding arbitration;
  both parties must sign a consent form before it becomes binding.
- New Hampshire's Attorney Discipline Office has told the public directly, in its own FAQ, that
  it has no authority to order a lawyer to refund fees, even where it finds misconduct.
- New Hampshire's Ethics Committee has stated plainly that Rule 1.5 does not permit an
  absolutely non-refundable retainer, citing *Doherty's Case*, 142 N.H. 446 (1997).
- A small claims appeal in New Hampshire is not a new trial. It goes straight to the Supreme
  Court on the existing record, only on a question of law, findings of fact are final.
- New Hampshire's general personal-actions limitation period, which covers most fee and
  malpractice claims, is 3 years, unusually short next to states with a separate longer
  contract period.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct, numbering and adoption

New Hampshire's Rules of Professional Conduct are adopted by the New Hampshire Supreme Court
and published on the Judicial Branch's own site as the "New Hampshire Rules of Professional
Conduct," at
<https://www.courts.nh.gov/new-hampshire-rules-professional-conduct>. Cite them as "Rule"
followed by the number, for example "Rule 1.5," the same numbering a reader coming from the
ABA model rules would expect. Rule 1.4 (communication), Rule 1.5 (fees), Rule 1.15 (safekeeping
property), and Rule 1.16 (declining or terminating representation) all sit at the numbers the
model rules use; no renumbering was found, confirmed by the rule index and by each rule's own
URL slug (for example "rule-15-fees" and "rule-115-safekeeping-property").

Each rule's page carries the rule text, then an NHBA Ethics Committee Comment (provided by the
Ethics Committee of the New Hampshire Bar Association, describing how the New Hampshire version
differs from the ABA model), then a separate ABA Model Rule Comment. This two-comment structure
is itself part of the official publication and is unusual; most states publish only one comment
layer. The Ethics Committee Comments are dated to when the Court last received notice of an
update from the Ethics Committee, and the site notes the Court does not always receive notice
when the ABA's own comments change, so the ABA Comment layer can lag the current ABA text.

New Hampshire replaced the ABA Model Preamble and Scope entirely with its own "Statement of
Purpose," discussed further in Section 8. Because New Hampshire never adopted the ABA Preamble
and Scope in any form, there was no base text for the Ethics Committee to amend; it wrote a
freestanding purpose statement instead.

Live retrieval of courts.nh.gov returned an access-denied response to every direct fetch
attempted this session; the rule text in this pack was confirmed through Wayback Machine
snapshots of the same pages instead. See the flagged table in
[verification_nh.md](verification_nh.md) for exactly which pages this affected and the
snapshot dates used. If courts.nh.gov is reachable when you read this, confirm the current text
there directly; a blocked automated fetch is not evidence the page has moved or changed.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

New Hampshire's Rule 1.5(a) tracks the ABA model's eight reasonableness factors, but the
prohibition itself is broader than the model. The rule bars a lawyer from entering an agreement
for, charging, or collecting an "illegal or unreasonable fee," adding "illegal" to the model's
"unreasonable" standard. A fee that is unreasonable under the eight factors is not the only way
to violate this rule; a fee that is unreasonable under some other law is a separate, independent
violation.

Rule 1.5(f), governing division of a fee between lawyers not in the same firm, keeps the ABA
model's core conditions: the client must agree to the arrangement in writing, and the total fee
must be reasonable. Where New Hampshire departs from the model is narrower than it may first
appear: alongside the model's proportionality basis (the split reflects the work each lawyer
performed, or both lawyers assume joint responsibility for the representation), New Hampshire
adds a second, independent basis, a written agreement with the referring lawyer, so a referral
alone can support the split without the referring lawyer doing further work or taking on joint
responsibility, as long as the client consents in writing and the total fee stays reasonable.

Nonrefundable fees. The Ethics Committee Comment to Rule 1.15 states plainly that "Rule 1.5
does not permit a retainer for services that is absolutely non-refundable," because an
absolutely nonrefundable fee is inconsistent with the requirement that any fee remain
reasonable. It cites *Doherty's Case*, 142 N.H. 446 (1997), a bankruptcy-court-context case in
which the Supreme Court affirmed a finding that a lawyer violated Rule 1.15(a) by failing to
segregate a client's retainer into trust. The one recognized exception is a "general,"
"classic," or "engagement" retainer, paid for the lawyer's availability rather than for
specific work, which is treated as earned when paid because it is not compensation for services
rendered. A flat or minimum fee for actual legal work must still be held in trust and withdrawn
only as work of comparable value is performed.

File retention on termination. New Hampshire has no rule setting a minimum number of years a
lawyer must keep a former client's file. A 1997 proposal to require file retention for at least
six years, or the applicable statute of limitations period if longer, was never formally
adopted. The operative source is Rule 1.16(d)'s duty to take steps, on termination, that are
reasonably practicable to protect a client's interests, including surrendering papers and
property to which the client is entitled. This pack could not independently read Rule 1.16's own
text; courts.nh.gov never archived that page and live retrieval was blocked. Two other sources
confirm how New Hampshire applies that duty to a client's file. The New Hampshire Supreme Court,
in *Averill v. Cox*, 145 N.H. 328 (2000), addressed a client's right to a former lawyer's file on
termination. And the NHBA Ethics Committee's own formal opinion on the subject, Opinion 2015-16/05,
addresses what a file consists of and how long to keep it, tying the duty back to Rule 1.16(d). Neither Rule 1.16's own text nor a specific number of years to retain a file was
independently confirmed this session; see the gap list in
[verification_nh.md](verification_nh.md).

Contingent fees carry a New Hampshire-specific statutory layer beyond Rule 1.5(c) and (d), at
RSA 508:4-e. Before signing a contingent fee agreement, the lawyer must advise the client of the
right to instead retain the lawyer on an hourly, reasonable-value basis. Separately, in any
action resulting in a settlement or judgment of $200,000 or more, all fees and costs are subject
to court approval.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

New Hampshire runs one statewide program, the NHBA Dispute Resolution Committee (DRC), at
<https://www.nhbar.org/nhba-dispute-resolution-committee/>. It handles disputes between clients
and their lawyers, and between lawyers, that do not fall into one of three excluded categories:
the dispute is already the subject of a pending court case, the fee is set by statute, court
rule, or court order, or the dispute raises an ethical issue; any of those three sends the matter
elsewhere rather than to the DRC.

The DRC's own General Provisions and Procedures (revised June 2019) describe a two-stage
process: informal or formal Mediation first, and if that does not resolve the dispute, Binding
Arbitration. Both stages are voluntary on both sides. Arbitration becomes binding only after the
parties sign a Consent to Binding Arbitration form; a lawyer cannot be forced into it by the
client's request alone, and there is no asymmetry making it mandatory on the lawyer. The service
is free of charge. No dollar floor or ceiling on what the DRC will hear is stated anywhere in
its own procedures, and no filing deadline for opening a case is stated either; this pack did not
find one and is not asserting there is none. No provision requiring the lawyer to notify the
client of a right to arbitrate before suing for fees was found in the DRC's own materials.

Once a case is open, the DRC's own procedures set internal scheduling targets, not statutory
deadlines: mediation targeted to conclude within 90 days; a 10-day reminder if a required form
is not returned within 30 days; a binding arbitration hearing targeted within 45 days of the
consent form; a decision due within 20 days of the hearing; and 10 days after the decision to
request reconsideration. The procedures do not say what happens if a target is missed.

Binding arbitration under this program is governed by RSA Chapter 542. An award may be enforced,
vacated, or modified under RSA 542:8, generally within one year, and appealed under RSA 542:10.
The burden of proof in a fee dispute is placed on the attorney, not the client bringing the
dispute, one of the few places this pack found the burden shifted in the client's favor by
default.

Because arbitration here requires both sides' agreement, a lawyer who refuses to participate
cannot be compelled into it through this program; the next path is a bar complaint (Section 4)
or small claims court (Section 6) if the amount is within its limit.

## 4. Bar complaint process

New Hampshire's disciplinary body is the Attorney Discipline Office (ADO), operating under the
Professional Conduct Committee (PCC), the Complaint Screening Committee, and the Hearings
Committee, at <https://www.nhattyreg.org>. A grievance is filed with the ADO and a copy must
also go to the lawyer being complained about.

The ADO's own FAQ does not describe a separate informal-assistance track distinct from the
grievance itself; a filed grievance may be screened and dismissed or not docketed without a
hearing if there is no reasonable basis to conclude a rule was violated by clear and convincing
evidence, or it can proceed to a hearing before the Hearings Committee and then the PCC. This
pack did not find a distinct "informal request for assistance" intake separate from the
grievance process itself, the way some other states structure it.

The general filing deadline is 2 years after the act or acts of alleged misconduct, or, where
the misconduct was not and could not reasonably have been discovered at the time, 2 years from
when it was or reasonably should have been discovered, under Rule 37A(I)(i). Where the
misconduct is a course of conduct rather than a single act, the ADO's own FAQ states that the
2-year period can begin at the end of that course of conduct, not at its start.

Restitution. Asked directly in its own FAQ, "Will any money that I have paid to the attorney be
returned to me?", the ADO answers: "The Attorney Discipline System does not have authority to
order that fees be returned, even in the event there is a finding of misconduct." It points the
complainant instead to the NHBA Dispute Resolution Committee (Section 3) and notes that some
civil remedies may be available. Separately, Rule 37(18) and Rule 37A(II)(b)(1) state that a
settlement, compromise, or restitution between the parties does not by itself require the ADO to
stop investigating a lawyer's conduct; that provision protects the investigation, it does not
give the ADO power to order restitution. Read together, New Hampshire's disciplinary system does
not have a restitution-ordering mechanism, despite the word "restitution" appearing in its own
rules.

Possible sanctions are reprimand, public censure, suspension, or disbarment. The PCC can impose
suspension of up to six months on its own; longer suspension or disbarment requires the PCC to
file a recommendation with the Supreme Court. Discipline is not a refund; getting money back
runs through Section 3, Section 5, or a separate court claim.

## 5. Client protection fund

The Public Protection Fund is established by New Hampshire Supreme Court Rule 55 and
administered by the New Hampshire Bar Association through a nine-member committee, under the
Supreme Court's general oversight, at <https://www.nhbar.org/public-protection-fund/>.

It covers a loss of money or property, with a determinable monetary value, taken through theft
or misappropriation by a New Hampshire lawyer, occurring after June 1, 1998, during a
client-lawyer or fiduciary relationship (as executor, trustee, conservator, guardian, or
attorney-in-fact).

Exclusions, from the Fund's own regulations: the mere failure of a lawyer to return an unearned
fee is not "theft" for Fund purposes unless the lawyer performed no work on the client's behalf
and obtained no result; investment losses are excluded unless the lawyer was retained as an
attorney for purposes other than investment and specific other conditions are met; and certain
claimants are categorically ineligible, including the lawyer's relatives, business associates,
employer or employees, and any governmental entity or insurer. A claimant must also exhaust all
other reasonably available sources of recovery before the Fund will pay, and the Fund is
explicitly a last resort.

Filing deadline: a claim must be submitted within 3 years of when the claimant discovered, or
reasonably should have discovered, the loss. Notice of the claim must reach the Committee within
that 3-year window even if other sources of recovery have not yet been exhausted; filing the
claim is what stops that clock. Separately, a claim cannot be filed more than 1 year after the
lawyer who caused the loss was suspended, disbarred, died, or was judged incompetent.

Payout is discretionary: the Committee decides, after a hearing if requested, whether and how
much to reimburse. Rule 55 itself, which is where a dollar cap would appear, could not be
reached directly this session: it returned no snapshot in the Wayback Machine and courts.nh.gov
blocked live retrieval throughout. The Fund's own regulations, which were reached and read in
full, describe eligibility and procedure but do not state a dollar limit. A 1998 New Hampshire
Supreme Court opinion, issued when the current Fund rule was adopted, quotes Rule 55(3) and (4)
directly: a cap of $150,000 for losses caused by any one lawyer, and an aggregate cap of
$1,000,000 for losses in any one year across all claims. Treat these as 1998 figures only; this
pack could not confirm whether either cap has since been amended, because it could not read the
current text of Rule 55 itself. Do not rely on these numbers as current without confirming them
against Rule 55 or the Committee directly.

## 6. Small claims or equivalent court

New Hampshire's small claims process runs in the Circuit Court, District Division, under RSA
Chapter 503. The jurisdictional limit is $10,000, exclusive of interest and costs, at RSA 503:1,
effective July 1, 2015. This limit is set by statute and applies statewide; no county-level
variation was found.

Filing fee: $125 for a claim of $5,000 or less, $180 for a claim of $5,001 to $10,000, per the
Circuit Court's own filing fee schedule effective July 1, 2025.

An attorney may appear for a party in small claims, but must file an appearance with the court
and serve it on all other parties at least 7 days before the hearing, under RSA 503:2-a. A
corporation, partnership, LLC, or trust may appear through a non-lawyer officer, employee,
partner, owner, or trustee who presents written, notarized authorization, under RSA 503:11; a
lawyer is not required for an organization to appear.

A claim of more than $5,000 where no one has claimed a jury trial is subject to mandatory
mediation under RSA 503:1, paragraph IV, before it proceeds to a hearing on the merits; a claim
of $5,000 or less is not required to go through mediation first.

If the debt or damages claimed exceed $1,500, either side can claim a jury trial, which
transfers the case to superior court and is tried there as if it began there, under RSA 503:1. A
defendant who wants a jury trial must make that demand within 5 business days of being served
with the claim; this pack did not confirm an equivalent deadline running against the plaintiff,
who chose to file in small claims in the first place.

Appeal is not a trial de novo. Under RSA 503:9, the trial judge's findings of fact are final;
only questions of law may be transferred to the Supreme Court, in the same manner as an appeal
from superior court. Under RSA 503:10, a party has 30 days from the judgment or the notice of
judgment date to appeal to the Supreme Court. The Supreme Court's own Rule 49 filing fee
schedule, effective July 1, 2025, lists a $250 fee specifically for "Small Claim cases RSA 503"
at that court; this pack read that schedule directly and it carries no surcharge disclaimer of
any kind. That is unlike the separate Circuit Court filing fee schedule used for the original
small claims filing, which does carry a general note that a "total fee" can include "any
applicable surcharges or other fees authorized by the Supreme Court and/or New Hampshire
Statutes." Read as written, the $250 appears to be the complete Supreme Court fee. No bond
requirement for a small claims appeal was found in RSA Chapter 503.

There is a real, easily missed added cost to the appeal itself: because the Supreme Court
reviews on the existing record rather than retrying the case, a party appealing may need a sound
recording of the trial. Under RSA 503:10, a party who wants that recording made must request it
at least 5 business days before trial and is responsible for the cost of the recording and any
transcription. A reader planning to appeal should request the recording before trial, not after
losing, and budget for the transcription cost on top of the $250 Supreme Court fee.

No rule specific to a claim against a lawyer was found in RSA Chapter 503; a fee dispute is
treated the same as any other small claim.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

New Hampshire does not have a separate statute for ordinary contract claims. RSA 508:4, "Personal
Actions," is a catch-all: except as otherwise provided by law, all personal actions other than
slander or libel, which includes both a contract claim over a fee agreement and a negligence
claim such as legal malpractice, must be brought within 3 years of the act or omission
complained of. Built into the same sentence is a discovery rule: where the injury and its causal
connection to the act or omission were not, and could not reasonably have been, discovered at
the time, the 3-year period instead runs from when the plaintiff discovered, or reasonably
should have discovered, the injury and its causal connection to the act or omission.

Because both a contract claim and a malpractice claim fall under the same 3-year, same-discovery
-rule statute, RSA 508:4, whether a legal malpractice claim is framed in tort or in contract does
not change the limitation period available in New Hampshire; it is the same clock either way.
This is New Hampshire's own version of the "unusually short" period: many states give ordinary
written contract claims 4 to 6 years, and New Hampshire gives 3, with no separate longer period
for a written fee agreement.

A separate, longer period applies only to actions "of debt upon judgments, recognizances, and
contracts under seal," at RSA 508:5, 20 years; that is a narrow category (formally sealed
instruments) that an ordinary fee agreement does not fall into.

No statute of repose applicable to a legal malpractice or fee dispute claim was found. RSA
508:4-b sets an 8-year repose period, but it is expressly limited to actions arising from a
deficiency in the design, construction, or related work on an improvement to real property, and
does not apply here.

RSA 508:13 provides that in determining whether a professional met the standard of care in a
malpractice claim, the fact-finder is not bound to any particular geographic locality's
standard, but considers the training, experience, and professed skill of the average
practitioner in the profession generally.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

New Hampshire's Rules of Professional Conduct open with a "Statement of Purpose" rather than the
ABA's Preamble and Scope; New Hampshire never adopted the ABA Preamble and Scope in any form, so
there was no base text for its own committee to amend, and the Statement of Purpose replaces
that section entirely. It states plainly that the Rules "are not designed to be a basis for
civil liability," that violation of a Rule "should not itself give rise to a cause of action
against a lawyer nor should it create any presumption in such a case that a legal duty has been
breached," and that violation "does not necessarily warrant any other nondisciplinary remedy,
such as disqualification." It goes on to say that, because the Rules do establish a standard of
conduct, "a lawyer's violation of a Rule may be evidence of breach of the applicable standard of
conduct," meaning it can be used as evidence in an otherwise recognized claim such as
malpractice, without itself being the claim.

Do not confuse this Statement of Purpose with New Hampshire's Code of Judicial Conduct, which
has its own, similarly worded scope section governing judges rather than lawyers; they are
separate documents.

The client's actual money routes, given a rules violation alone does not support a lawsuit, run
through the rest of this pack: a contract claim over the fee agreement or a claim for its
unearned portion (Section 7's limitation period applies), the NHBA Dispute Resolution Committee
if the lawyer will agree to participate (Section 3), the Public Protection Fund if the loss
involved theft or misappropriation (Section 5), and small claims court for amounts within its
limit (Section 6). Getting the lawyer disciplined (Section 4) is a separate goal, aimed at the
lawyer's license rather than at the client's money.
