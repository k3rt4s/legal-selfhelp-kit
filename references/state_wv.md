# West Virginia: handling a dispute with your own lawyer

This section is for a person in West Virginia who has a problem with their own lawyer over
fees, billing, communication, getting their file back, or an advance payment that was never
earned or never refunded. It does not cover legal malpractice claims or how to run one
yourself. Retrieved August 20, 2026.

What is different about West Virginia:

- West Virginia keeps standard, unmodified ABA-style rule numbers. Rule 1.5 is Rule 1.5. But
  Rule 1.5(b), the requirement to tell a client the basis or rate of the fee, is mandatory in
  writing here, not merely preferred as it is under the national model. Read section 1 and 2.
- West Virginia has no binding statewide fee arbitration program. The State Bar runs only a
  Voluntary Fee Dispute Resolution Program, an informal 30-day facilitation that either side
  can walk away from at any time, with no dollar limits, no filing fee, and no binding award.
  Read section 3 before you count on this as a forced remedy.
- A West Virginia lawyer can hold your case file under a common-law retaining lien for unpaid
  fees, but a 1992 ethics opinion carves out a mandatory exception: your actual case
  materials, correspondence, pleadings, and discoverable documents must be turned over
  regardless of unpaid fees. Only pure attorney work product can be withheld, and only if you
  clearly still owe money. Read section 2.
- The magistrate court civil filing fee schedule has a separate, unexplained $20 line sitting
  next to the main tiered fee schedule. This kit could not resolve what that line covers.
  Confirm the exact filing fee with the magistrate clerk before you file. Read section 6.
- A magistrate court civil appeal costs far more than the appeal bond alone: a $200 circuit
  court filing fee is collected on top of the bond. Read section 6 for the full cost.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct: numbering and adoption

The West Virginia Rules of Professional Conduct are adopted by the Supreme Court of Appeals
of West Virginia, the state's highest court. By order entered September 29, 2014, the Court
approved comprehensive amendments to the rules, effective January 1, 2015. That is the
current governing text.

West Virginia uses standard ABA-style numbering with no state-specific renumbering or
chapter-prefix system. The fee rule is cited as "Rule 1.5," the same form used in the
national baseline, not a state-specific variant like "RPC 1.5" or a chapter-prefixed number.
Communication duties are Rule 1.4, safekeeping property is Rule 1.15, and duties on
termination are Rule 1.16, matching the model numbering exactly. A reader looking for any of
these rule numbers will find them where expected.

The rules are published by the West Virginia Judiciary at
<https://www.courtswv.gov/legal-community/court-rules/west-virginia-rules-professional-conduct>,
including both an "Engrossed version" and a "Strikethrough version" showing the 2014-2015
amendments.

Lawyer discipline in West Virginia is handled by several distinct bodies working together,
and a reader should know all of them by name:

- The Office of Lawyer Disciplinary Counsel (ODC), the investigative and prosecuting arm,
  informally called the Office of Disciplinary Counsel in its own procedural rules.
- The Lawyer Disciplinary Board, the governing body over the ODC, made up of 22 members,
  15 lawyers and 7 lay persons.
- The Investigative Panel of the Board, which screens and investigates complaints.
- Hearing Panel Subcommittees, which hold hearings and recommend a disposition to the
  Supreme Court of Appeals, which has final authority to impose discipline.
- The Lawyers' Fund for Client Protection Committee, a separate body under the West Virginia
  State Bar that administers the client protection fund described in section 5.

The disciplinary procedure itself, the Rules of Lawyer Disciplinary Procedure, is published
by the ODC at <https://wvodc.org/lawyer-disciplinary-procedure>.

## 2. How West Virginia's fee rule differs from the model

Rule 1.5(a) lists the same eight reasonableness factors as the ABA model rule, in the same
order, with nothing added and nothing removed: the time, labor, novelty, and skill the matter
required; whether taking the case precluded other work; the customary local fee; the amount
involved and results obtained; any time pressure; the length of the lawyer-client
relationship; the lawyer's experience, reputation, and ability; and whether the fee is fixed
or contingent.

Where West Virginia departs from the model is in Rule 1.5(b). The national baseline describes
the Model Rule as merely preferring a written fee-basis disclosure. West Virginia's version
does not: it states that the scope of representation and the basis or rate of the fee and
expenses for which the client will be responsible "shall be communicated to the client in
writing before or within a reasonable time after commencing the representation," with the
same exception for a client the lawyer regularly represents on the same basis. Any change in
the basis or rate of the fee "shall also be communicated to the client in writing." If your
lawyer never gave you anything in writing about how the fee would be calculated, that is
itself a rule violation here, not just a best practice they skipped.

This kit looked specifically for a separate West Virginia rule banning nonrefundable, flat,
or earned-upon-receipt fee language, and did not find one beyond the ordinary trust-account
requirement in Rule 1.15, which tracks the model rule: advance fees and expenses go into a
trust account and can only be withdrawn as they are actually earned or incurred. A retainer
labeled "nonrefundable" does not override that requirement; an unearned portion is still the
client's money.

The file-return question is the most consequential deviation this kit found. West Virginia
recognizes a common-law attorney retaining lien: a lawyer may retain a client's documents,
money, or other property that comes into the lawyer's hands professionally, until a general
balance due for professional services is paid (Capehart v. Church, 69 S.E.2d 127, 129
(W. Va. 1952); Fuller v. Stonewall Casualty Co. of W. Va., 304 S.E.2d 347, 349 n.1 (1983)).
On its face that could let a lawyer hold your entire file over unpaid fees.

Legal Ethics Inquiry 92-02, issued by the State Bar's ethics committee (the predecessor body
to today's Lawyer Disciplinary Board opinion process) and read in conjunction with Rule
1.16(d), narrows that lien sharply. The file that must be turned over regardless of unpaid
fees includes: all material the client provided, all correspondence, all pleadings and
motions, and all documents with evidentiary value that are discoverable under the Rules of
Civil Procedure, including depositions and business records. Only attorney work product
protected under West Virginia Rule of Civil Procedure 26(b)(3) may be withheld, and only if
the client clearly still owes fees; if the client owes nothing, even work product must be
turned over. This opinion was not among the small set of older opinions the Lawyer
Disciplinary Board formally vacated by order in 2012, so it remains current guidance on this
question as of this pack's retrieval date. In short: your lawyer can refuse to hand over
their own drafts and internal notes if you owe them money, but not your own documents,
correspondence, filings, or anything a court could make them produce in discovery.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

West Virginia does not run a binding statewide fee arbitration program. This kit searched the
West Virginia State Bar's Constitution, By-Laws, and Rules and Regulations in full for a
separate fee-arbitration procedural rules document, the kind Utah and some other states
publish as a standalone document, and found none; the program description on the Bar's own
public page is the complete and only description of the process.

What exists instead is the Voluntary Fee Dispute Resolution Program, run by the West Virginia
State Bar. Either the client or the lawyer may start it by sending the dispute, with
supporting documents, to the Executive Director of the State Bar. The materials are assigned
to a lawyer member of the local District Grievance Committee, approved by the Bar's Board of
Governors, who resides in the same county as the client. The other party is given a copy and
a chance to respond, and the assigned Committee member tries to resolve the dispute directly
with both sides. The Committee member is asked to complete these efforts within a 30-day
timeframe.

This kit found no dollar floor or ceiling on what the program will take, no filing fee, no
binding award mechanism, and therefore no appeal process, because there is no award to
appeal. Participation is not mandatory on either side at any stage; a lawyer or a client can
decline to take part or can walk away at any point, since nothing in the program's own
description makes the 30-day facilitation binding on either party. This kit found no rule
requiring a West Virginia lawyer to notify a client of this program before suing the client
for fees.

If your lawyer will not cooperate with this informal process, or the 30 days pass without
resolution, your money routes are magistrate court (section 6) or the client protection fund
(section 5) if the conduct involved dishonesty rather than a plain billing disagreement.

The program is described at
<https://www.wvbar.org/for-the-public/voluntary-fee-dispute-resolution-program/>.

## 4. Filing a bar complaint

West Virginia's disciplinary regulator is the Office of Lawyer Disciplinary Counsel (ODC),
operating under the Rules of Lawyer Disciplinary Procedure and the Lawyer Disciplinary Board
described in section 1. A complaint can be submitted using the complaint packet and form
linked from the ODC's own site.

Rule 2.14 of the Rules of Lawyer Disciplinary Procedure sets the filing deadline: any
complaint filed more than two years after the complainant knew, or in the exercise of
reasonable diligence should have known, of the existence of a rule violation must be
dismissed by the Investigative Panel. This is a discovery-rule deadline, not a flat two years
from the underlying conduct.

West Virginia's process has an informal component built into the same track rather than a
separate consumer-assistance office. Under Rule 2.4, the ODC evaluates every complaint it
receives and may refer a matter to a State Bar committee for resolution, or attempt to
resolve it informally without ever docketing it as a formal complaint, before deciding
whether a full investigation is warranted. A plain billing or communication problem may be
resolved this way without becoming a formal disciplinary case. Once a matter is docketed and
investigated, it can proceed through the Investigative Panel to a Hearing Panel Subcommittee
and, if disputed, to the Supreme Court of Appeals itself.

The ODC's process is not a way to get your money back. Its purpose is to evaluate whether a
lawyer violated the Rules of Professional Conduct and, if so, to recommend or impose
discipline. West Virginia does have a direct restitution mechanism, unlike many states,
though: Rule 3.15 of the Rules of Lawyer Disciplinary Procedure lists restitution as one of
nine sanctions a Hearing Panel Subcommittee may recommend or the Supreme Court of Appeals may
impose for a rule violation, alongside probation, practice limits, suspension, and
annulment. This is decided case by case as part of a disciplinary outcome; it is not
something a complainant files for or is guaranteed, and it is not a substitute for the
routes described in sections 3, 5, and 6.

The ODC is reached at Office of Lawyer Disciplinary Counsel, West Virginia Judicial Tower,
4700 MacCorkle Avenue SE, Suite 1200, Charleston, West Virginia 25304, phone (304) 558-7999.
Its site is <https://www.wvodc.org/>. That site is a single page. The complaint packet and
the complaint form are posted on it, behind buttons labeled Complaint Packet and Complaint
Form. Both open as PDFs.

## 5. Client protection fund

West Virginia's fund is the Lawyers' Fund for Client Protection, administered by a Committee
under the West Virginia State Bar's Board of Governors, governed by the Fund's own
procedural rules.

Under Rule 10 of those procedural rules, the fund reimburses a loss caused by the "dishonest
conduct" of a lawyer, arising out of a lawyer-client or fiduciary relationship. Dishonest
conduct is defined to mean wrongful acts in the nature of theft, embezzlement, or wrongful
taking or conversion of money or property, and the rule expressly includes within that
definition "refusal to refund unearned fees received in advance as required by Rule 1.16"
and borrowing money from a client without intent or reasonable ability to repay it. That
matters for this kit's audience directly: an advance you paid that your lawyer simply kept
without doing the work, and refuses to return, is not automatically treated as a mere
billing disagreement here. It can be a "dishonest conduct" claim the fund is built to cover,
separate from an ordinary dispute over whether a fee already earned was reasonable.

Rule 10.D lists exclusions, unless Rule 10.E's hardship exception applies: losses of the
lawyer's spouse, children, parents, grandparents, siblings, partners, associates, and
employees; losses already covered by a bond, surety agreement, or insurance contract; losses
of a financial institution recoverable under a banker's blanket bond; losses through a
business entity the lawyer or those same insiders control; and losses of any governmental
entity or agency. Rule 10.F lets the Committee deny a claim in its discretion where there
would be unjust enrichment or the claimant knowingly contributed to the loss.

The caps are set in Rule 7.C: the Committee may recommend payment of claims up to $40,000
per attorney per year, with a $20,000 cap per individual claim, subject to change from time
to time by the Board of Governors. This kit checked the fund's separate public overview page
for a different, more current figure and found none stated there, so no discrepancy between
sources was found, unlike some other states' funds.

Rule 10.B sets the filing deadline: a claim must be filed no later than two years after the
claimant knew or should have known of the lawyer's dishonest conduct and that a loss
resulted. This is a discovery-rule deadline, not a fixed date from the loss itself.

The Fund's overview page for the public is at
<https://wvbar.org/public-information/lawyers-fund-for-client-protection/>, and its
procedural rules, including Rule 7.C and Rule 10, are published at
<https://wvbar.org/committees/bar-committees/lawyers-fund-for-client-protection/lawyers-fund-for-client-protection-rules/>.
The Fund also publishes a running list of claims it has actually paid. That list shows
several claimants paid exactly $20,000, the per-claim cap, in recent years, and none paid
more than that. This kit found no separate figure anywhere on the Fund's public pages that
conflicts with the $40,000/$20,000 caps in Rule 7.C.

## 6. Small claims equivalent: magistrate court

West Virginia does not have a court called "small claims." The equivalent is magistrate
court, a court of limited jurisdiction in every county.

The jurisdictional limit for a civil action in magistrate court is $20,000, under W. Va. Code
section 50-2-1. This kit found no county-level variation in that figure; it is a single statewide
statutory limit.

A party does not need a lawyer in magistrate court. Individuals may represent themselves.
Rule 10 of the Administrative Rules for the Magistrate Courts states that a corporation may
appear either by any attorney licensed to practice in West Virginia or by any officer or
employee of the corporation; if a non-attorney is used solely to represent the corporation in
legal matters, that person must be a full-time employee of the corporation. In other words, a
business you are suing, or that is suing you, does not need to hire a lawyer to appear in
magistrate court, and neither do you.

The magistrate court civil filing fee is tiered by the amount claimed under W. Va. Code
section 50-3-1(a): $30.00 for a claim of $500 or less, $35.00 for more than $500 up to $1,000,
$40.00 for more than $1,000 up to $2,000, and $50.00 for more than $2,000, or $30.00 for a
claim seeking relief other than money damages. The same statute contains a separate
subsection, section 50-3-1(g), reading only "For filing and trying any civil action $20.00," with no
explanation of when it applies instead of, or in addition to, the tiered schedule in
subsection (a). This kit could not resolve what section 50-3-1(g) covers or why both figures appear
in the same statute. Do not assume either figure is the complete cost; confirm the exact fee
with the magistrate court clerk in the county where you would file before you go.

Either party may appeal a magistrate court civil judgment to circuit court as a matter of
right under W. Va. Code section 50-5-12(a), within 20 days of judgment, or within 20 days of a
ruling on a motion to set the judgment aside. The circuit court may allow an appeal up to 90
days after judgment on a showing of good cause for missing the 20-day deadline. Filing or
granting the appeal automatically stays enforcement of the judgment.

The true cost of that appeal has two separate pieces, and missing either one understates it:

1. An appeal bond, collected by the magistrate court clerk when the appeal is filed. Under
   section 50-5-12(a), the bond amount is not a fixed figure; it must be "not less than the
   reasonable court costs of the appeal nor more than the sum of the judgment and the
   reasonable court costs of the appeal." No bond is required of a governmental party or of
   someone permitted to proceed without prepayment of costs under W. Va. Code section 59-2-1.
2. A separate circuit court filing fee, also collected by the magistrate court clerk at the
   time the appeal is filed and forwarded to the circuit clerk. Section 50-5-12(a) refers to this
   fee generically as "the circuit court filing fee" without stating the amount in that
   section. This kit traced the actual figure to W. Va. Code section 59-1-11(a)(1): the circuit
   clerk's fee for, among other things, "the docketing of civil appeals or removals of civil
   cases from magistrate court" is $200.

Budget for both the variable bond and the separate $200 circuit court filing fee, not just
one or the other.

Whether the appeal is a full new trial or a review of the record depends on how the case was
tried below, under section 50-5-12(b): if the magistrate court case was tried to a jury, the circuit
court appeal is a hearing on the record, not a new trial. If the magistrate court case was
tried to the magistrate alone, without a jury, the circuit court appeal is a trial de novo, a
full new trial before the circuit court without a jury.

This kit found no magistrate court rule specific to suing your own lawyer; an ordinary fee
dispute is treated the same as any other magistrate court civil claim, subject to the
$20,000 jurisdictional limit.

The jurisdictional statute is published at <https://code.wvlegislature.gov/50-2-1/>, the fee
schedule at <https://code.wvlegislature.gov/50-3-1/>, the appeal statute at
<https://code.wvlegislature.gov/50-5-12/>, the circuit clerk fee schedule at
<https://code.wvlegislature.gov/59-1-11/>, and the Administrative Rules for the Magistrate
Courts at
<https://www.courtswv.gov/legal-community/court-rules/magistrate-court-proceedings-administrative-rules>.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A dispute over what you owed your lawyer, or what your lawyer owed you back, under your fee
agreement is a contract claim. West Virginia Code section 55-2-6 splits the period by the form of
the contract: an action on a contract in writing, signed by the party to be charged, gets ten
years, the same period given to a contract under seal. An action on any other contract,
meaning an oral or otherwise unwritten agreement, gets five years.

Legal malpractice claims in West Virginia are not confined to one characterization. Smith v.
Stacy, 482 S.E.2d 115, 198 W. Va. 498, docket no. 23196 (W. Va. 1996), states in its second
syllabus point, quoting Syllabus Point 1 of Hall v. Nichols, 184 W. Va. 466, 400 S.E.2d 901
(1990), which in turn quotes the Syllabus of Harrison v. Casto, 165 W. Va. 787, 271 S.E.2d 774
(1980): "[u]nless a complaint in a malpractice action against an attorney sounds only in tort,
such action may be brought on contract or in tort and the fact that the statute of limitations
bars the tort action does not preclude an action on contract which is not barred by the
applicable limitation statute." Which characterization applies
turns on what the claim actually alleges: under Syllabus Point 2 of Hall v. Nichols, 184
W. Va. 466, 400 S.E.2d 901 (1990), as restated in Smith v. Stacy, a claim alleging breach of
specific contract terms, without reference to the legal duties the law imposes on the
attorney-client relationship, is contractual; a claim alleging breach of a duty the law
imposes on that relationship, rather than breach of the contract itself, sounds in tort. If
the claim could reasonably be read either way, Cochran v. Appalachian Power Co., 162 W. Va.
86, 246 S.E.2d 624 (1978), as restated in the same case, presumes it is a contract claim
whenever treating it as a tort claim would mean it is time-barred.

Where a legal malpractice claim sounds in tort, Smith v. Stacy confirms the trial court and
the parties treated "the two-year tort statute of limitations" as the applicable period for
an attorney malpractice claim. That two-year period corresponds to the general personal
actions limitation in W. Va. Code section 55-2-12(b), for actions not otherwise provided for, which
applies to actions for damages for personal injuries. Where the same claim instead sounds in
contract, the contract periods in section 55-2-6 described above apply instead.

West Virginia recognizes a tolling doctrine specific to attorney malpractice. Smith v. Stacy
holds that West Virginia adopts the continuous representation doctrine, under which the
statute of limitations on an attorney malpractice claim is tolled until the professional
relationship terminates as to the matter underlying the claim. The same decision limits that
doctrine: it does not apply where the attorney's later role is only tangentially related to
the representation in which the malpractice allegedly occurred, and it applies only where
there is clear evidence of an ongoing, continuous, developing, and dependent relationship
between the client and the attorney on the same or related matter.

This kit did not find a statute of repose specific to legal malpractice claims separate from
the ordinary limitation periods described above.

This kit does not cover running a legal malpractice claim. These periods are here so you know
a clock exists and can get your own counsel in time if you need it, not so you can calculate
your own deadline.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

West Virginia's Rules of Professional Conduct carry the same Scope-section language as the
ABA model rule, in paragraph [20] of the Scope section that precedes the numbered rules
(this is the Scope section of the Rules of Professional Conduct itself, not the separate
Code of Judicial Conduct, which has its own differently numbered scope provision): violation
of a rule "should not itself give rise to a cause of action against a lawyer nor should it
create any presumption in such a case that a legal duty has been breached," is not designed
to be a basis for civil liability, and does not by itself warrant a remedy such as
disqualifying a lawyer from pending litigation. The same paragraph adds that because the
rules do establish standards of conduct, a lawyer's violation of a rule "may be evidence of
breach" of the applicable standard of care in a case that is otherwise a recognized claim,
such as a malpractice action.

In practical terms, a disciplinary complaint against your lawyer does not by itself put money
in your hands. Your actual money routes are the ones covered above: a contract claim over the
fee itself under section 7, the informal fee dispute program in section 3, the client
protection fund in section 5 if the conduct amounted to dishonest conduct as that fund
defines it, a disciplinary restitution order under section 4 if the case reaches that stage,
or magistrate court under section 6, or a separate malpractice claim if that applies to you,
which is outside what this kit covers.
