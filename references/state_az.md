# Arizona

This pack is for a person in Arizona who has a fee, billing, communication, or file-return
dispute with their own lawyer. It does not cover legal malpractice as a claim to run yourself.
Retrieved 2026-08-20.

What an Arizona reader most needs to know, that a reader in another state does not:

- Arizona does not call its rules of professional conduct "Rules." It calls them Ethical
  Rules, cited as "ER," and they sit inside Rule 42 of the Rules of the Supreme Court of
  Arizona. A reader who searches for "Rule 1.5" alone will not find it. The citation form is
  "ER 1.5, Rule 42, Ariz. R. Sup. Ct."
- Arizona's eighth reasonableness factor is not the model rule's "whether the fee is fixed or
  contingent." It is "the degree of risk assumed by the lawyer," a different and more specific
  question.
- The State Bar of Arizona runs a real, statewide Fee Arbitration Program, but it is voluntary
  for both the lawyer and the client, not mandatory on the lawyer, unless the fee agreement
  itself already contains a promise to arbitrate. Once both sides sign on, the award is
  binding, and there is a three-year filing window and a $1,000 minimum amount in controversy.
- Arizona's small claims division has a $5,000 limit and bars lawyers from appearing at all,
  for either side, unless every party consents in writing, a stricter rule than most states'
  small claims courts.
- The Client Protection Fund will not consider a claim until the lawyer has been suspended
  more than six months, disbarred, or hit one of a short list of other severe discipline
  triggers, a much higher bar than most states' funds.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct, numbering and adoption

Arizona adopts its rules of professional conduct as Rule 42 of the Rules of the Supreme Court
of Arizona. It calls them Ethical Rules rather than Rules, and the citation form is "ER"
followed by the rule number, for example "ER 1.5," not "Rule 1.5" and not "RPC 1.5." A reader
searching a form letter for "Rule 1.5" by itself will not find Arizona's fee rule; the correct
citation is "ER 1.5, Rule 42, Ariz. R. Sup. Ct."

Numbering otherwise tracks the rules a client is most likely to need: ER 1.4 (communication),
ER 1.5 (fees), ER 1.15 (safekeeping property), and ER 1.16 (declining or terminating
representation) sit at the same numbers used elsewhere.

The current text of ER 1.5, reproduced in the State Bar of Arizona's own Fee Arbitration
Program materials, carries an effective date of January 1, 2021, reflecting the Arizona
Supreme Court's 2021 overhaul of the fee-sharing and referral provisions in ER 1.5(e). The
reasonableness factors in ER 1.5(a), covered in Section 2, were not part of that 2021 change.

The Arizona Supreme Court's own rules portal is hosted through a Westlaw-run court rules site
linked from azcourts.gov, at <https://www.azcourts.gov/rules> and
<http://azrules.westgroup.com>. Both pages loaded normally this session, but the rule-text
pages inside that site are built with JavaScript navigation that did not return readable rule
text on this session's attempts. See [verification_az.md](verification_az.md) for what was
used instead: the State Bar of Arizona's Fee Arbitration Program Rules, which reproduce ER
1.5's full current text as an attachment, and the State Bar's own ER 1.5 summary page.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

Arizona lists eight reasonableness factors in ER 1.5(a), the same count as the ABA model rule.
Seven track the model list. The eighth does not:

8. The degree of risk assumed by the lawyer. The ABA model rule's eighth factor asks whether
   the fee is fixed or contingent. Arizona's version asks a different, more specific question:
   how much risk the lawyer actually carried in taking on the arrangement. A contingent fee on
   a strong, well-documented case carries less risk than a contingent fee taken on a case
   everyone expected to lose, and Arizona's factor lets a dispute over a bill point to that
   difference directly, rather than just to the type of fee.

ER 1.5(b) requires the scope of representation and the basis or rate of the fee to be
communicated to the client in writing, before or within a reasonable time after the
representation begins, with a later change in the basis or rate also requiring written notice
before the client is billed at the higher rate. Arizona names two exceptions: court-appointed
lawyers paid by a court or government entity, and lawyers providing short-term limited legal
services under ER 6.5, a limited-scope clinic-style representation rule. Outside those two
exceptions, the written notice requirement applies to every new engagement.

ER 1.5(c) on contingent fees otherwise tracks the model rule: a signed writing stating the
method for calculating the fee, the percentages at settlement, trial, and appeal, which
expenses are deducted and when, and a written closing statement at the end of the matter.

ER 1.5(d)(3) is Arizona's clearest deviation from the model rule's bare text: a fee labeled
"earned upon receipt," "nonrefundable," or similar terms is only permitted if the client is
told in writing, at the same time, that the client may still discharge the lawyer at any time
and may still be entitled to a refund of part or all of the fee based on the value of the work
actually done. Labeling a fee nonrefundable does not, by itself, make it nonrefundable in
Arizona.

Contingent fees are barred in the same two situations as the model rule: a domestic relations
matter where the fee depends on securing a divorce or on the amount of alimony, support, or a
property settlement, and representing a defendant in a criminal case.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

The State Bar of Arizona runs a statewide Fee Arbitration Program, at
<https://www.azbar.org/for-legal-professionals/communities/advisory-groups/fee-arbitration-program/>,
also described for consumers at
<https://www.azbar.org/for-the-public/bar-provided-services-for-consumers/fee-arbitration/>,
governed by its own Rules of Arbitration of Fee Disputes, currently
<https://www.azbar.org/media/u3lam2nn/fa-rules-signed-10-21-22.pdf>.

Participation is voluntary on both sides as a general matter. Nothing forces a lawyer into the
program the way some other states do; both the lawyer and the client must sign an Agreement to
Arbitrate before it proceeds. The one exception: if the client's fee agreement already contains
a promise to use the Program, the State Bar's own guidance for a person weighing whether to
file a complaint says the lawyer must participate when the client invokes that promise. Outside
that situation, either side can decline. Once both sign the Agreement to Arbitrate, the
arbitrator's award is final and binding, enforceable in superior court, with only a limited
right to object.

The Program's current rules decline jurisdiction if the amount in controversy is less than
$1,000, or if the petition is filed more than three years after the lawyer-client relationship
ended or the final bill was received, whichever is longer. An earlier version of the Program's
rules, revised 05/18/2018, set the floor at $500 instead. That 2018 revision was superseded by
a further revision dated 10/21/2022, which raised the floor to $1,000, matching the figure the
State Bar's own overview and consumer-facing fee arbitration pages have used all along. This
pack now uses $1,000 as the current figure. See the confirmed table in
[verification_az.md](verification_az.md) for how the two versions were compared and dated.

The program is free. No filing fee is mentioned anywhere in the Program's current rules or its
own step-by-step guidance, and the State Bar describes the program as "free" on its overview
page.

No requirement was found that a lawyer must notify a client of the right to arbitrate before
suing the client for unpaid fees, except where the client's own fee agreement already commits
the lawyer to the Program. If your lawyer sues you for fees, ask whether Fee Arbitration is
available and check your fee agreement for an arbitration clause; outside that clause, nothing
in the Program's rules requires the lawyer to have raised it first.

If your dispute is $1,000 or more and within three years of the end of the representation or
the final bill, this is very likely the cheapest and fastest path in Arizona, ahead of a bar
complaint (Section 4) or small claims (Section 6), because the State Bar administers the
process and the award is enforceable in court without a lawsuit of your own. A dispute under
$1,000 falls outside the Program's jurisdiction and belongs in small claims instead
(Section 6).

## 4. Bar complaint process

The State Bar of Arizona's Lawyer Regulation Office investigates alleged violations of the
Rules of Professional Conduct and can refer matters to the Presiding Disciplinary Judge for
formal proceedings. Complaints are filed online through the State Bar's Charge Form, described
from <https://www.azbar.org/for-the-public/concerns-about-your-legal-professional/>.

The process runs through Intake first: an assigned lawyer reviews a charge and decides whether
to dismiss it, resolve it informally, or move it to a formal investigation. Informal outcomes
include diversion, an admonition, probation, or restitution; formal outcomes, which require the
Presiding Disciplinary Judge or the Arizona Supreme Court, are reprimand, suspension, or
disbarment. Source:
<https://www.azbar.org/for-legal-professionals/lawyer-regulation/discipline-bar-charges/the-discipline-process/>.

A fee dispute where the lawyer did some work belongs in the Fee Arbitration Program (Section
3), not a bar charge. The State Bar's own "Before Submitting a Charge" guidance, at
<https://www.azbar.org/for-the-public/concerns-about-your-legal-professional/before-submitting-a-charge/>,
points a reader with a fee complaint toward requesting a billing statement, talking to the
lawyer directly, and the Fee Arbitration Program, and states that the Lawyer Regulation Office
does not investigate malpractice claims or matters that do not involve a violation of the
conduct rules.

No deadline for filing a bar complaint was found on either page. That is not the same as a
confirmed statement that no deadline exists; it means none was located this session. See the
gap list.

Restitution is available as an informal sanction, meaning the disciplinary process itself,
separate from Fee Arbitration and the Client Protection Fund, can order money paid back to a
client. But it is discretionary, tied to a disciplinary outcome, and not the primary route for
a straightforward billing disagreement.

Discipline is not automatically a refund, even where restitution is on the table. It puts the
lawyer's conduct on the record and can lead to sanctions; getting your money back reliably runs
through Fee Arbitration, the Client Protection Fund, or a contract claim (Sections 3, 5, and 6).

## 5. Client protection fund

The Client Protection Fund, established by the Arizona Supreme Court in 1961, reimburses
people who lost money to the dishonest conduct of an Arizona-licensed lawyer. It is
administered by a five-member Board of Trustees appointed by the State Bar's Board of
Governors, is a trust separate from the State Bar itself, and is funded by a yearly assessment
on every licensed Arizona lawyer, not tax dollars. Source:
<https://www.azbar.org/for-the-public/bar-provided-services-for-consumers/client-protection-fund/>.

What it covers: theft or embezzlement of money or property, wrongful taking or conversion,
failure to refund an unearned advance fee when no work was performed, and a lawyer's
intentional dishonesty or deceit that directly caused the financial loss.

What it excludes: negligence, incompetence, or malpractice; a fee dispute where the lawyer did
perform some work; money given to a lawyer for investment or any purpose unrelated to the
lawyer-client relationship; interest, other lawyers' fees, or other damages; and any loss where
the lawyer was not in good standing at the time.

The per-claim cap is $100,000. The aggregate cap against any one lawyer, across all claims, is
$250,000.

Before a claim is even eligible for consideration, the lawyer must fall into one of several
severe categories: suspended for longer than six months, disbarred, on interim suspension,
transferred to disability inactive status, deceased, or convicted of a felony arising from the
same facts. A claim against a lawyer who received a lighter sanction, or none at all, is not
eligible no matter how dishonest the conduct was. A disciplinary complaint does not have to be
on file first, but the Fund prefers a claimant to have pursued other reasonable remedies,
including a claim against the lawyer's estate if the lawyer is deceased.

The filing deadline is five years from when the claimant knew, or reasonably should have known,
of the lawyer's dishonest conduct.

Awards are paid entirely at the discretion of the Trustees, described on the Fund's own page as
"not as a matter of right." A prior restitution order against the lawyer is considered but does
not bind the Trustees.

## 6. Small claims or equivalent court

Arizona's small claims equivalent is the small claims division of the justice court,
established in every justice court under A.R.S. § 22-502.

The jurisdictional limit is $5,000, exclusive of interest and costs, under A.R.S. § 22-503,
both for money claims and for actions to disaffirm, avoid, or rescind a contract, or seeking
equitable relief, within that amount. The statute sets this limit statewide; no county
variation was found. Source: <https://www.azleg.gov/ars/22/00503.htm>.

Lawyers are barred from appearing in a small claims case at all, for either side, unless every
party consents in writing, under A.R.S. § 22-512(B)(7) and (D). This is stricter than a rule
that merely discourages lawyers; it is an outright bar unless both sides agree, and any party
can block it by withholding consent. A lawyer may always represent themselves. A corporation
must be represented by a full-time officer or authorized employee, not a lawyer, unless the
consent exception applies. Source: <https://www.azleg.gov/ars/22/00512.htm>.

Either party can move the case out of the small claims division and into the regular justice
court docket, which restores the right to appeal, by objecting in writing at least ten days
before the hearing, under A.R.S. § 22-504(A). The complaint form itself must carry a bold
warning about this. Source: <https://www.azleg.gov/ars/22/00504.htm>.

If nobody moves the case out, there is no appeal at all from a small claims judgment. A.R.S.
§ 22-519 says so directly: the hearing officer's or justice of the peace's decision is final
and binding on both parties. Source: <https://www.azleg.gov/ars/22/00519.htm>. There is also no
jury trial available in the small claims division, under A.R.S. § 22-518.

The statewide base filing fee for a small claims case is $30.00, with an $18.00 fee to file an
answer, set by the Arizona Supreme Court's court filing fee schedule. Source:
<https://www.azcourts.gov/courtfilingfees/Justice-Court-Filing-Fees>. Counties add local
surcharges on top of this base fee, so confirm the total with the clerk in the county where you
plan to file.

No rule specific to suing a lawyer in the small claims division was found.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A fee agreement dispute, such as a lawyer's failure to refund an unearned fee, is a contract
claim. Arizona's limitation period for a debt founded on a written contract executed in
Arizona is six years, at A.R.S. § 12-548, at <https://www.azleg.gov/ars/12/00548.htm>. A debt
not evidenced by a writing, an oral fee arrangement, has a three-year period instead, at
A.R.S. § 12-543, at <https://www.azleg.gov/ars/12/00543.htm>. Both statutes run from when the
cause of action accrues; § 12-543 separately provides that a claim based on fraud or mistake
does not accrue until the aggrieved party discovers the underlying facts.

Legal malpractice, a claim that the lawyer's own negligence caused you harm, needs its own
counsel rather than self-help. It is not covered further in this kit beyond the deadline,
included so you know a clock exists and can get counsel in time. Arizona applies a two-year
period, at A.R.S. § 12-542, at <https://www.azleg.gov/ars/12/00542.htm>, confirmed directly in
a 2026 Arizona Court of Appeals opinion, *DMB Realco, LLC v. Mariscal, Weeks, McIntyre &
Friedlander, P.A.*, No. 1 CA-CV 24-0278 (Ariz. Ct. App. Div. 1, July 8, 2026). The legislature
has not defined when a legal malpractice claim accrues; Arizona courts have filled that gap by
holding that, outside the litigation context, the claim accrues when the client should know
the lawyer's negligence caused harm, and that the harm must be irremediable or irrevocable, not
just a bad outcome that might still be fixed. Where the malpractice happened during litigation,
the same line of case law holds the damages are not ascertainable, and the clock does not
start, until the underlying case and any appeal are over.

No statute of repose specific to legal malpractice was found in Arizona. The repose provisions
located apply to product liability, at A.R.S. § 12-551, not to legal malpractice; no outer
limit beyond the two-year discovery-based period above was found for a claim against your own
lawyer.

Whether Arizona lets a client choose between framing a claim against a lawyer as a tort or as a
contract claim, with different periods attached to each, was not confirmed either way this
session. The case law located treats "legal malpractice" as a single claim governed by the
two-year period in § 12-542 regardless of framing, but no square holding on an election
question was found. See the gap list.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

Arizona does not give a client a private cause of action for a violation of the Ethical Rules.
Comment 20 to the Preamble of Arizona's Rules of Professional Conduct, patterned on the
equivalent paragraph in the ABA model rules' Preamble, states that a violation of a rule should
not itself create a cause of action against a lawyer or any presumption that a legal duty was
breached, because the rules are not designed to be the basis for civil liability. Source:
Arizona Attorney Magazine, "Eye on Ethics," October 2018, at
<https://www.myazbar.org/AZAttorney/PDF_Articles/1018EyeonEthicsWEb.pdf>.

The same Comment adds a qualifier: because the rules do establish standards of conduct, a
violation can still be used as evidence of a breach of the standard of care in a case that is
otherwise a recognized claim, such as malpractice. Arizona's courts have applied that qualifier
since at least *Elliott v. Videan*, a 1989 Arizona Court of Appeals decision holding that a
violation of the ethics rules does not by itself establish malpractice but is evidence a jury
can weigh.

Your actual money route runs through the other forums in this pack: a contract claim for a
written or oral fee agreement (Section 7), the Fee Arbitration Program (Section 3), or the
Client Protection Fund where the lawyer's conduct was dishonest rather than merely a bad bill
(Section 5). Getting the lawyer disciplined (Section 4) is a separate goal, pursued in a
separate forum.
