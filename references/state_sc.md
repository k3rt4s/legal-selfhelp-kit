# South Carolina: handling a dispute with your own lawyer

This section is for a person in South Carolina who has a problem with their own lawyer
over fees, billing, communication, getting their file back, or an advance payment that
was never earned or never refunded. It does not cover legal malpractice claims or how to
run one yourself. Retrieved August 20, 2026.

What is different about South Carolina:

- South Carolina numbers its rules of professional conduct the same way the ABA model
  does, inside Rule 407 of the South Carolina Appellate Court Rules (SCACR). You cite
  "Rule 1.5" the same way you would in most states, but the formal citation nests it
  inside Rule 407, SCACR. Read section 1 below.
- South Carolina lets a lawyer treat an advance fee as earned the moment it is received,
  and deposited straight into the lawyer's own account rather than a trust account, if a
  written fee agreement meets five specific conditions. If your lawyer used this option,
  your money was never sitting in trust waiting to be refunded in the way you might
  expect. Read section 2.
- South Carolina runs a single statewide fee arbitration program, the Resolution of Fee
  Disputes Board, and the state bar's own materials say a lawyer is compelled to
  participate once a client files and consents to be bound, while the client can still
  walk away at any point before a final decision. That asymmetry is the single most
  useful fact in this pack. Read section 3 before you assume your lawyer can simply
  refuse to arbitrate.
- A complaint to the state's disciplinary office that is really just a dispute over
  whether a fee was earned will usually be dismissed and redirected to the fee
  arbitration program, not investigated as misconduct. Read section 4.
- South Carolina's small claims-equivalent court, magistrate's court, has one statewide
  dollar limit, not a patchwork of county limits.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct: numbering and adoption

South Carolina's rules of professional conduct for lawyers are Rule 407 of the South
Carolina Appellate Court Rules (SCACR), adopted and amended by the South Carolina
Supreme Court. Individual rules are numbered the same way the ABA Model Rules are
numbered: Rule 1.4, Rule 1.5, Rule 1.15, Rule 1.16, and so on, with no renumbering
scheme layered on top the way some states do. A reader looking for 1.5, 1.4, 1.15, or
1.16 will find them at those same numbers.

The full, formal citation form is "Rule 1.5, RPC, Rule 407, SCACR," though in practice
you will see it shortened to "Rule 1.5, RPC" or simply "Rule 1.5." Either short form is
understood; just be aware that the rule technically lives inside Rule 407.

The official publication point is the South Carolina Judicial Branch's own site,
sccourts.org, which publishes each rule of Rule 407 as its own page under Court Rules,
Appellate, Rule 407. The fee rule, Rule 1.5, was last amended by an order dated July 30,
2012, which is where the advance-fee provisions described in section 2 come from. Other
rules within Rule 407 were amended more recently; for example, Rules 1.0, 1.1, 1.18,
4.4, 5.3, 5.5, and 8.5 were amended by a Supreme Court order dated November 17, 2021, to
address lawyers' duties around technology. This kit did not find a single confirmed date
for South Carolina's original, wholesale adoption of the Model Rules; treat the amendment
dates above as what is confirmed, not as the rule's founding date.

Start at the court's own rule pages, beginning with the Preamble, at
<https://www.sccourts.org/resources/judicial-community/court-rules/appellate/rule-407/preamble/>.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

Rule 1.5(a) lists the same eight reasonableness factors as the ABA model rule: time and
labor, the difficulty of the matter, the customary local fee, the amount involved and
results obtained, time limits, the length of the relationship, the lawyer's experience
and reputation, and whether the fee is fixed or contingent. South Carolina does not add
to or subtract from that list of eight. The rule's own comment does note one internal
difference: South Carolina makes the second factor, whether taking the case would
preclude other work, an objective test rather than the subjective one used in the ABA
comment. That is a narrow point that mainly matters to a lawyer defending a fee, not to
you as the person disputing one.

Rule 1.5(b) requires the scope of representation and the fee basis to be communicated to
you, "preferably in writing," before or shortly after the lawyer takes you on. Unlike
some states, South Carolina's rule does not set a dollar threshold above which writing
becomes mandatory. A purely oral fee agreement is not automatically a rule violation
here the way it might be treated elsewhere.

Where South Carolina goes further than the model is what a lawyer may do with an advance
fee payment. The default, under Rule 1.15(c), is that any fee paid in advance and not
yet earned goes into the lawyer's trust account. Rule 1.5(f) creates an alternative: a
lawyer may treat an advance fee as immediately earned, and deposit it directly into the
lawyer's own operating account, if the lawyer and client agree in advance in a written
fee agreement that tells the client all of the following: the nature of the fee
arrangement and scope of services; the total amount of the fee and payment terms; that
the fee will not be held in trust until earned; that the client has the right to
terminate the relationship and discharge the lawyer; and that the client may be entitled
to a refund of all or part of the fee if the agreed-upon services are not provided. The
rule's comment says this kind of arrangement goes by different names, including "flat
fee," "earned on receipt," or "nonrefundable retainer," but all of them stay subject to
refund if the lawyer does not do the work.

This matters to you directly. If your lawyer used this option, look at the written fee
agreement you signed. If it reads like the notice Rule 1.5(f) describes, your money was
never sitting in trust the way an ordinary retainer would be, but the agreement itself
should still promise you a refund of anything not actually earned, and that promise is
enforceable.

On ending the relationship and returning your file, Rule 1.16(d) requires a lawyer, on
termination, to take reasonably practicable steps to protect your interests: reasonable
notice, time to find new counsel, surrendering papers and property you are entitled to,
and refunding any advance payment of fee or expense that was not earned or incurred. The
rule also says a lawyer "may retain papers relating to the client to the extent
permitted by other law" and "may retain a reasonable nonrefundable retainer." Unlike some
states, South Carolina's rule text does not disclaim a lawyer's retaining lien over your
file; this kit did not find a South Carolina ethics opinion or statute on that point
either way, so treat the question of whether a lawyer can hold your file over an unpaid
bill as open here rather than settled, and see section 7 on where a genuine dispute over
that would ultimately have to be resolved.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

South Carolina runs a single statewide program: the Resolution of Fee Disputes Board of
the South Carolina Bar, created under Rule 416, SCACR, and administered through the
Bar's committee structure of roughly 200 volunteer attorneys organized by judicial
circuit. It is not a court program, though it operates under a Supreme Court rule and its
decisions are enforceable like a court judgment.

Participation is asymmetric. The South Carolina Bar's own published FAQ for attorneys
states plainly that a lawyer is "compelled to participate" once a client files an
application and consents in writing to be bound by the Board's decision; the lawyer then
becomes bound as well. The program remains voluntary for the client in a different sense:
under the program's own rules, the party who initiated the process may voluntarily
terminate it at any time before a final decision, taking the dispute elsewhere instead.
So a client can walk in, and walk back out, on their own terms; a lawyer who is brought
in by a client's application cannot simply decline to take part.

Once a final decision is reached, it is binding and enforceable in any court of
competent jurisdiction. A party may appeal to the circuit court in the county where the
lawyer's principal place of practice is located, but only within 30 days, and only on
narrow grounds similar to those used to vacate any arbitration award: corruption, fraud,
undue means, evident partiality, exceeding the arbitrators' powers, or a serious
procedural defect. The appeal is not a fresh trial.

There is a dollar ceiling: the Board will not hear a dispute involving $50,000 or more,
and it will not hear a dispute over a fee that by law must be set or approved by a court
or tribunal. There is no published dollar floor. A dispute must be filed with the Board
within three years after the dispute arose, or the Board loses jurisdiction over it. If
the amount in dispute is $7,500 or less, the decision below the hearing-panel level is
usually final; above that amount, either side can request a three-member hearing panel.

This kit did not find a stated filing fee anywhere in the Board's own application
materials or its rules of procedure; the application form asks about costs and fees paid
to the lawyer, not a filing fee owed to the Board itself. Do not assume the process is
free, but this kit could not confirm a specific figure either way; ask the Bar's fee
dispute staff directly before you file. This kit also did not find a rule requiring a
lawyer to notify a client of the right to arbitrate before suing for fees, the way some
states require; treat that as unconfirmed rather than assuming it exists here.

If your dispute does not fit this program, for example because the amount is $50,000 or
more, your money route runs through the courts described in section 6, or through the
client protection fund in section 5 if the lawyer's conduct went beyond a billing
disagreement into dishonesty.

The Resolution of Fee Disputes Board's own page, including how the process works and
where to send an application, is at
<https://www.scbar.org/for-lawyers/networking/committees/resolution-of-fee-disputes-board/>.

## 4. Bar complaint process

South Carolina's lawyer discipline system is run by the Office of Disciplinary Counsel
(ODC), an office of the South Carolina Supreme Court, working with the Commission on
Lawyer Conduct. This is a separate track from the fee arbitration program above.

To file, a person submits a written, signed complaint to ODC by mail or in person; ODC
does not accept complaints by email. There is a separate optional complaint form. ODC's
own published description of its screening process says directly that a complaint will
be dismissed without an investigation if it "is a dispute over whether a lawyer has
earned a fee that has been paid but is more appropriately handled by the South Carolina
Bar's Resolution of Fee Disputes Board." In other words, if your problem is purely about
whether a bill was fair, filing it as a disciplinary complaint is likely to end in a
dismissal and a referral to the program in section 3, not an investigation. ODC treats
complaints that raise an actual ethics violation, dishonesty, or misconduct differently
from a plain billing disagreement.

Separately, the South Carolina Bar operates a Client Assistance Program (CAP), intended
for lower-level disputes and misunderstandings, such as a communication breakdown,
where the lawyer's conduct does not rise to an ethical violation. If your issue is mostly
that your lawyer will not return calls or explain what is happening, CAP may fit better
than a formal ODC complaint.

This kit did not find a stated time limit on filing an ODC complaint. What the process
will and will not produce matters more than any deadline: discipline is not a refund.
ODC's process can end in dismissal, a confidential letter of caution, a public reprimand,
suspension, or disbarment, depending on what is found. South Carolina is one of the
states where formal discipline can also include a monetary sanction: Rule 7(b)(5) of the
Rules for Lawyer Disciplinary Enforcement (Rule 413, SCACR) lists, among the sanctions
available for misconduct, "restitution to persons financially injured, repayment of
unearned or inequitable attorney's fees or costs advanced by the client, and
reimbursement to the Lawyers' Fund for Client Protection." This restitution power is
discretionary and only available after a full disciplinary process finds misconduct; do
not treat a bar complaint as a way to recover money on a predictable timetable.

Complaints go to the Office of Disciplinary Counsel; its filing instructions are at
<https://www.sccourts.org/about/lawyer-judicial-discipline/how-to-file-a-complaint/>.

## 5. Client protection fund

South Carolina's fund is the Lawyers' Fund for Client Protection, established by the
South Carolina Supreme Court under Rule 411, SCACR, and administered by a committee of
the South Carolina Bar made up of thirteen Bar members and one public member.

The Fund covers losses caused by a lawyer's "dishonest conduct," a term the Fund's own
Rules of Procedure define as wrongful acts such as defalcation, embezzlement, or the
wrongful taking or conversion of money or property. That definition specifically
includes "failure to refund fees in accordance with the Resolution of Fee Disputes Board
findings against the suspended or disbarred lawyer." Read that carefully: unlike some
states, South Carolina's own definition ties a fee-refund failure to a prior finding by
the fee arbitration Board against a lawyer who has already been suspended or disbarred,
rather than treating a plain failure to refund, standing alone, as automatically
qualifying. A simple billing dispute, without more, is not what this fund is for.

The Fund's Rules of Procedure also require that the lawyer has died, been disbarred or
suspended, resigned, disappeared, left the state, been adjudicated bankrupt or mentally
incompetent, or that the loss has otherwise been certified to the committee by the Bar
or the Commission on Lawyer Conduct as an appropriate case.

Exclusions, stated in the Fund's own rules: losses of the lawyer's spouse, other close
relatives, partners, associates, and employees; and losses already covered, to the
extent covered, by a bond, surety agreement, or insurance policy, including banking
institutions' losses recoverable under a banker's blanket bond.

The per-claimant cap is $40,000. The aggregate cap on claims paid out per dishonest
attorney is $200,000. Both figures come from the Fund's own Rules of Procedure, not from
a commercial summary.

An application must be received within three years of the date the applicant discovered
or reasonably should have discovered the dishonest conduct, and no application may be
considered more than six years after the date of the dishonest conduct itself, whichever
comes first. If an application is transferred to the Resolution of Fee Disputes Board
because it is more appropriately a fee dispute, that transfer tolls the Fund's own
filing deadline.

Awards from the Fund are entirely discretionary. The Fund's own required application
language states this in capital letters: reimbursement is "a matter of grace in the sole
discretion of the Committee administering the Fund and not a matter of right," and no
client has any right in the Fund "as a third party beneficiary or otherwise." The
committee weighs your own conduct contributing to the loss, your comparative hardship,
the total losses attributed to that lawyer, the Fund's available assets, and any other
source of recovery available to you.

The South Carolina Bar's public page describing the Fund, along with the disciplinary
complaint process it sits next to, is at
<https://www.scbar.org/for-the-public/quicklinks/help-with-your-attorney/>.

## 6. Small claims court

South Carolina does not have a court separately branded "small claims court." The
equivalent is magistrate's court, staffed by roughly 300 magistrates, one or more per
county, appointed by the Governor for four-year terms.

The jurisdictional dollar limit is $7,500, set by S.C. Code Ann. Section 22-3-10, and it
applies the same way statewide; this kit did not find a county-by-county variation in
that dollar figure. Section 22-3-10(1) covers "actions arising on contracts for the
recovery of money only," which is the category a fee-related dispute against your
lawyer would typically fall into, if it were not already routed through the fee
arbitration program in section 3 or excluded from magistrate's court by some other rule.

A party may be represented by an attorney in magistrate's court but is not required to
have one; self-represented litigants are common, and magistrate's court personnel will
help explain procedure and forms, though they cannot give legal advice. The filing fee
for issuing a summons and obtaining judgment in a civil action is $45 under S.C. Code
Ann. Section 8-21-1010(A)(6); other magistrate's court fees in that same statute cover
different kinds of filings and can differ from this figure, so confirm the fee for your
specific filing with the clerk. This kit did not find a South Carolina magistrate's
court rule specific to suing a lawyer.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A dispute over what you owed your lawyer, or what your lawyer owed you back, under your
fee agreement is a contract claim. S.C. Code Ann. Section 15-3-530(1) sets a three-year
period for "an action upon a contract, obligation, or liability, express or implied."
This kit did not find a separate, longer period for written contracts as distinct from
oral ones; the statute does not appear to split them for this purpose.

A legal malpractice claim is different from a fee dispute and this kit does not cover
running one. For reference only: South Carolina courts have held that legal malpractice
claims fall under the same general three-year period in Section 15-3-530(5), which
covers "any injury to the person or rights of another, not arising on contract and not
enumerated by law." The South Carolina Court of Appeals confirmed this directly, citing
its own earlier decision in Berry v. McLeod, in a published opinion available on the
court's own site. A discovery rule applies: under Section 15-3-535, the three-year clock
for a Section 15-3-530(5) claim starts when "the person knew or by the exercise of
reasonable diligence should have known that he had a cause of action," not necessarily
at the moment of the lawyer's error. This kit did not find a South Carolina statute of
repose specific to legal malpractice claims, separate from the discovery rule described
here; South Carolina does have a repose-type provision for medical malpractice, but that
provision applies to licensed health care providers, not lawyers, and this kit did not
extend it to legal malpractice claims.

Do not treat any of this as legal advice about your own deadline. If you suspect
malpractice rather than a billing or communication problem, that is outside what this
kit covers, and you should get your own lawyer's advice quickly given how short these
periods run.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

The note on Scope that precedes the numbered rules in Rule 407, SCACR, says directly
that violation of a rule "should not itself give rise to a cause of action against a
lawyer nor should it create any presumption in such a case that a legal duty has been
breached." The same note adds that the rules "are not designed to be a basis for civil
liability," and warns that their purpose "can be subverted when they are invoked by
opposing parties as procedural weapons." At the same time, the note says that because the
rules do establish standards of conduct, "a lawyer's violation of a Rule may be evidence
of breach of the applicable standard of conduct," which can matter in a malpractice case
even though the rule itself creates no separate claim. This tracks the ABA model
language closely; this kit did not find a South Carolina-specific departure from it.

In practical terms, a rule violation on its own does not give you a lawsuit against your
lawyer in South Carolina. Your actual routes to money are the ones covered above: a
contract claim over the fee itself, the fee arbitration program if it fits, the client
protection fund if the conduct was dishonest in the sense described in section 5, or a
separate malpractice claim if that applies and you get advice on it, which is outside
this kit.
