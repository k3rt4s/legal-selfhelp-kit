# Alaska: handling a dispute with your own lawyer

This section is for a person in Alaska who has a problem with their own lawyer over fees,
billing, communication, getting their file back, or an advance payment that was never
earned or never refunded. It does not cover legal malpractice claims or how to run one
yourself. Retrieved August 20, 2026.

What is different about Alaska:

- Alaska's fee arbitration program is mandatory for the lawyer once the client files a
  petition. The lawyer cannot refuse to arbitrate a client-initiated fee dispute. If a
  lawyer sues you for unpaid fees, the lawyer must also serve you written notice of your
  right to arbitrate at the same time, and failing to give that notice is grounds for the
  lawyer's own lawsuit to be dismissed. Read section 3.
- A written fee agreement is required any time a fee will exceed $1,000, not just for
  contingent fees. This is a real deviation from the bare reasonableness standard most
  states use. Read section 2.
- A small claims appeal in Alaska has its own, lower filing fee: $100, not the $250 fee
  charged for a general district-court civil appeal. Using the wrong figure overstates
  the true cost of appealing a small claims judgment. Read section 6.
- In 2025 the Alaska Supreme Court adopted the "continuous representation rule" for legal
  malpractice claims: the three-year clock does not start until the same lawyer's
  representation in that matter ends. Read section 7.
- Alaska's Client Protection Fund only pays for dishonest conduct such as embezzlement or
  conversion of client money. It does not cover negligence, incompetence, or an ordinary
  fee dispute, and payment is entirely discretionary, not a right. Read section 5.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct: numbering and adoption

Alaska's lawyer conduct rules are the Alaska Rules of Professional Conduct (ARPC),
adopted and amended by the Alaska Supreme Court through numbered Supreme Court Orders
(SCO). Alaska uses the standard ABA model numbering (Rule 1.1, Rule 1.5, Rule 1.16, and
so on) without a renumbering or chapter-prefix scheme, so a rule citation looks the same
in Alaska as it does in most other states.

The current comprehensive version of the ARPC was rescinded and repromulgated by SCO 1680,
effective April 15, 2009, which brought Alaska's rules in line with the ABA's Ethics 2000
revisions. Individual rules have been amended since on their own schedule. Rule 1.16, the
rule on ending representation, was most recently amended by SCO 2040, effective October
15, 2025, so it is worth double-checking you are reading the current text if you find an
older printout.

The Bar itself uses "ARPC" as a working abbreviation (for example, inside its own Bar
Rules, at Bar Rule 11.1(c)). "Alaska R. Prof. Conduct 1.5" or "ARPC 1.5" are both
recognizable citation forms.

Source: <https://courts.alaska.gov/rules/docs/prof.pdf>

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

Rule 1.5(a) lists the same eight reasonableness factors as the ABA model rule, in the same
order, with nothing added or removed.

The real deviation is Rule 1.5(b): if a fee will exceed $1,000, the lawyer must give the
client a written fee agreement, before or within a reasonable time after starting the
representation, describing the scope of the representation. This is broader than the ABA
model, which has no general written-fee-agreement mandate outside contingent fees.
Contingent fees must still be in writing under Rule 1.5(c), tracking the model rule.

Rule 1.15(c) requires a lawyer to deposit advance fees and expenses into a client trust
account and withdraw only as fees are earned or expenses are incurred. No separate
Alaska-specific "nonrefundable fee" or "earned upon receipt" provision was found beyond
this trust-accounting rule.

Rule 1.16(d) requires that, on termination, a lawyer take reasonably practicable steps to
protect the client, including surrendering papers and property the client is entitled to
and refunding any advance payment of a fee or expense that was not earned or incurred. The
same paragraph then says the lawyer "may retain papers relating to the client to the
extent permitted by other law." The rule does not itself give a lawyer a right to hold
your file for unpaid fees; it defers to whatever separate Alaska law on attorney's liens
exists. Rule 1.8(i)(1) separately confirms a lawyer may acquire "a lien authorized by law"
to secure a fee. This kit did not locate a specific Alaska statute or confirmed case
naming the scope of that lien; see the gap list in verification_ak.md. Do not assume a
lawyer can or cannot hold your file without checking that question with your own counsel.

Source: <https://courts.alaska.gov/rules/docs/prof.pdf>

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

This is the single most important section in this kit for a fee dispute. Alaska runs a
statewide Fee Dispute Resolution Program through the Alaska Bar Association, set out in
Alaska Bar Rules 34 through 42.

Arbitration is mandatory for the attorney once the client (or anyone legally responsible
for paying the fee) files a petition, per Bar Rule 34(b). The lawyer cannot refuse. It
remains the client's choice whether to start the process at all.

If a lawyer sues you in court to collect a fee, Bar Rule 39(a) requires the lawyer to
serve you, at the same time as the summons, a written "notice of client's right to
arbitrate or mediate." You then have 20 days from receiving that notice to file for
arbitration or mediation and stay the lawsuit; if you do not act within 20 days you waive
the right. If the lawyer fails to give you this notice at all, the rule states that
failure is grounds for the lawyer's own civil action to be dismissed.

The arbitrator's or panel's decision is binding on both parties. A losing party may ask a
superior court to review it, but review is limited to the narrow grounds set out in
Alaska's arbitration statutes, AS 09.43.120 through .180 or AS 09.43.500 through .595. A
binding award can be reduced to a court judgment.

There is no dollar ceiling on which fee disputes the program can hear. Dollar figures in
the rules ($5,000, and a further threshold at more than 8 hours of hearing time or
$50,000 in dispute) only control whether Bar Counsel assigns a single arbitrator or a
panel, not whether the dispute is eligible.

The filing deadline: Bar Rule 34(c)(4) excludes disputes over fees charged more than three
years earlier, unless the underlying civil claim over that amount could still be brought.
No separate filing fee for the petition itself was found in the rule text reviewed.

Source: <https://courts.alaska.gov/rules/docs/bar.pdf>

## 4. Bar complaint process

Complaints against a lawyer (called grievances) are filed with the Bar Counsel of the
Alaska Bar Association. Every member of the Bar has a duty to help a member of the public
prepare or file a grievance, and grievance forms are available from the Bar and from every
court clerk's office (Bar Rules 9(b) and 11(b)).

The filing deadline is generous and discovery-based: Bar Rule 18 gives you five years from
the time you discover, or reasonably should have discovered, the misconduct, with
traditional tolling, equity, and due process principles still available beyond that.

Bar Counsel investigates and can dismiss a grievance for lack of probable cause, refer a
fee-related grievance to the fee arbitration program described above, or refer it to a
mediator. Two tracks follow a finding of misconduct: an informal track, a written private
admonition issued by Bar Counsel with the approval of one Area Division member and no
public proceeding, and a formal track, a petition for a public hearing before a Hearing
Committee, with appeal rights to the Disciplinary Board and the Alaska Supreme Court.

Restitution is available: when a finding of misconduct is made, the Board may order
restitution to the person harmed, in addition to any other discipline, alongside possible
reimbursement to the Lawyers' Fund for Client Protection and a costs assessment (Bar Rule
16(c)).

Source: <https://courts.alaska.gov/rules/docs/bar.pdf>

## 5. Client protection fund

The Lawyers' Fund for Client Protection of the Alaska Bar Association is set out in Bar
Rules 45 through 60. It reimburses only losses caused by a lawyer's "dishonest conduct,"
defined as defalcation, embezzlement, or the wrongful taking or conversion of money,
property, or other things of value (Rule 45(e)). It does not cover negligence,
incompetence, malpractice generally, or a routine fee overcharge dispute.

The per-claim and per-year caps sit directly in the rule text, not in an outside
administrative history: under Rule 53(d), a claimant's loss from any one lawyer in any one
matter is capped at the lesser of $100,000 or 10 percent of the Fund at the time of the
award, and total payments to all claimants in one year cannot exceed 50 percent of the
Fund as of January 1 of that year. These figures were last amended by SCO 2037, effective
March 6, 2025.

Payment from the Fund is expressly "a matter of grace and not of right," made in the sole
discretion of the Alaska Bar Association; Rule 54 states no client or member of the public
has any right in the Fund as a third-party beneficiary or otherwise.

There is a filing deadline, and it is easy to miss because it sits inside the definitions
rule rather than a rule that sounds procedural. Rule 45(f)(4) makes a "reimbursable loss"
one where the claim "has been filed no later than three years after the claimant knew or
should have known of the dishonest conduct of the lawyer." Miss that three-year window and
the loss is not a reimbursable one under the rule's own definition, regardless of how strong
the underlying claim is. If you believe you have a dishonest-conduct loss, act well inside
three years of when you knew or should have known about it.

Source: <https://courts.alaska.gov/rules/docs/bar.pdf>

## 6. Small claims court

Small claims cases in Alaska are heard in district court under the Small Claims Rules,
Part II of the District Court Civil Rules (Rules 8 through 22). The jurisdictional limit
is $10,000, set by AS 22.15.040.

Unlike some states, Alaska allows a lawyer to represent a party in small claims court at
any stage, including on appeal, under Small Claims Rule 15(b). A corporation may also
appear through an authorized officer or employee without a lawyer.

Filing fees, per Alaska Administrative Rule 9(c)(2): $50 for a claim of $2,500 or less,
$100 for a claim of more than $2,500.

Appeal type: Small Claims Rule 18 says the superior court "shall grant trial de novo if
the proceedings in the district court were not of record." The Alaska Small Claims
Handbook (a court-published guide) confirms that small claims trials are, as standard
practice, audio-recorded, and that on appeal the superior court reviews the audio
recording, the trial exhibits, the court file, and the parties' legal memoranda rather
than hearing new evidence. In practice this means most small claims appeals proceed as an
on-the-record review, not as a brand-new trial, even though the rule's own wording is
conditional.

Appeal deadline: 30 days from the date the judgment is sent or given to you, confirmed
independently by the Small Claims Handbook and by Appellate Rule 602(a)(1) (30 days from
the date shown on the clerk's certificate of distribution of the judgment).

True total appeal cost: the filing fee for a notice of appeal from small claims court to
superior court is $100, under Administrative Rule 9(b)(4). That rule sets the general
district-court-to-superior-court appeal fee at $250, but carves out a separate, lower fee
of $100 "for any similar appeal from small claims court." Do not use the $250 general
appeal figure for a small claims appeal.

Sources: <https://courts.alaska.gov/rules/docs/dciv.pdf>,
<https://courts.alaska.gov/rules/docs/app.pdf>,
<https://courts.alaska.gov/rules/docs/adm.pdf>,
<https://public.courts.alaska.gov/web/forms/docs/sc-100.pdf>

## 7. Statutes of limitation

General contract claims: three years, under AS 09.10.053. General tort claims not arising
on contract, including personal injury and injury to the rights of another, are two years
under AS 09.10.070(a)(2).

Legal malpractice specifically runs on the three-year period, not the two-year tort
period. The Alaska Supreme Court confirmed this and, in the same decision, adopted a
"continuous representation rule" for when the claim accrues: a legal malpractice claim
does not accrue, and the three-year clock does not start, while the same attorney
continues to represent the client in the matter in which the malpractice is alleged. The
case is Holly Sheldon-Lee v. Birch Horton Bittner, Inc., David K. Gross, and Mara E.
Michaletz, Alaska Supreme Court Opinion No. 7757, Supreme Court No. S-18214 (decided March
21, 2025), 565 P.3d 985. This kit reached the court's own opinion document this session and
read the operative sentence directly: "We adopt the continuous representation rule to
determine when a legal malpractice claim accrues." The opinion's own footnote confirms the
malpractice limitation period runs on AS 09.10.053, the three-year contract statute, not the
two-year tort statute.

A separate ten-year statute of repose exists at AS 09.10.055, but by its own terms it
applies to personal injury, death, or property damage tied to construction and similar
matters. Nothing found this session extends that repose period to legal malpractice or
attorney-fee claims, so it is not stated here as applicable to a lawyer dispute.

This kit does not cover running a legal malpractice claim. These periods are here so you
know a clock exists and can get counsel in time if you need it, not so you can calculate
your own deadline, particularly given how fact-dependent the continuous representation
rule is.

## 8. Private right of action for RPC violations

The Scope section preceding the numbered Alaska Rules of Professional Conduct states that
violation of a rule "should not itself give rise to a cause of action against a lawyer nor
should it create any presumption... that a legal duty has been breached," and that the
rules "are not designed to be a basis for civil liability." The same Scope section states
that a rule violation may still be considered as evidence that a lawyer breached the
standard of care in a case that is otherwise a recognized claim, such as malpractice.

In practical terms, a bar complaint or a cited rule violation does not by itself put money
in your hands. The routes that can are: the fee arbitration program in section 3, a
straightforward contract claim over the fee itself under the limitation periods in section
7, the Client Protection Fund in section 5 if the lawyer's conduct was dishonest in the
sense described there, or a separate legal malpractice claim, which is outside what this
kit covers.

Source: <https://courts.alaska.gov/rules/docs/prof.pdf>
