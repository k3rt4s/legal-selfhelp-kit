# District of Columbia

For a person in the District of Columbia who is in a dispute with their own lawyer over fees,
billing, communication, getting a file back, or an unearned advance. Retrieval date for the
sourcing in this file is 2026-08-21. Read [national_baseline.md](national_baseline.md) first.

What is different in the District of Columbia:

- Fee arbitration is mandatory on the lawyer whenever a present or former client requests it.
  This is the single most useful fact in this pack: a DC resident can force their own lawyer
  into arbitration over a fee dispute just by asking. See section 3.
- The lawyer's side of that arbitration is mandatory by court rule; the client's side is
  ordinarily voluntary, so a client who does not want to arbitrate is not forced to. A client
  can also become bound the other way, by signing an Agreement to Arbitrate, or through a
  separately valid mandatory-arbitration clause already in the fee agreement.
- The DC Rules of Professional Conduct are Appendix A to a separate set of regulatory rules
  called the Rules Governing the District of Columbia Bar (Rules I through XVI). Both are
  adopted by the DC Court of Appeals but are published on the DC Bar's own website, not on the
  court's site.
- DC allows a contingent fee in a domestic relations case. Many states bar this; DC's own
  rule comment says so directly. DC still bars a contingent fee in a criminal defense case.
- DC's disciplinary process can order an attorney to pay restitution, to the injured person or
  to the Clients' Security Trust Fund, as a condition of probation or reinstatement. That is a
  real, if indirect and discretionary, money remedy inside the discipline system itself.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct, numbering and adoption

The DC Court of Appeals adopted the District of Columbia Rules of Professional Conduct
(D.C. RPC). Rule X of the Rules Governing the District of Columbia Bar states that "[t]he
District of Columbia Rules of Professional Conduct and any amendments thereto, appearing as
Appendix A, shall provide the standards governing the practice of law in the District of
Columbia," confirmed at
[dcbar.org, Rule X, Rules Governing the DC Bar](https://www.dcbar.org/about/who-we-are/rules-and-bylaws/rules-governing-the-district-of-columbia-bar/rule-x-rules-of-professional-conduct).
The most recent confirmed amendments were adopted by the Court on May 14, 2025 and took effect
September 15, 2025, changing Rule 1.6 (Confidentiality), Rule 1.7 (Conflict of Interest:
General Rule), Rule 1.16 (Declining or Terminating Representation), and Rule 5.6 (Restrictions
on Right to Practice).

Cite it in a letter as "D.C. Rule of Professional Conduct 1.5" or "D.C. RPC 1.5." The numbering
tracks the ABA model directly. Rule 1.4 (Communication), Rule 1.5 (Fees), Rule 1.15
(Safekeeping Property), and Rule 1.16 (Declining or Terminating Representation) are exactly
where a reader familiar with the model would expect them. I did not find a superseded-numbering
trap for DC of the kind some other states carry from an older code.

The regulatory and procedural side lives in a separate document, the Rules Governing the
District of Columbia Bar (Rules I through XVI), also adopted by the DC Court of Appeals. Both
the RPC and the Rules Governing the Bar are hosted for public reading on the DC Bar's own site,
dcbar.org, rather than on the court's own site, dccourts.gov, which links out to dcbar.org for
this material. That routing is a genuine quirk worth knowing before you go looking for the text
on the court's own website and do not find it there.

DC has a unified, mandatory bar: every lawyer admitted to practice in DC is a member of the DC
Bar and subject to its disciplinary jurisdiction for conduct covered by these rules.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

D.C. RPC 1.5(a) lists the same eight reasonableness factors as the ABA model, in the same
order, with no ninth or tenth factor added. If you are used to a state that added extra
factors, DC did not.

Two real deviations sit elsewhere in Rule 1.5, not in the factor list itself. First, Rule
1.5(b) makes the writing requirement mandatory rather than merely preferred: "the basis or
rate of the fee, the scope of the lawyer's representation, and the expenses for which the
client will be responsible shall be communicated to the client, in writing, before or within a
reasonable time after commencing the representation," whenever the lawyer has not regularly
represented the client. The word is "shall," not "should" or "preferably."

Second, Rule 1.5(d) prohibits a contingent fee only in a criminal defense matter. Comment [7]
to the rule says directly: "Contingent fees in domestic relations cases, while rarely
justified, are not prohibited by Rule 1.5. Contingent fees in such cases are permitted in
order that lawyers may provide representation to clients who might not otherwise be able to
afford to contract for the payment of fees on a noncontingent basis." Many other states bar a
contingent fee in both criminal defense and domestic relations matters; DC bars only the
former.

On whether a lawyer may hold a client's file to force payment of a disputed fee: DC's answer is
narrower than an outright ban and narrower than an outright right to withhold. Rule 1.16(d)
requires a lawyer, on termination, to take timely steps to protect the client's interests,
including surrendering papers and property to which the client is entitled, and Rule 1.16
Comment [11] adds that "where a lawyer has a valid lien covering undisputed amounts of property
or money, the lawyer may continue to hold such property or money to the extent permitted by the
substantive law governing the lien asserted," citing two DC Court of Appeals decisions, 667
A.2d 1350 (D.C. 1995) and 698 A.2d 412 (D.C. 1997), and cross-referencing Rule 1.8(i), the
rule that governs a lawyer's lien on client property generally. Comment [11] also requires the
lawyer to segregate any genuinely disputed portion of property or money, hold it in trust under
Rule 1.15, and promptly turn over the undisputed remainder. I did not independently fetch the
full text of Rule 1.8(i) itself; the scope of a permitted lien beyond what Comment [11] states
is not confirmed in this pack.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

DC runs one program, the Attorney/Client Arbitration Board (ACAB), administered by the DC Bar
under Rule XIII of the Rules Governing the District of Columbia Bar, at
[dcbar.org/for-the-public/resolve-attorney-problems/fee-dispute-program](https://www.dcbar.org/for-the-public/resolve-attorney-problems/fee-dispute-program).

The binding rule is asymmetric, and this is the most useful fact in the whole pack. Rule
XIII(a) reads: "An attorney subject to the disciplinary jurisdiction of this Court shall be
deemed to have agreed to arbitrate disputes over fees for legal services and disbursements
related thereto when such arbitration is requested by a present or former client," if the
client was a DC resident when the lawyer was engaged, or a substantial part of the services
were performed in DC, or the services included representation before a DC court or DC
government agency. In plain terms: if you qualify, and you ask, your lawyer cannot refuse.
Rule XIII(b) makes the resulting award "final and binding on the parties" and enforceable in
DC Superior Court.

The client's side is not automatically bound the same way. Under the ACAB's own Rules of
Procedure, Rule 8(a), if the client files the petition, the lawyer is deemed to have agreed and
the case proceeds once the lawyer responds. If instead the lawyer files first, the ACAB sends
the client a proposed Agreement to Arbitrate; the client is only bound if they sign it, and if
they refuse, "the ACAB cannot compel the client to do so" (Rule 8(b)(ii)). There is one
exception that can bind the client without a fresh signature: Rule 8(b)(iii) lets the ACAB
enforce a pre-existing attorney/client agreement to arbitrate fee disputes if that agreement is
valid and enforceable, signed by all parties, covers fee disputes, and the client was
adequately advised about it. DC Bar Legal Ethics Opinion 376 confirms this reading and traces
it through Comment [13] to Rule 1.8 (Conflict of Interest: Specific Rules), which the Court
promulgated after two earlier, now superseded ethics opinions had taken a more skeptical view
of mandatory-arbitration clauses in fee agreements.

Eligibility has no dollar floor or ceiling; I searched the ACAB Rules of Procedure for a
minimum or maximum amount in dispute and found none. The amount in dispute does control the
form of the tribunal: a claim of $10,000 or less, combined with any counterclaim, goes to a
single arbitrator; above $10,000 it goes to a three-member panel (ACAB Rule 13).

The filing deadline tracks DC's contract statute of limitations. ACAB Rule 3(e) requires the
petition to be filed "within the statute of limitations for contract disputes as defined by
District of Columbia law (D.C. Code Sec. 12-301). In general, a petition involving a fee
dispute must be filed with the ACAB within three years of the date the basis for the claim was
known or should have been known to the party making it." See section 7 for the underlying
statute.

There is a filing fee, on a sliding scale by amount in dispute, published at
[dcbar.org, fee dispute filing fees](https://www.dcbar.org/for-the-public/resolve-attorney-problems/fee-dispute-program/filing-fees):
$25 for claims up to $5,000; $50 for $5,001 to $49,999; $100 for $50,000 to $149,999; $250 for
$150,000 to $499,999; $500 for $500,000 to $999,999; and $1,000 for $1,000,000 and above. The
same schedule applies to a counterclaim. The fee is nonrefundable, but the ACAB may waive it on
a showing of indigency.

## 4. Bar complaint process

Complaints against DC lawyers go to the Office of Disciplinary Counsel (ODC), the chief
prosecutor for attorney discipline under Rule XI of the Rules Governing the DC Bar, at
[dcbar.org/attorney-discipline/office-of-disciplinary-counsel](https://www.dcbar.org/attorney-discipline/office-of-disciplinary-counsel/filing-a-complaint).
Formal proceedings run through the Board on Professional Responsibility and, ultimately, the DC
Court of Appeals. Both bodies sit at 515 5th Street NW, Building A, Suite 117, in the same
courthouse as the DC Court of Appeals.

You can file online or by mail. ODC reviews every complaint for jurisdiction, then assigns it
either to a preliminary inquiry or a full disciplinary investigation. You will get written
notice of the file number, of which track is used, and eventually of the disposition. Rule XI
lists the possible outcomes: dismissal, a private diversion agreement, an informal admonition
(public discipline), or formal charges leading to a hearing before a Hearing Committee, the
Board, and potentially the Court of Appeals, which can impose reprimand, suspension of up to
three years, or disbarment.

I found no separate consumer-assistance or non-disciplinary intake track distinct from ODC's
own screening step; ODC performs that screening itself, deciding whether a complaint falls
within its jurisdiction before assigning it further.

Unlike some states, DC's discipline system has a real, if limited and discretionary, money
remedy built in. Rule XI, section 3, allows the Court or the Board, when imposing discipline,
to "require an attorney to make restitution either to persons financially injured by the
attorney's conduct or to the Clients' Security Trust Fund (see Rule XII), or both, as a
condition of probation or of reinstatement." This is not something a complainant can demand as
of right, and a straightforward billing disagreement, without dishonest conduct, is unlikely to
lead to a disciplinary restitution order. Fee arbitration under section 3, or a Clients'
Security Fund claim under section 5, are the more direct paths to money back.

## 5. Client protection fund

The DC Bar's Clients' Security Fund (CSF) is administered by five trustees appointed by the DC
Court of Appeals under Rule XII of the Rules Governing the DC Bar, at
[dcbar.org, Clients' Security Fund](https://www.dcbar.org/for-the-public/resolve-attorney-problems/clients%E2%80%99-security-fund).

Coverage is limited to losses caused by the dishonest conduct of a DC Bar member acting as a
lawyer or in another fiduciary capacity. The Fund's own FAQ defines "dishonest conduct" as
theft, including embezzlement or the wrongful taking or conversion of money, property, or other
things of value, and states plainly that dishonest conduct "does not include the negligent,
inadequate, or ineffective performance of services." An ordinary billing dispute or a
malpractice claim, without theft, is not covered here; see sections 3 and 8 for the paths that
do apply to those.

To be considered, the loss must have been caused by a member's dishonest conduct, the member
must have represented the claimant as a lawyer or fiduciary, and the client must have already
tried other available ways to recover the loss, including demand on the lawyer, insurance, or a
lawsuit.

The reimbursement limit is $100,000 per claim, stated directly on the Fund's own page. I read
the full text of Rule XII itself and did not find any additional aggregate cap, either overall
or per lawyer, in the rule text; any such cap, if one exists, would live in separate Trustee
regulations I could not locate. Rule XII also states plainly that "[a]ll reimbursements shall
be a matter of grace and not of right," and that the Trustees "have complete discretion in
determining the order and manner of payment of claims" out of whatever funds are available. No
attorney may be compensated for prosecuting a claim against the Fund.

I found no fixed filing deadline stated in Rule XII's own text. The process starts with a call
to the Bar's Fund administrator; if the loss appears to fit, you receive a full application.
One volunteer trustee is assigned to your claim, decisions typically take about six months, and
the Trustees' decision is final.

## 6. Small claims court

DC's small claims court is the Small Claims Branch of the Civil Division of the DC Superior
Court, described at
[dccourts.gov, Civil Division, Small Claims Branch](https://www.dccourts.gov/superior-court/superior-court-divisions/civil-division/small-claims).
The jurisdictional limit is money damages of $10,000 or less; the branch's own page states
this directly. Relief in a Statement of Claim is limited to money, up to that $10,000 figure,
though you may include court costs, attorney's fees, and interest as part of that request.

The filing fee to start a case is tiered by the amount claimed, published on the court's own
civil filing fees page: $5.00 for a Statement of Claim up to $500, $10.00 for $500.01 to
$2,500, and $45.00 for $2,500.01 up to the $10,000 jurisdictional limit. A defendant may file a
set-off for no fee, or a counterclaim for a $10.00 filing fee; a counterclaim is not itself
limited to $10,000. Either side may demand a jury trial in writing before the first court date,
for a $75.00 fee unless a judge waives it; once a jury is demanded, the case moves out of the
Small Claims courtroom and is heard by an Associate Judge in the Civil Division, though papers
still go through the Small Claims Clerk's Office. A business filing as plaintiff must be
represented by a lawyer; an individual generally does not need one, on either side.

There is a real appeal route out of Small Claims, confirmed on the DC Court of Appeals' own
guide for self-represented small claims appellants, at
[dccourts.gov, Representing Yourself in a Small Claims Appeal](https://www.dccourts.gov/sites/default/files/matters-docs/AppealsSmallClaimsGuide.pdf).
If the order was signed by a Magistrate
Judge, you have 10 days to file a Motion for Review by an Associate Judge; if that is denied
or if an Associate Judge signed the order directly, you have only 3 days to file an
Application for Allowance of Appeal with the Court of Appeals Clerk's Office. The fee is
$10.00, waivable by motion, or waived automatically if you already had costs waived in the
Small Claims trial court. These applications are described as not granted very often; if granted, the case proceeds as
a regular civil appeal, with $40.00 more due. A denial can be challenged by a petition for
reconsideration within 7 days.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

DC Code section 12-301 is the general limitations statute, at
[code.dccouncil.gov, section 12-301](https://code.dccouncil.gov/us/dc/council/code/sections/12-301).
A simple contract, express or implied, must be sued on within 3 years, under subsection
(a)(7). A claim for which no other limitation is specifically prescribed also gets 3 years,
under the catch-all in subsection (a)(8). Legal malpractice is not given its own numbered
subsection in the statute; DC case law treats it under this general framework rather than a
malpractice-specific statute.

DC recognizes a discovery-based accrual rule in professional negligence cases, applied in
reported DC Court of Appeals decisions including Knight v. Furlow, 553 A.2d 1232 (D.C. 1989), a
case confirmed as real and correctly cited through the CourtListener case database. This pack
does not state the exact test that rule applies, because the underlying opinion text could not
be independently fetched and read in this pass. See the gap list in
[verification_dc.md](verification_dc.md) for what was looked for and where the search stopped.
I found no statute of repose provision in section 12-301 itself for either a contract claim or
a malpractice claim.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

A violation of the D.C. Rules of Professional Conduct does not by itself create a civil cause
of action. The Scope section of the rules themselves, Comment [4], states: "Nothing in these
Rules, the Comments associated with them, or this Scope section is intended to enlarge or
restrict existing law regarding the liability of lawyers to others... nothing in the Rules or
associated Comments or this Scope section is intended to confer rights on an adversary of a
lawyer to enforce the Rules in a proceeding other than a disciplinary proceeding." The same
Comment goes on: "Some judicial decisions have considered the standard of conduct established
in these Rules in determining the standard of care applicable in a proceeding other than a
disciplinary proceeding." That leaves room for a rule violation to support the standard-of-care
element of a separate malpractice claim, without itself being a standalone claim. Scope
Comment [3] separately confirms that a rule violation is "a basis for invoking the disciplinary
process," which is the channel covered in section 4.

The money routes available to a DC client stay the ones covered above: fee arbitration under
section 3 for a fee dispute, the Clients' Security Fund under section 5 for a loss caused by
dishonest conduct, and an ordinary contract or malpractice claim under section 7's limitation
periods for anything else.
