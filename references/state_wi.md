# Wisconsin: handling a dispute with your own lawyer

This section is for a person in Wisconsin who has a problem with their own lawyer over
fees, billing, communication, getting their file back, or an advance payment that was
never earned or never refunded. It does not cover legal malpractice claims or how to
run one yourself. Retrieved August 20, 2026.

What is different about Wisconsin:

- Wisconsin numbers its rules of professional conduct as Supreme Court Rules in Chapter
  20, cited "SCR 20:1.5," not just "Rule 1.5."
- Wisconsin gives a lawyer two different ways to hold an advance fee, and one of those
  ways comes with a written promise, built into the rule itself, that the lawyer will take
  an unresolved fee dispute to binding arbitration within 30 days. Read section 2 below.
- Wisconsin has a statewide fee arbitration program, but it is voluntary for both sides in
  most cases. It becomes effectively mandatory for the lawyer only in specific situations.
  Read section 3 before you assume you can force your lawyer into arbitration.
- Wisconsin's small claims dollar limit is not one number. It depends on what kind of
  claim you are bringing. A plain claim for money owed has a higher limit than a personal
  injury or tort claim filed in the same court.
- Bar discipline in Wisconsin can include an order of restitution, not just a reprimand or
  suspension. That is unusual. It is still discretionary and it is not the same thing as
  suing for your money.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct: numbering and adoption

Wisconsin's rules of professional conduct for lawyers are Supreme Court Rules, adopted
and amended by the Wisconsin Supreme Court, and organized as Chapter 20 of the
Supreme Court Rules. The current version of Chapter 20 was adopted by Wisconsin
Supreme Court Order 2007 WI 4 (case No. 04-07), effective July 1, 2007, which repealed
and recreated Chapter 20 based on a proposal from the state's Ethics 2000 Committee.
Chapter 20 has been amended since then; the version referenced here carries an effective
date of July 1, 2023.

Cite these rules to your lawyer or in a letter as "SCR 20:1.5," "SCR 20:1.4," and so on,
using the colon between the chapter number and the rule number. That is the form the
court itself uses. You will sometimes see "Rule 1.5" used loosely, but "SCR 20:1.5" is the
correct citation in Wisconsin.

The official publication point for the current rule text is the Wisconsin court system's own
site, wicourts.gov, not the legislature's statute site. The legislature's site used to publish
Supreme Court Rules but no longer does; it now points readers to the courts' own site.
Chapter 20 is split there into two parts by document, one covering the Preamble through
Rule 1.18, the other covering Rules 2.1 through 8.5. Numbering itself does not deviate
from the ABA model. A reader looking for 1.5, 1.4, 1.15, or 1.16 will find them at those
same numbers.

The rules themselves are published on the court system's own site at
<https://www.wicourts.gov/supreme/sc_rules.jsp>. Start there rather than at the legislature's
statute site, which no longer carries them.

## 2. How Wisconsin's fee rule differs from the model

SCR 20:1.5(a) lists the same eight reasonableness factors as the ABA model rule: time and
labor, the difficulty of the matter, the customary local fee, the amount involved and
results obtained, time limits imposed by the client or circumstances, the length of the
relationship, the lawyer's experience and reputation, and whether the fee is fixed or
contingent. Wisconsin does not add to or subtract from that list.

Where Wisconsin goes further than the model is what a lawyer must do with money you
pay in advance. SCR 20:1.5(b) requires the scope of representation and the fee basis to be
communicated to you in writing before or shortly after the lawyer takes you on, unless the
total cost is reasonably expected to be $1,000 or less, in which case an oral agreement is
allowed. If the total cost is expected to be more than $1,000, the purpose and effect of any
retainer or advance fee must also be in writing.

Wisconsin then gives the lawyer a choice for how to hold an advance fee payment.

- Under SCR 20:1.5(f), the default rule, any unearned advance fee must be held in the
  lawyer's trust account until it is actually earned.
- Under SCR 20:1.5(g), a lawyer may instead deposit your advance fee directly into the
  lawyer's own business account, but only if the lawyer gives you specific written notice
  when accepting the money, and only if the lawyer follows through on specific promises
  later. The written notice required at the start must state the amount of the advance, the
  fee basis or rate, any expenses you will be responsible for, the lawyer's obligation to
  refund any unearned part of the advance with an accounting when the representation
  ends, the lawyer's obligation to submit any unresolved fee dispute to binding arbitration
  within 30 days of getting your written notice of the dispute, and your ability to file a
  claim with the Wisconsin Lawyers' Fund for Client Protection if the lawyer fails to refund
  an unearned advance. When the representation ends, the lawyer must give you a final
  accounting, refund any unearned advance, and tell you that you have 30 days from that
  accounting to dispute the amount in writing. If you do dispute it and the lawyer cannot
  resolve it with you, the lawyer must submit the dispute to binding arbitration with the
  State Bar Fee Arbitration Program or a similar local bar program within 30 days of
  receiving your written notice.

This matters to you directly. If your lawyer used the business-account method under SCR
20:1.5(g) for your advance fee, the lawyer has already agreed, in writing, to take an
unresolved dispute over that money to binding arbitration on a fixed timetable. Look at
whatever written notice your lawyer gave you when you paid the advance. If it reads like
the notice SCR 20:1.5(g) describes, you may be able to hold the lawyer to that 30-day
promise directly.

SCR 20:1.5(h) adds one more consumer-facing protection: before a lawyer can pay
themselves out of trust funds for fees already earned (other than a contingent fee or a
court-ordered fee), the lawyer must send you an itemized bill or accounting and notice of
the amount and date of the planned withdrawal at least five business days in advance.

On ending the relationship and returning your file, SCR 20:1.16(d) requires a lawyer, on
termination, to take reasonably practicable steps to protect your interests: reasonable
notice, time to find new counsel, surrendering papers and property you are entitled to,
and refunding any advance payment of fee or expense that was not earned or incurred.
The official Wisconsin comment to this rule notes that a State Bar ethics opinion (Formal
Op. E-95-4, 1995) concludes Wisconsin lawyers do not have a "retaining lien," meaning a
lawyer generally cannot hold your file hostage against unpaid fees. That is worth quoting
back to a lawyer who refuses to release your file until you pay a disputed bill.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

Wisconsin runs a single statewide program: the Fee Arbitration Program of the State Bar
of Wisconsin, administered through its Committee on Resolution of Fee Disputes. It is not
a court program. The State Bar of Wisconsin is the state's mandatory (integrated) bar
association, meaning every lawyer licensed here must belong to it, but the arbitration
program itself is run by the bar's committee structure, not by a judge.

Participation is generally voluntary for both the lawyer and the client. Either side can
request arbitration, but the other side has to agree before a hearing happens, and either
side can decline. There are two situations where it stops being voluntary for the lawyer:
if you were referred to the lawyer through the State Bar's own Lawyer Referral and
Information Service, the lawyer must agree to arbitration; and if your advance fee was
held under the SCR 20:1.5(g) business-account method described above, the lawyer has
already committed in writing to submit an unresolved dispute to this program (or a
similar local bar program) within 30 days.

Once both sides agree to arbitrate, the process is binding, subject only to the limited
grounds for vacating or modifying an arbitration award under Wisconsin's arbitration
statute, chapter 788. There is no dollar floor or ceiling on what the Committee will hear;
disputes of $7,500 or less are generally assigned a single arbitrator, disputes over $7,500
generally get a three-member panel, and the Committee's chair has discretion to route
claims under $2,000 to a statewide panel. The Committee will not accept an application if
the lawyer was not licensed in Wisconsin, the services were performed outside Wisconsin,
there was no attorney-client relationship, the fee was fixed or approved by statute or
court order (for example a guardian ad litem fee), or the claim does not appear to involve
a genuine fee dispute (for example if a lawsuit over the fee has already been filed).

Filing costs a set fee based on the amount in dispute: $25 if the disputed amount is
$3,000 or less, $50 if it is more than $3,000 but less than $20,000, and $100 if it is
$20,000 or more. Each party pays this fee separately, and it is not refunded if the other
side declines to arbitrate. The Program's own materials describe no separate fee waiver.
The whole process typically takes three to six months from application to decision;
payment or refund under the award is due within 30 days of the decision being mailed.

No specific filing deadline for requesting arbitration itself was found in the Program's
published rules. The practical outer limit is likely the ordinary contract limitation period
described in section 7 below, since a fee agreement dispute is a contract claim, but this
kit did not find a rule stating that directly for the arbitration program, so treat it as
unconfirmed and do not wait to find out.

If Wisconsin's program does not fit your situation, for example because your lawyer
declines to arbitrate and was not required to, your money route runs through small
claims or circuit court for the amount in dispute, described in section 6, or through the
client protection fund in section 5 if the lawyer's conduct went beyond a billing
disagreement into dishonesty.

The State Bar's own page for the Fee Arbitration Program, including how to request it, is at
<https://www.wisbar.org/forPublic/IHaveaDisputeWithMyLawyer/Pages/Fee-Arbitration-Program.aspx>.

## 4. Filing a bar complaint

Wisconsin's lawyer discipline system is run by the Office of Lawyer Regulation (OLR), an
agency created by and operating under the Wisconsin Supreme Court, not by the State Bar
of Wisconsin. That distinction matters: the fee arbitration program above is a State Bar
program, while OLR is a court agency with authority to investigate and prosecute
misconduct.

To start the process, a person submits a grievance to OLR. OLR's director investigates any
grievance that presents enough information to support a possible finding of cause to
proceed; a lawyer who is the subject of an investigation must fully and fairly respond
within 20 days of being asked. A grievance must be brought to OLR's attention within six
years after the person knew or reasonably should have known of the conduct, though that
period does not run while the person is under a legal disability or while the lawyer
concealed or misled them about the conduct.

A bar complaint is not the way to get your money back in most cases, and OLR's own
process is disciplinary, not compensatory. That said, Wisconsin is unusual: discipline
under SCR 21.16 can include an order of restitution, either paying the client back directly
for money or property the lawyer misappropriated, or reimbursing the Wisconsin Lawyers'
Fund for Client Protection for what it already paid out on that claim. This restitution
order does not limit or replace any separate civil claim for amounts beyond what is
ordered. Do not count on this happening. It requires a finding of misconduct through the
full disciplinary process, and it is one possible outcome among several, not a guarantee.

Wisconsin calls this filing a grievance rather than a complaint. The court system's instructions
for filing one against a lawyer are at
<https://www.wicourts.gov/services/public/lawyerreg/filegrievance.htm>.

## 5. Client protection fund

Wisconsin's fund is the Wisconsin Lawyers' Fund for Client Protection, established in 1981
(originally called the Clients' Security Fund) and operated by the State Bar of Wisconsin
under Supreme Court Rule chapter 12. It is administered by a committee of five lawyers
and two nonlawyer members appointed by the State Bar's president.

The Fund covers losses caused by an attorney's "dishonest conduct," which the rule
defines narrowly as: a willful act causing loss through defalcation or embezzlement of
money, the intentional taking or conversion of money or property, or the failure to refund
an unearned advanced fee. That last category is worth noting directly: in Wisconsin, a
lawyer's plain failure to refund money you were owed after your advance fee went
unearned can itself qualify as reimbursable dishonest conduct. You do not necessarily
need the lawyer to already be disciplined or disbarred first; the rule lists "failed to refund
an unearned advanced fee" as its own qualifying condition, alongside death, bankruptcy,
disbarment, suspension, license revocation by consent, becoming a judgment debtor, a
related criminal conviction, or the lawyer disappearing.

The Fund excludes: losses belonging to the lawyer's own spouse, child, parent,
grandparent, sibling, partner, associate, or employee; losses already covered by a bond,
surety, or insurance policy to the extent covered; losses a bank could recover under its
own blanket bond; losses recoverable from some other source; and any loss barred by an
applicable statute of limitations. Ordinary attorney negligence or malpractice that does
not meet the narrow "dishonest conduct" definition above does not qualify for this fund at
all; that kind of claim belongs in a malpractice case, which this kit does not cover.

The committee is directed by rule to set, by its own separate rule, the maximum amount
any one claimant may recover and may also set an aggregate cap per dishonest attorney.
The Supreme Court Rule itself does not state that dollar figure, and the committee's own
separate rule setting it was not found published online. The best figure this kit could
find is a per-claimant maximum of $150,000, raised from a prior maximum of $75,000 in
August 2009. Three separate State Bar of Wisconsin sources agree on that number and none
reports a later change: the 2009 State Bar article announcing the increase, the Fund's own
official annual report for fiscal year 2017-18 showing an actual claim paid at that
maximum, and a State Bar consumer FAQ page that is live today. That is not the same as
reading the current rule itself, so this figure is not fully confirmed. No aggregate
per-attorney cap figure could be located at all; see the gap list in the verification
file. Confirm the current maximum directly with the Fund before relying on any number
here.

No filing deadline specific to the Fund itself was found beyond the general rule that a loss
already barred by an applicable statute of limitations is not reimbursable, and a rule that
any claim not fully paid within five years of being made has its unpaid balance closed out.
File as soon as you can rather than relying on either of those as a safe margin.

There is a practical timing point the rules do not tell you. The committee that decides claims
meets three times a year, not on demand, and it publishes an application deadline that falls
roughly two months before each meeting, with a separate later deadline for the attorney's
response. So the real cost of filing late is not usually a bar, it is waiting for the next
meeting. The Fund's page publishes the current meeting date and the two deadlines that go with
it, so check them before you assume you have time:
<https://www.wisbar.org/forPublic/IHaveaDisputeWithMyLawyer/Pages/Client-Protection-Fund.aspx>.
That page is also where you ask what the current maximum is, and where the claim form lives.
Questions and claims go to WLFCP@wisbar.org.

Awards from the Fund are entirely discretionary, not a matter of right, and the committee
weighs the size of your loss against other pending claims, your own hardship, any
negligence of your own that contributed to the loss, and whether you have another source
of recovery. No attorney representing a claimant before the committee may be paid from
the fund for that representation.

## 6. Small claims court

Wisconsin does not have a separately named "small claims court." Small claims cases are
filed in circuit court, using the simplified procedure in Chapter 799 of the Wisconsin
Statutes.

The dollar limit is not one number; it depends on the type of claim, under Wis. Stat.
799.01(1). A claim for a money judgment only, which is what a fee dispute usually is, is
limited to $10,000 or less, rising to $15,000 for cases filed on or after January 1, 2027
under a 2025 law change. A personal injury or other tort claim is capped lower, at $5,000
or less, and that tort cap is not changing. Eviction actions and claims for return of earnest
money on a real estate purchase must use this procedure regardless of amount. If your
dispute with your lawyer is about money owed, it is the higher, non-tort limit that applies.

A party may appear personally or through an attorney regularly authorized to practice in
Wisconsin; the statute does not restrict lawyers from appearing in small claims cases, and
a business party may also appear through a member, agent, or authorized employee.
Appeal from a small claims judgment goes to the Wisconsin Court of Appeals under Wis.
Stat. 799.30. The filing fee for a small claims action under Wis. Stat. 814.62(3)(a) is $22 as
of this writing, rising to $39 for filings on or after November 1, 2026 under a separate
2025 law change. This kit did not confirm whether county-level filing procedures vary
beyond the statewide fee and dollar limits above; check with your county's clerk of circuit
court.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A dispute over what you owed your lawyer, or what your lawyer owed you back, under
your fee agreement is a contract claim. Wisconsin does not split contract claims by written
versus oral form for this purpose: Wis. Stat. 893.43 sets a single six-year period for an
action on any contract, express or implied, and the statute specifically names actions to
recover fees for professional services as falling under this same six-year period.

A legal malpractice claim is different from a fee dispute and this kit does not cover
running one. For reference only: Wisconsin's own statutory annotations to Wis. Stat.
893.53, the state's general and residual period for injury to rights not arising on contract,
identify that section as the one applied to legal malpractice claims that sound in tort,
citing Acharya v. Carroll (Wisconsin Court of Appeals, 1989). That period is three years.
The same annotations describe a "discovery rule" applied to malpractice claims, meaning
the clock can start when the client discovered or should have discovered the injury rather
than strictly at the moment of the lawyer's error, citing Hennekens v. Hoerl (Wisconsin
Supreme Court, 1991). This kit did not independently verify the text of those opinions;
the case names come from the state's own official annotated statutes, not from reading
the opinions themselves.

Do not treat any of this as legal advice about your own deadline. If you suspect
malpractice rather than a billing or communication problem, that is outside what this kit
covers, and you should get your own lawyer's advice quickly given how short these
periods run.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

Wisconsin's rules of professional conduct say directly, in the Scope section that precedes
the numbered rules, that violating a rule does not by itself create a cause of action against
a lawyer and does not create a presumption that a legal duty was breached. The comment
states plainly that the rules are not designed to be a basis for civil liability, and that their
purpose can be undermined if opposing parties try to use them as procedural weapons.
At the same time, the same comment says a lawyer's violation of a rule may be evidence of
a breach of the applicable standard of conduct, which can matter in a malpractice case
even though the rule itself creates no separate claim.

In practical terms, a rule violation on its own does not give you a lawsuit against your
lawyer. Your actual routes to money are the ones covered above: a contract claim over the
fee itself, the fee arbitration program, the client protection fund if the conduct was
dishonest in the sense described in section 5, or a separate malpractice claim if that
applies and you get advice on it, which is outside this kit.
