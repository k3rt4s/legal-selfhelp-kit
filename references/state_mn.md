# Minnesota: handling a dispute with your own lawyer

This section is for a person in Minnesota who has a problem with their own lawyer over
fees, billing, communication, getting their file back, or an advance payment that was
never earned or never refunded. It does not cover legal malpractice claims or how to
run one yourself. Retrieved August 20, 2026.

What is different about Minnesota:

- Minnesota does not run a single statewide fee arbitration program. Fee arbitration
  happens through local district bar associations, and a lawyer cannot be forced into it.
  Participation is voluntary on the lawyer's side, not just the client's. Read section 3
  before you count on arbitration to get money back.
- Minnesota's rule on advance fees bans the words "nonrefundable" and "earned upon
  receipt" in a fee agreement, and requires specific written disclosures for flat fees and
  availability fees. A lawyer who took a flat-fee advance and did little or no work still
  owes you the unearned part back. Read section 2.
- Minnesota's discipline regulator is split into two separate offices with two
  separate websites: the Office of Lawyers Professional Responsibility (OLPR)
  investigates and pursues discipline, and the Lawyers Professional Responsibility Board
  (LPRB) adjudicates. Both are administered by the state's court system, not the bar
  association.
- Minnesota's malpractice statute of limitations uses a "some damage" rule, not a pure
  discovery rule. The clock can start running the moment you suffer any damage, even if
  you have not yet discovered the lawyer's error. That makes the deadline harsher than in
  many other states.
- Minnesota's small claims court is called conciliation court, and its general dollar
  limit is $20,000, not a smaller figure repeated in some general online summaries.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct: numbering and adoption

Minnesota's rules of professional conduct for lawyers are the Minnesota Rules of
Professional Conduct (MRPC), adopted and amended by the Minnesota Supreme Court. The
current version is effective September 1, 1985, with amendments effective through July 1,

2026. Numbering follows the ABA model rule numbers; a reader looking for Rule 1.5, 1.4,

1.15, or 1.16 will find them at those same numbers.

Cite these rules in a letter as "Rule 1.5, Minnesota Rules of Professional Conduct" or
"MRPC 1.5." Both forms are used by Minnesota's own regulator.

The official publication point for the current rule text is the Office of the Revisor of
Statutes, which publishes Minnesota's court rules on the state's behalf, at
<https://www.revisor.mn.gov/court_rules/pr/subtype/cond/>. The rules are also mirrored,
without an independent publication date of their own, on the Lawyers Professional
Responsibility Board's site.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

Rule 1.5(a) of the Minnesota Rules of Professional Conduct lists the same eight
reasonableness factors as the ABA model rule: time and labor, the difficulty of the
questions involved, the skill required, whether taking the case precluded other work, the
customary local fee, the amount involved and results obtained, time limitations imposed
by the client or circumstances, the length of the professional relationship, the lawyer's
experience and reputation, and whether the fee is fixed or contingent. Minnesota does not
add to or subtract from that list.

Where Minnesota goes well beyond the model rule is Rule 1.5(b), which regulates advance
fees directly and in detail:

- Fee payments a lawyer receives before doing the work are presumed unearned and must be
  held in a trust account under Rule 1.15, unless one of the two exceptions below applies.
- A lawyer may charge a flat fee that becomes the lawyer's own property on payment, but
  only if the client signed a written agreement disclosing the scope of services, the
  total fee and payment terms, the fact that the money will not sit in trust until
  earned, the client's right to end the relationship, and the client's right to a refund
  of any part of the fee for services not actually provided.
- A lawyer may separately charge an "availability fee," a reasonable fee just to keep the
  lawyer available to the client during a period or for a matter, apart from fees for
  actual legal work. This also requires a signed writing, must clearly state it covers
  availability only, and is refundable in whole or in part if the lawyer is not actually
  available as promised.
- Rule 1.5(b)(3) directly bans describing any fee as "nonrefundable" or "earned upon
  receipt." A fee agreement can say the advance is the lawyer's property, but the money
  still comes back if the representation ends before it is fully earned. If a client
  disputes how much was actually earned, the lawyer must take reasonable and prompt
  action to resolve the dispute.

This matters directly if your dispute is about an advance the lawyer took and stopped
working on. If your written fee agreement used the word "nonrefundable," that language
itself does not comply with Minnesota's rule, and it does not defeat your right to a
refund of whatever part of the fee was not earned.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration: THE BIG ONE

This is the section that changes what you should actually do next, so read it before you
assume any path will work.

Minnesota does not run one statewide, court-administered fee arbitration program.
Instead, local district bar associations maintain their own fee arbitration panels. This
kit could directly confirm one such program still operating: the Ramsey County Bar
Association's fee arbitration service, described at
<https://mnbars.org/?pg=dispute-with-an-attorney>. That page states the service is free
to all participants and is designed to help Ramsey County attorneys and clients resolve
legal fee disputes, with links to program rules and a petition form that could not be
retrieved for this kit (see the gap list in the verification file).

The single most important fact about Minnesota fee arbitration is who it binds. Under a
2011 article by the Director of the Office of Lawyers Professional Responsibility,
published on the official regulator's own site, participation in fee arbitration in
Minnesota is voluntary on the lawyer's part, not mandatory. A pilot program that made fee
arbitration mandatory for lawyers was adopted by the Minnesota Supreme Court for three
district bar associations, including Ramsey County, but the Court allowed that pilot to
lapse in 1999, and the article's author states he was not aware of any effort to revive
it. That source is dated 2011 and is not itself the current program rules, so treat the
"still voluntary today" conclusion as likely but not freshly confirmed; see the
verification file. The Rules on Lawyers Professional Responsibility, the court rules that
govern the discipline system, do not mention fee arbitration at all; this kit checked
Rule 6 and Rule 15 directly and found no reference to it. Fee arbitration sits entirely
outside the court rule scheme, run by bar associations, so there is no rule text to check
this claim against either way; the 2011 article is the only source this kit found for
whether it binds a lawyer. Source, a Wayback Machine snapshot of the OLPR Director's
article dated 2024-06-29:
<http://web.archive.org/web/20240629151731/https://lprb.mncourts.gov/articles/Articles/Fee%20Disputes.pdf>.

What that means in practice: if your lawyer refuses to arbitrate a fee dispute, Minnesota
gives you no rule forcing them into it, unlike states that make arbitration mandatory on
the lawyer once the client asks. Your fallback routes are the ones described in sections
4, 5, and 6 below, contract negotiation, a bar complaint if there is more than a billing
disagreement, the Client Security Board if the conduct was dishonest, or conciliation
court or district court for the money itself.

This kit could not confirm, for any specific district program including Ramsey County's,
the dollar floor or ceiling the program will hear, the filing deadline, the filing fee
itself beyond "free" for Ramsey County, whether an award is binding on either side, or
whether a lawyer must notify a client of the right to arbitrate before suing for fees.
Those program-level details did not resolve to a source that this kit could fetch and
read directly; they are recorded as a gap rather than guessed at. If you are in a
different Minnesota county, start by asking your county or district bar association
whether it runs a fee arbitration program at all, since this kit did not attempt to
confirm one for every county.

One more official OLPR practice worth knowing: the Board's own guidelines let the
Director's Office summarily dismiss a complaint, or part of one, that is really only a
fee dispute better handled through arbitration or other civil action, and the Director's
Office will give the complainant contact information for the local arbitration committee
when it does. That is consistent with the discipline system not being a substitute for
arbitration, and it is why section 4 below says plainly that discipline is not a refund.

## 4. Bar complaint process

Minnesota's attorney discipline function is split across two related but separate
bodies, both created by and operating under the Minnesota Supreme Court. The Office of
Lawyers Professional Responsibility (OLPR) investigates and pursues discipline. The
Lawyers Professional Responsibility Board (LPRB) is the adjudicative body and does not
investigate complaints itself. A client files a complaint with OLPR, not the LPRB.

You file a complaint with OLPR using its online submission form or by printed form mailed
in, at <https://olpr.mncourts.gov/filing-a-complaint/>. This kit did not find a stated
time limit for filing a complaint on OLPR's complaint or rules pages; treat that as
unconfirmed rather than as proof there is no deadline at all.

Under Rule 6 of the Rules on Lawyers Professional Responsibility (RLPR), a complaint
submitted to OLPR is referred to the District Committee for the district where the
lawyer's principal office is located for investigation, unless the Director chooses to
investigate it directly or decides discipline is not warranted. Source:
<https://www.revisor.mn.gov/court_rules/pr/subtype/rule/id/6/>. As described in section 3
above, the Director's Office has guidelines that let it summarily dismiss allegations
that amount only to a fee dispute and redirect the complainant to local fee arbitration
instead of investigating them as discipline.

Discipline is usually not a refund. This kit searched the full text of every numbered
rule in the Rules on Lawyers Professional Responsibility (Rules 1 through 30, at
<https://www.revisor.mn.gov/court_rules/pr/subtype/rule/id/1/> through
.../30/) for any restitution mechanism, meaning a rule letting the disciplinary system
order a lawyer to pay a client back. No rule using the words "restitution," "repay," or
"reimburse" as a remedy owed to a client was found. Rule 15(a) does let the Minnesota
Supreme Court, on concluding a disciplinary proceeding, disbar, suspend, place a lawyer on
probation with conditions, reprimand, order the lawyer to pay costs, or take several other
listed actions, and Rule 15(b) separately lets the Court "issue orders as may be
appropriate for the protection of clients or other persons" when a lawyer is disciplined
or resigns, which is broad enough that it could in theory include a client-protective
order, but no rule text or published case describes that clause being used to order money
paid to a client, and this kit did not find one that says so. Treat Minnesota discipline
as not producing a refund. Your money routes are fee arbitration where available, the
Client Security Board described next, or civil court.

## 5. Client protection fund

Minnesota's fund is the Lawyers Professional Responsibility Fund, administered by the
Minnesota Client Security Board (CSB), a body appointed by the Minnesota Supreme Court.
Its public site is <https://csb.mncourts.gov/>, with its own rules published at
<https://csb.mncourts.gov/rules/> and a claim intake page at
<https://csb.mncourts.gov/file-a-claim/>.

The Fund covers losses caused by a lawyer's dishonest conduct arising from an
attorney-client relationship, or from a lawyer acting as a fiduciary or escrow agent in
that relationship. It excludes professional negligence or malpractice, fee disputes,
consequential damages, and losses from unwise investments placed through a lawyer.

The maximum the Board may pay on a single claim is $150,000, and the Board describes its
payment authority as a matter of grace rather than of right. This kit could not locate any
published aggregate cap on total payments the Board might make across multiple claims
against the same lawyer, only the $150,000 per-claim limit. Under Rule 3.14 of the CSB's
own rules, when the Board decides whether and how much to pay on a claim, it may consider
several factors including "the total amount of losses caused by the dishonest conduct of
any one lawyer," which is a discretionary factor in that decision, not a stated dollar
cap.

A claim must be filed within five years of the date the claimant knew or should have known
of the lawyer's dishonest conduct. The Board typically waits until any pending
disciplinary complaint against the lawyer is resolved before investigating and deciding a
claim, but a claimant must still file within the five-year window regardless of whether a
disciplinary matter is pending. Claims can be filed online, by mail, or by email.

Source for this section, csb.mncourts.gov's own rules and FAQ pages, both fetched
directly: <https://csb.mncourts.gov/rules/> and
<https://csb.mncourts.gov/frequently-asked-questions/>.

## 6. Small claims: conciliation court

Minnesota's small claims court is called conciliation court. The general jurisdictional
limit is $20,000, under Minn. Stat. section 491A.01, subd. 3a, with a narrower $4,000 limit for
claims arising from consumer credit transactions specifically, source
<https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/491A.01>. Note that some search results online
describe a lower general figure; this kit confirmed $20,000 directly against the statute's
own text.

A person may be represented by a lawyer in conciliation court; the rule against
non-lawyer representation applies to non-lawyer agents, not to lawyers themselves. Source,
a Wayback Machine snapshot of the Minnesota court system's own conciliation court FAQ page
dated 2026-06-08, because the live mncourts.gov page could not be reached directly by this
kit's retrieval method:
<http://web.archive.org/web/20260608044604/https://mncourts.gov/help-topics/conciliation-court/faqs>.

The filing fee is set under Minn. Stat. section 357.022, a separate fee statute, together with
any applicable law library fee, rather than being stated as a fixed figure inside the
conciliation court statute itself. Confirm the current amount with your county court
administrator or the fee schedule published at revisor.mn.gov before filing. This kit did
not confirm whether the $20,000 general limit or the $4,000 consumer-credit limit varies
by county; Minn. Stat. section 491A.01 as fetched states the limits as statewide figures. Either
side that disagrees with a conciliation court judgment may remove the matter for a trial
in district court, a right this kit did not independently trace to its own statutory
citation within the scope of this task.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A dispute over what you owed your lawyer, or what your lawyer owed you back, under your
fee agreement is a contract claim. Minn. Stat. section 541.05, subd. 1(1) sets a six-year period
for an action on a contract or other obligation, express or implied, where no other
limitation is expressly stated. Source: <https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/541.05>.

Minnesota does not carry a separate, numbered statute specific to legal malpractice.
Instead, the same six-year statute's catch-all clause at subd. 1(5), covering "any other
injury to the person or rights of another, not arising on contract, and not hereinafter
enumerated," is the provision that has been applied to malpractice claims sounding in
tort. Because Minnesota's contract catch-all and its general tort catch-all both sit
inside this same six-year statute, whether a malpractice claim is characterized as
sounding in contract or in tort matters less in Minnesota than in states where the two
periods differ; either characterization lands on the same six-year period under this
statute's text.

Minnesota's accrual rule for legal malpractice claims is harsher than a simple discovery
rule. This kit did not find, and did not attempt to independently verify, the text of the
governing case itself; it relies on case metadata only. Per the case metadata for Antone
v. Mirviss, 720 N.W.2d 331 (Minn. 2006), retrieved through the CourtListener case search
API, Minnesota applies a "some damage" rule: the six-year period can begin running once
the client suffers any damage from the lawyer's error, even before the client discovers
that an error occurred. This is a case-citation-only finding, flagged rather than
confirmed in the verification file, because the case opinion's own text was not
independently fetched and read.

This kit did not find a Minnesota statute of repose specific to legal malpractice, meaning
an outer deadline that runs regardless of discovery or damage. Section 541.05's text as
fetched contains no repose language; this is recorded as a gap rather than an assertion
that no repose period exists anywhere in Minnesota law.

Do not treat any of this as legal advice about your own deadline. If you suspect
malpractice rather than a billing or communication problem, that is outside what this kit
covers, and you should get your own lawyer's advice quickly given how these accrual rules
can run against you before you even know you have a claim.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

The Scope section that precedes the numbered Minnesota Rules of Professional Conduct
states directly that violation of a rule should not itself give rise to a cause of action
against a lawyer, nor should it create a presumption that a legal duty has been breached.
It also states that violation of a rule does not necessarily warrant any other
nondisciplinary remedy, such as disqualifying a lawyer in pending litigation. At the same
time, the Scope section's own comment [21] states that because the rules do establish
standards of conduct for lawyers, a lawyer's violation of a rule may be evidence of a
breach of the applicable standard of conduct, meaning it can matter in a separate
malpractice case even though the rule itself creates no independent claim. Source:
<https://www.revisor.mn.gov/court_rules/rule/prcond-1.0_P>.

In practical terms, a rule violation on its own does not give you a lawsuit against your
lawyer in Minnesota. Your actual routes to money are the ones covered above: a contract
claim over the fee itself, fee arbitration where a local program exists and your lawyer
agrees to it, the Client Security Board if the conduct was dishonest as described in
section 5, or a separate malpractice claim if that applies, which is outside what this
kit covers.
