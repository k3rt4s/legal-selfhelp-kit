# Maryland

This pack is for a person in Maryland who has a fee, billing, communication, or file-return
dispute with their own lawyer. It does not cover malpractice claims or litigation strategy.
Retrieved August 20, 2026.

What a Maryland reader most needs to know that another state's pack will not tell you:

- Fee arbitration in Maryland is run by the Maryland State Bar Association, not by a court, and
  only kicks in once your dispute is over Maryland's small claims threshold. If the amount you
  are disputing is $5,000 or less, the MSBA program treats it as a small claims matter and there
  is no arbitration track, only informal settlement help. If it is over $5,000, arbitration is
  offered but only if both you and the lawyer agree in writing. Neither side can be forced into
  it.
- Four jurisdictions, Baltimore City, Baltimore County, Montgomery County, and Prince George's
  County, are carved out of the MSBA program entirely. A dispute involving a lawyer in one of
  those places goes to the local bar association instead, and this pack could not confirm what
  those local programs do.
- Maryland's Client Protection Fund was created by the state's highest court, not by the bar. Its
  own FAQ says it does not handle malpractice claims and draws a fine line between an ordinary fee
  dispute and outright theft, covering only the theft side. It pays out on a percentage of its own
  fund balance rather than a fixed dollar cap, and you must file with the Attorney Grievance
  Commission first.
- Maryland's general civil statute of limitations is a flat three years with no split between
  written and oral contracts, unlike some states.
- Lawyers are allowed to appear for either side in Maryland small claims cases. This is not true
  everywhere.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct, numbering and adoption

Maryland's Rules of Professional Conduct are adopted by the Supreme Court of Maryland (formerly
the Court of Appeals of Maryland) as Title 19, Chapter 300 of the Maryland Rules, called the
Maryland Attorneys' Rules of Professional Conduct, or MARPC. Cite a rule as "Rule 19-301.5"
(fees), "Rule 19-301.4" (communication), "Rule 19-301.15" (safekeeping property), or
"Rule 19-301.16" (terminating representation). The numbering does not match the ABA model
numbers directly: the model's Rule 1.5 is Maryland's Rule 19-301.5, model 1.4 is 19-301.4, and
so on, so if you are told to look up "Rule 1.5" in Maryland, look for 19-301.5 instead.

The current Chapter 300 rules were adopted effective July 1, 2016. Rule 19-301.5, the fee rule,
carries a credit line showing it was adopted June 6, 2016, effective July 1, 2016, and is current
with amendments received through June 1, 2026. This pack could not confirm, from a source it
could read, whether Maryland used a different rule-number scheme before 2016. That point is in
the gap list below rather than stated as fact.

The Attorney Grievance Commission publishes the current rules with official commentary, linking
out to the state's designated free-access host for the full rule text. See the Commission's own
rules page at <https://www.courts.state.md.us/attygrievance/rules>.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

Maryland's Rule 19-301.5(a) lists the same eight reasonableness factors as the ABA model rule, in
the same order, with no ninth factor added. This pack checked the full rule text directly and
found no deviation in the factor list itself.

The real Maryland-specific difference is in Rule 19-301.5(d)(1), the contingent-fee ban for
domestic relations matters. Maryland bans a contingent fee tied to securing a divorce or child
custody, or tied to the amount of alimony, support, or a property settlement, and it adds an
explicit cross-reference not in the ABA model: the ban also covers a fee contingent on the amount
of an award under Md. Code, Family Law Article, sections 8-201 through 8-213 (Maryland's marital
property and monetary award statute). The rule's own official commentary describes Rule 19-301.5
as "substantially similar" to the ABA's Ethics 2000 amendments, with Maryland retaining its own
language in 19-301.5(d)(1) and making wording changes in 19-301.5(e)(2) and its Comment 7.
Contingent fees for criminal defense are also banned, matching the ABA model.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration (the big one)

Maryland's fee dispute program is run by the Maryland State Bar Association's Fee Dispute
Resolution Committee, not by a court and not statewide in full. It covers every Maryland county
except Baltimore City, Baltimore County, Montgomery County, and Prince George's County, which
route fee disputes to their own local bar associations instead. The MSBA's own public page,
<https://www.msba.org/site/site/content/Resources-and-Tools-Content/For-The-Public.aspx>, states
this directly and lists a phone number for each carved-out jurisdiction's local bar association.

How it actually works, from the Committee's own regulations:

- To open a case, the amount in dispute must be at least $500, and the lawyer must have been
  admitted to practice in Maryland both when the fee agreement was made and when the disputed
  work happened.
- A Settlement Facilitator first tries to resolve the dispute informally, then offers a two-hour
  settlement conference.
- What happens next depends on the dollar amount and Maryland's own small claims threshold of
  $5,000 (Md. Courts and Judicial Proceedings Article section 4-405): if the dispute is at or
  under that amount and informal resolution fails, the file is simply closed with a letter
  explaining each side's options. If the dispute is over that amount and informal resolution
  fails, the Facilitator asks both sides to consent in writing to binding arbitration.
- Arbitration is never mandatory on either side. If either the client or the lawyer refuses to
  consent, the matter is closed instead of arbitrated. This is different from states that force
  the lawyer to arbitrate whenever the client asks.
- Arbitrators are drawn from the Committee and operate under the powers of the Maryland Uniform
  Arbitration Act.
- This pack could not find a stated filing deadline, a stated filing fee, or a requirement that
  the lawyer notify the client of a right to arbitrate before suing for fees, anywhere in the
  regulations. Those points are in the gap list.

Because arbitration here is consent-based and only reachable once you clear the small claims
threshold, a Maryland reader with a smaller dispute should expect informal Committee help or
small claims court, not arbitration.

## 4. Disciplinary/bar complaint process

The Attorney Grievance Commission of Maryland (AGC) is the state's disciplinary body. A person
can file a written complaint using the Commission's own complaint form and process, described on
its complaint page at <https://www.courts.state.md.us/attygrievance/complaint>. The AGC's own
materials frame a complaint as a way to report professional misconduct or violation of the Rules
of Professional Conduct, not as a way to get money back or have a fee refunded.

This pack could not confirm, from a source it could read, whether Maryland runs a separate
informal or consumer-assistance intake track apart from the formal complaint process, whether
there is any filing time limit, or whether Maryland has a disciplinary restitution mechanism that
can order money returned. Those points are in the gap list. Do not file a fee dispute as a
grievance complaint expecting a refund; use the MSBA Fee Dispute Committee, or small claims
court, for that instead.

## 5. Client protection fund

Maryland's fund is the Client Protection Fund of the Bar of Maryland. Unlike most states, it was
not created by the bar association. It was created by an order of the state's highest court, the
Court of Appeals of Maryland, described in the fund's own regulations as passed March 28, 1966,
though the fund's own FAQ page, <https://www.courts.state.md.us/cpf/faq>, separately states the
fund was established by the Court of Appeals on July 6, 1966. This pack could not resolve that
discrepancy between the fund's two own official publications, so both dates are given here.

- Coverage: the fund reimburses losses caused by dishonest conduct of a Maryland lawyer, in the
  attorney-client relationship, described by the fund's regulations as a "defalcation."
- Exclusions: the fund's own FAQ page says it does not handle malpractice claims, and separately
  says there is a fine line between an ordinary fee dispute and outright theft, meaning the fund
  covers the theft side and not a plain fee disagreement. This pack could not confirm, from a
  source it could read, that a loss caused by a lawyer's negligence short of malpractice is also
  excluded. That narrower point is in the gap list rather than stated as fact.
- Cap: there is no fixed dollar cap. Instead, the maximum paid to one claimant for claims against
  the same lawyer is 5 percent of the fund's net balance as of the close of the month before the
  Trustees' meeting that approves the claim. That figure moves with the fund's own balance.
- Deadline: a claim must be filed within six months after the claimant discovers the dishonest
  conduct, or later at the Trustees' discretion.
- Prerequisite: this pack found the fund's process assumes a related Attorney Grievance
  Commission matter but could not confirm, from a source it could read, that an AGC complaint or
  finding is a strict precondition to filing a fund claim. That is in the gap list.

## 6. Small claims or equivalent court

Maryland's small claims cases are heard in the District Court of Maryland. The jurisdictional
limit is $5,000, exclusive of interest, costs, and attorney's fees, under Md. Courts and Judicial
Proceedings Article section 4-405. A small claim must also be a claim for money only, not for the
return of property or for a service, and cannot involve a request for discovery such as written
interrogatories.

Lawyers may appear in Maryland small claims cases; the District Court's own self-help materials
say a party "can hire a lawyer if you choose," while noting the simplified procedure is meant to
make self-representation workable. This pack found no separate rule requiring a corporate party
to have a lawyer.

To appeal a District Court judgment, a party files an order for appeal with the District Court
clerk within 30 days of the final judgment (CJP section 12-401(e)(1)). If the amount in
controversy exceeds $5,000, the appeal is heard on the record made in the District Court, in the
Circuit Court; if it is $5,000 or less, the appeal is tried de novo in Circuit Court, meaning the
case is heard again from the start (CJP section 12-401(f)).

Filing fees are set by the District Court's own Civil Cost Schedule (form DCA-109) rather than
fixed in the statute this pack read; a reader should check that schedule directly rather than
rely on a number here.

This pack found no rule specific to suing a lawyer in small claims court, beyond the general
small claims rules above.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

- Written and oral contracts: Maryland does not split these. A civil action at law must be filed
  within three years from the date it accrues, under Md. Courts and Judicial Proceedings Article
  section 5-101, unless another Code provision sets a different period. A fee agreement dispute
  is ordinarily a contract claim and falls under this three-year period.
- Discovery rule: Maryland's Court of Appeals addressed how the discovery rule applies to section
  5-101 in Poffenberger v. Risser, 290 Md. 631 (1981). This pack confirmed the case exists and its
  citation through case-law search, but could not read the full opinion text to state its holding
  precisely, so no specific discovery-rule test is described here beyond noting that Maryland
  courts have interpreted section 5-101 to include one. Treat the exact trigger for the clock as
  unconfirmed and get that confirmed by a lawyer.
- Statute of repose: this pack found a 20-year, and for architects and contractors a 10-year,
  statute of repose in CJP section 5-108, but that statute applies only to claims arising from a
  defective or unsafe improvement to real property, such as construction defect claims. It is not
  a repose period for a legal fee or malpractice dispute. This pack found no separate statute of
  repose that applies to legal malpractice claims specifically; that absence is treated here as a
  confirmed finding, not a gap, because section 5-108 was read directly and does not reach legal
  services.
- Legal malpractice: this pack could not confirm a limitation period specific to legal malpractice
  that differs from the general three-year period in section 5-101, or confirm whether Maryland
  treats a malpractice claim as sounding in tort, contract, or either at the client's election.
  Those points are in the gap list. Do not assume the three-year period definitely applies to a
  malpractice claim without confirming it with a lawyer, since accrual and discovery-rule
  questions can move the actual deadline earlier or later than three years from the original
  event.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

Maryland's Rules of Professional Conduct include a Preamble and Scope section, cited as Rule
19-300.1, that in most states is where the "rules are not a basis for civil liability" language
lives. This pack tried three times to read that section directly from the state's designated
free-access host and was told each time that the content was not available on that site's
subscription plan. That source could not be read, and no other primary Maryland source for the
Scope section's text could be reached in the time available. This point is in the gap list.

Separately, this pack found that Maryland's Court of Appeals decided Son v. Margolius, Mallios,
Davis, Rider & Tomar, 349 Md. 441 (1998), a case the official commentary to Rule 19-301.5 itself
cross-references as related to that fee rule. This pack confirmed the case's name, citation,
court, and date through case-law search, but could not read the full opinion text, so it cannot
state here what the case actually holds about civil liability or standard-of-care use of a rules
violation. Anyone relying on that case for its holding should read it directly rather than rely
on this pack.

What this pack can say with confidence, because it is true in nearly every state and is the
premise the national baseline is built on: a rules violation on its own is not usually how a
client gets money back. The routes that can actually produce money in Maryland are the ones
described above: the MSBA Fee Dispute Committee (or your local bar association's program if you
are in Baltimore City, Baltimore County, Montgomery County, or Prince George's County), the
Client Protection Fund if the loss involved dishonest conduct, and small claims court for a
straightforward contract dispute over the bill.
