# Louisiana

This pack is for a client in Louisiana who has a dispute with their own lawyer over fees,
billing, communication, getting their file back, or an unearned advance. It does not cover
suing a lawyer for malpractice. Retrieval date for this pack is 2026-08-20.

What a reader in Louisiana most needs to know that a reader in another state does not:

- Louisiana is a civil law state. Its deadlines are not called statutes of limitation. A
  contract dispute runs on liberative prescription, and legal malpractice claims run on a
  peremptive period. Peremption cannot be paused, interrupted, or agreed around, even by
  the parties. See section 7.
- The Louisiana State Bar Association runs a Lawyer Dispute Resolution (fee arbitration)
  program, but it is voluntary for the lawyer too. If your lawyer refuses to participate,
  you cannot force arbitration. See section 3.
- There is no statewide court called "small claims court." The forum is the Small Claims
  Division of a city court, and not every parish has one, since each city court decides
  whether to create one and may limit how attorneys participate in it. See section 6.
- Legal malpractice claims in Louisiana have their own one-year and three-year deadlines
  set by a single statute, separate from the general ten-year contract period. See section 7.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct, numbering and adoption

Louisiana lawyers are governed by the Louisiana Rules of Professional Conduct, adopted by
the Louisiana Supreme Court as Article XVI of the Articles of Incorporation of the Louisiana
State Bar Association, under the court's rule-making authority in Article V, Sections 1 and 5
of the Louisiana Constitution of 1974. The rules follow the ABA Model Rules numbering, so a
reader can cite "Rule 1.5" and expect the same numbering a lawyer in most other states would
recognize.

The official compiled text is published by the Louisiana Attorney Disciplinary Board (LADB),
the body that also runs discipline intake. The edition read for this pack states it carries
amendments through May 10, 2023. Read the current rules at
<https://www.ladb.org/docs/Publication/ROPC/ROPC.pdf>. A reader should
confirm no rule has changed since that date before relying on a specific paragraph number.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

Louisiana Rule 1.5(a) uses the same standard as the ABA Model Rule: a lawyer shall not make
an agreement for, charge, or collect an unreasonable fee. Louisiana does not use a "clearly
excessive" standard or any other materially higher bar. The eight reasonableness factors in
Rule 1.5(a)(1) through (8) track the national baseline's list with no added or removed factor
and no material rewording found.

The real Louisiana deviation is in Rule 1.5(f), which the national baseline does not have an
equivalent for. Louisiana sorts every advance payment into one of four categories, and the
category controls whether the money belongs to the lawyer right away or has to sit in a trust
account as the client's money until earned:

1. A general retainer paid to secure the lawyer's availability becomes the lawyer's property
   when paid.
2. A fixed fee or minimum fee for a specific matter also becomes the lawyer's property when
   paid, and does not have to sit in a trust account, subject to paragraph (f)(5) below.
3. An advance deposit against fees that will accrue on an hourly or other agreed basis
   remains the client's money and must sit in the lawyer's trust account until earned.
4. An advance deposit for costs and expenses also remains the client's money and must sit in
   trust until spent.

Rule 1.5(f)(5) then sets the actual dispute procedure: if a fee dispute arises over a fixed
fee, minimum fee, or a fee drawn from an advance deposit, the lawyer must immediately refund
whatever both sides agree is unearned, and must hold the genuinely disputed portion in trust
until the dispute is resolved, not use it as leverage. The rule directs the lawyer to suggest
mediation or arbitration, and names the LSBA Fee Dispute Program by name as an option. This
matters for a reader: if your lawyer treated your entire retainer as earned income the day you
paid it and refuses to account for any of it, that is itself a rule problem, separate from
whether the underlying amount was reasonable.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

Louisiana runs one statewide program: the LSBA Lawyer Dispute Resolution Program, administered
by the Louisiana State Bar Association. Read the program overview at
<https://www.lsba.org/Public/FeeDisputeResolution.aspx>.

Participation is voluntary on both sides, not mandatory on the lawyer. Agreeing to submit to
the program is voluntary, but once both parties have agreed to submit a matter, neither side
may withdraw without a joint voluntary dismissal. If a lawyer named as respondent declines to
participate at all, the client is not stuck: the program's own materials tell the client that
the next step is a complaint to the Louisiana Attorney Disciplinary Board or a court filing on
the fee dispute, not a forced arbitration. This is the opposite of states where the program is
mandatory on the lawyer once the client requests it, so filing here does not by itself compel
your lawyer's participation.

The administrative fee runs on a sliding schedule: disputes under $10,000 require $50 per
party, and disputes of $10,000 or more require $100 per party. A dispute between two attorneys
carries a flat $100 fee per lawyer party regardless of amount. The LSBA may waive, defer, or
reduce the fee for extreme hardship. There is no stated dollar floor or ceiling on what the
program will arbitrate.

A respondent who is served with a claim has ten days from notice to file an answering
statement. Once both sides have agreed to arbitrate, the award is binding and enforceable in
court; the program's own materials describe the arbitration as binding once submitted. There
is no confirmed statewide requirement that a lawyer notify the client of a right to arbitrate
before suing for fees.

## 4. Bar complaint process

The Office of Disciplinary Counsel (ODC), operating under the Louisiana Attorney Disciplinary
Board, investigates complaints against Louisiana lawyers. File a complaint through the LADB at
<https://www.ladb.org/Complaint/HowTo.aspx>.

Louisiana does not split intake into a separate informal consumer-assistance track and a
formal complaint track the way some states do. After a complaint is filed, ODC screens it and
either opens an investigation, refers the matter to the LSBA Practice Assistance and
Improvement Program for diversion, or administratively declines to proceed. If ODC does
investigate and finds sufficient evidence of a rule violation, it can recommend outcomes
ranging from a private admonition (the least severe form of discipline, confidential but kept
on the lawyer's record) up through formal charges before the disciplinary board.

No confirmed time limit on filing a bar complaint was found in the LADB's published how-to
material, and no confirmed disciplinary restitution mechanism (an order that a lawyer return
money as part of discipline) was found either. Both are recorded as gaps below rather than
guessed at. Discipline against a lawyer is not the same thing as getting your money back:
Louisiana's process can admonish, suspend, or disbar a lawyer, but nothing in the material
reviewed for this pack describes it as ordering a refund.

## 5. Client protection fund

Louisiana's fund is the LSBA Client Assistance Fund, administered by the Louisiana State Bar
Association. Read the fund's page at <https://www.lsba.org/Public/ClientAssistance.aspx>.

The fund reimburses clients who lose money because of a lawyer's dishonest conduct, up to
$50,000 per claim. It does not cover losses from a lawyer's negligence or an outcome the
client is simply unhappy with, and the fund's own application materials state plainly that
the committee does not consider or act on fee disputes, negligent acts, or a lawyer's failure
to obtain a desired result. A fee dispute belongs in section 3 or 6, not here.

The fund describes itself as free to apply to, with no lawyer required, and states that
deadlines apply to starting the process, but the page reviewed for this pack does not state
what those deadlines are, and no dated rule setting a specific filing deadline was found.
That gap is recorded below. Awards are discretionary; the material reviewed does not state
whether awards are paid in full or at a percentage when fund resources are limited.

## 6. Small claims or equivalent court

Louisiana's low-dollar civil forum is the Small Claims Division of a city court, created and
governed by La. R.S. 13:5200 through 13:5212. It is not automatic: each city court decides
whether to establish a small claims division, so availability varies by parish and city.

The default jurisdictional limit is $5,000, exclusive of interest, court costs, attorney fees,
or penalties, under La. R.S. 13:5202(A). The City Court of East St. Tammany is a confirmed
exception: its small claims jurisdiction tracks whatever dollar limit applies in a justice of
the peace court in that district, rather than the flat $5,000 figure. Reporting during the
2020 legislative session referenced different, higher limits for the First and Second City
Courts of New Orleans, but that figure was not found in the current codified text of Section
5202 itself, so it is flagged rather than confirmed. Those higher figures ($25,000 and
$50,000) appear elsewhere to describe the First and Second City Courts' general civil
jurisdiction, not their small claims division specifically, which is a separate, narrower
forum under Section 5202.

An earlier draft of this pack repeated figures of $25,000 and $50,000 for those two courts,
drawn from reporting on a 2020 bill. That bill never became law, and the figures were wrong.
The current statutes were fetched and read directly: La. R.S. 13:2151(D) and La. R.S.
13:2151.4(D) both set the general civil jurisdiction of the First and Second City Courts of
New Orleans at fifty thousand dollars, as amended by Acts 2026, No. 748, effective June 3,
2026. Those are the courts' general civil limits, not a small claims limit. The small claims
division is the separate, narrower forum under Section 5202, and this pack did not find a
New Orleans specific small claims figure. A reader in New Orleans should confirm the current
small claims limit directly with that city court.
<https://legis.la.gov/Legis/Law.aspx?d=77131> and
<https://legis.la.gov/Legis/Law.aspx?d=77135>

A small claims division is a court not of record. The judges and clerks of the ordinary city
court also serve the small claims division, except that the judge may appoint an attorney to
serve as arbitrator if the parties agree to be bound by that arbitration, under La. R.S.
13:5207. On attorney representation: La. R.S. 13:5208 lets a court adopt a local rule limiting
the role of attorneys in small claims division proceedings. Louisiana law does not bar lawyers
outright, but a given city court may restrict how one may participate, so a reader should
confirm the local rule of the specific court before assuming full attorney representation.

There is no right of appeal from a small claims division judgment under La. R.S. 13:5204 and
13:5209, unless a party affirmatively moves within the statutory window to transfer the case
to the regular civil docket of that court; a case not transferred becomes final a few days
after judgment. No statute-specific rule for suing a lawyer in small claims division was
found; ordinary small claims procedure applies. Confirm the current filing fee with the
specific city court before filing.

## 7. Prescription and peremption

Louisiana does not use the term "statute of limitations." It uses two different concepts, and
the difference between them is the single most important thing in this pack.

Prescription is an ordinary deadline. In most circumstances Louisiana law allows prescription
to be interrupted (the clock resets, for example when a suit is filed) or suspended (the clock
pauses, for example while a claim could not reasonably have been discovered). A general
personal action, which includes most contract claims such as a dispute over a fee agreement,
prescribes in ten years under Louisiana Civil Code Article 3499, unless another law sets a
shorter period for that specific kind of claim.

Peremption is different, and it is not just a shorter clock. Louisiana Civil Code Article 3458
defines peremption as a period of time fixed by law for the existence of a right itself: if the
right is not exercised within the peremptive period, the right is extinguished, not just
barred as a remedy. Under Civil Code Article 3461, peremption may not be renounced,
interrupted, or suspended except where another law specifically says otherwise. In plain
terms, a discovery rule or an agreement to extend does not save a peremptive deadline the way
it might save an ordinary prescriptive one. A reader who assumes "I didn't know until later, so
I still have time" can be badly wrong if the deadline in question is peremptive.

Legal malpractice claims against a lawyer in Louisiana are governed exclusively by La. R.S.
9:5605, and that statute is both a prescriptive and a peremptive one. It sets a one-year period
running from the date of the alleged act, omission, or neglect, or from the date it was
discovered or should have been discovered, whichever applies. But it also sets an outer limit:
no matter when discovery happens, the claim must be filed within three years of the act,
omission, or neglect. The statute states expressly that both the one-year and the three-year
periods are peremptive periods within the meaning of Article 3458, and, under Article 3461,
may not be renounced, interrupted, or suspended. There is one confirmed exception: the
statute itself says the peremptive period it sets does not apply in cases of fraud, as fraud
is defined in Civil Code Article 1953. The statute text does not separately label the one-year
and three-year pieces when stating this exception, so this pack cannot confirm from the text
alone whether fraud lifts only the three-year outer cap or the one-year period as well; see
the verification file for what secondary sources say about how courts have read this
exception. This kit does not cover malpractice strategy or how to plead fraud; if either
deadline is close to passing or has passed, get a Louisiana lawyer to evaluate the fraud
exception and any other option before assuming the claim is gone.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

The official LADB-published Rules of Professional Conduct booklet reviewed for this pack
contains the numbered rules but no Preamble or Scope section stating whether a rule violation
creates a private civil cause of action. No alternate primary source stating Louisiana's
position on this question was found during this pack's research; it is recorded as a gap
below rather than answered by inference from other states.

What is confirmed is the practical route to money: a fee dispute in Louisiana runs through the
LSBA fee arbitration program in section 3, a contract or prescription-based court claim as
described in section 7, the small claims division in section 6 for amounts within its limit,
or the Client Assistance Fund in section 5 if the loss involves a lawyer's dishonest conduct.
A bar complaint under section 4 can result in discipline against the lawyer, which is a
separate outcome from getting money back.

## Before you rely on this

Confirm these for yourself before you act:

- The current text of Rule 1.5, at the LADB's published rules
  (<https://www.ladb.org/docs/Publication/ROPC/ROPC.pdf>), in case it has
  been amended since May 10, 2023.
- The current jurisdictional limit and local attorney-participation rule for the small claims
  division of the specific city court you would file in, especially in New Orleans.
- Whether the LSBA fee arbitration program or the ODC complaint process is still the right
  first step for your situation, since your lawyer's willingness to arbitrate changes the path.
