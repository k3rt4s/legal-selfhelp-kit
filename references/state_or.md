# Oregon: handling a dispute with your own lawyer

This section is for a person in Oregon who has a problem with their own lawyer over
fees, billing, communication, getting their file back, or an advance payment that was
never earned or never refunded. It does not cover legal malpractice claims or how to
run one yourself. Retrieved August 20, 2026.

What is different about Oregon:

- Oregon's fee reasonableness rule uses the words "clearly excessive," not
  "unreasonable." The test is whether a lawyer of ordinary prudence, reviewing the
  facts, would be left with a definite and firm conviction the fee is more than
  reasonable. That is a real, and higher, bar than the plain word "unreasonable." Read
  section 2 below.
- Oregon runs a statewide Fee Dispute Resolution Program that is voluntary in general,
  but the program's own rules carry a real exception: a lawyer must participate if the
  fee agreement said the lawyer would use the program, or if the lawyer took the case
  through the bar's Modest Means or Lawyer Referral programs. Read section 3 to see
  whether your situation falls inside that exception before you assume your lawyer can
  simply decline.
- Oregon keeps two lawyer-funded programs that sound alike and are not. The Client
  Security Fund pays clients back for a lawyer's dishonest conduct. The Professional
  Liability Fund is mandatory malpractice insurance that Oregon requires its lawyers to
  carry, and you do not file a claim against it as a client the way you would with a
  protection fund. Read section 5 so you do not confuse the two.
- Oregon's small claims department bars a lawyer from appearing for either side without
  the judge's consent. If you take a fee dispute to small claims, expect to face your
  own lawyer, or represent yourself against theirs, without either of you routinely
  having counsel in the room.
- Oregon's rules let a lawyer retain a client's papers, property, and money "to the
  extent permitted by other law," which points to an attorney's lien recognized outside
  the rules themselves. That is a different starting position than a state that flatly
  denies lawyers any retaining lien.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct: numbering and adoption

Oregon's rules of professional conduct for lawyers are the Oregon Rules of Professional
Conduct (ORPC), adopted by the Oregon Supreme Court. The court adopted the ORPC by
Order No. 04-044, at its public meeting of October 26, 2004, effective January 1, 2005,
replacing the prior Oregon Code of Professional Responsibility (the "DR" numbering).
Conduct before January 1, 2005 continued to be governed by the old Code. The court has
amended the ORPC repeatedly since then, and the compiled PDF's own cover page reads "as
effective February 1, 2026." That cover date is not the date of the fee-related changes
this pack relies on. Each rule in the compiled PDF carries its own separate amendment
history line, and the fee-structure changes to Rule 1.5(c)(3), 1.5(c)(4), and 1.15-1(c)
described in section 2 are each individually marked "Amended 01/01/26," meaning they
took effect January 1, 2026. The document's overall February 1, 2026 cover date instead
reflects the newest change anywhere in the compiled rules, a different and unrelated
provision on licensed-paralegal firm ownership adopted effective that date. Read
"effective February 1, 2026" as the publication date of this compiled snapshot, not as
the effective date of any specific rule described in this pack.

Cite these rules in a letter as "Oregon RPC 1.5" or "ORPC 1.5." Numbering generally
tracks the ABA model at the rule level a reader is likely to look for: 1.4, 1.5, and
1.16 sit at the same numbers as the model. One numbering difference to know: Oregon
splits the model's Rule 1.15 (Safekeeping Property) into ORPC 1.15-1 (Safekeeping
Property) and ORPC 1.15-2, which is currently reserved and unused. If you go looking
for "Rule 1.15" you will find the operative text at 1.15-1, not at a plain 1.15.

The official publication point is the Oregon State Bar's own compiled rules document,
published as a PDF at
<https://www.osbar.org/_docs/rulesregs/orpc.pdf>. The Oregon Supreme Court's order
adopting the original 2005 rules is published at
<https://www.courts.oregon.gov/publications/other/MiscellaneousNotifications/RULE80.htm>.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

The most valuable section in this pack. ORPC 1.5(a) does not use the word
"unreasonable." It says a lawyer shall not enter into an agreement for, charge, or
collect an illegal or clearly excessive fee, or a clearly excessive amount for
expenses. ORPC 1.5(b) defines "clearly excessive": after a review of the facts, a
lawyer of ordinary prudence would be left with a definite and firm conviction that the
fee is in excess of a reasonable fee. That definition sets a higher bar for a client
to clear than a bare "unreasonable" standard would, because it is not enough to show
the fee was on the high side of reasonable; you are pointing to a fee that a careful,
prudent lawyer would recognize as clearly too much.

The eight factors ORPC 1.5(b) lists for judging that question are, in substance, the
same eight factors in the national baseline: the time and labor required and the
difficulty of the questions involved; whether taking the case foreclosed other work;
the locality's customary fee; the amount involved and the result obtained; any time
limits imposed by the client or the circumstances; the length of the professional
relationship; the lawyer's experience, reputation, and ability; and whether the fee is
fixed or contingent. Oregon does not add a numbered factor beyond these eight.

Oregon does add fee-structure rules the model list does not have. ORPC 1.5(c)(3), as
amended effective January 1, 2026, prohibits a fee described as "earned on receipt,"
"nonrefundable," or similar terms outright. Before that amendment, Oregon allowed such
a fee with disclosure; it no longer does. ORPC 1.5(c)(4) instead lets a lawyer charge a
"prepaid fee," but only under a written agreement, signed by the client, that discloses:
the nature of the fee arrangement and the scope of services; the total fee and payment
terms; that the fee will not be deposited into the lawyer's trust account; that the
client may terminate the lawyer's services at any time, for any reason or no reason;
and that the client may be entitled to a refund of all or part of the fee if the
services it paid for are not completed, and how that refund would be calculated. If
your lawyer took a flat or prepaid fee, look for a written agreement matching those
five points. If you cannot find one, the fee itself may not have been properly charged
under Oregon's own rule.

ORPC 1.15-1(c) requires a lawyer to deposit fees and expenses paid in advance into a
lawyer trust account, to be withdrawn only as the fee is earned or the expense is
incurred, unless the fee instead qualifies as a compliant prepaid fee under 1.5(c)(4)
described above.

On the "clearly excessive" fee standard itself, Oregon's rule is worded differently
from the ABA model's plain "unreasonable" and this pack found no Oregon-specific case
law or bar publication interpreting how much practical difference that wording makes
in a fee dispute outside the arbitration and discipline contexts described below. Treat
the wording difference as real but do not assume it changes the outcome of your own
dispute.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

Oregon calls its statewide program the Fee Dispute Resolution Program (FDRP). It
resolves fee disputes between an Oregon attorney and a client, between an Oregon client
and an out-of-state attorney, and between Oregon attorneys dividing a fee. Since July
2024 the program has been administered for the Oregon State Bar by Arbitration Service
of Portland, Inc. (ASP), a private arbitration provider, replacing the bar's own
in-house administration.

The program's summary page describes participation as voluntary, and that is true as a
general matter. But the program's own governing rules, the OSB Fee Dispute Resolution
Rules, carry a real, written exception that a summary page does not mention. Rule 4.7
of those rules states: "A lawyer who agree with a client in their fee agreement to use
the FDRP to resolve fee disputes or a lawyer retained by a client referred by the OSB
Modest Means Program or the OSB Lawyer Referral Program must participate in arbitration
if the client files a petition for fee arbitration." (Quoted exactly, including the
rule's own grammar.) In plain terms: if your written fee agreement said you and your
lawyer would use the FDRP for fee disputes, or if you were referred to your lawyer
through the bar's Modest Means Program or Lawyer Referral Service, your lawyer must
show up if you file. Outside those two situations, the general voluntary rule applies
and a lawyer can decline. Check your fee agreement, and check how you found your
lawyer, before assuming either way. Nothing in the ORPC itself imposes a fee-arbitration
mandate; this exception lives only in the program's own rules.

If both sides participate, or the exception above applies, the process is an informal
hearing before an arbitrator or arbitration panel, followed by a written award that is
binding on both parties, subject to the limited judicial remedies in ORS 36.615,
36.700, 36.705, and 36.710. ASP also offers a nonbinding mediation option for parties
who would rather try to reach their own agreement than have one imposed. The filing fee
is $300 for a dispute under $25,000, or $400 for a dispute of $25,000 or more, assessed
on each party once the case proceeds. The rules state plainly that "any amount of fees
or costs in controversy may be mediated or arbitrated," so there is no dollar floor or
ceiling on what the program will hear.

The filing deadline is six years. The rules require a petitioner to submit a mediation
or arbitration petition "within 6 years of the completion of the legal services
involved in the dispute."

This pack found an older Oregon State Bar publication describing the program under its
prior name, the Fee Arbitration Program, with different filing fees ($50 or $75
depending on the amount in dispute) and describing the same voluntary structure and
binding-award outcome. That document is undated on its face beyond a "rev. 1/12"
notation and was last modified in 2015 according to the server, predating the July 2024
move to ASP. Treat the current FDRP Rules, not that older document, as the source for
current fees, deadlines, and the participation exception.

If FDRP does not fit your situation, for example because none of the mandatory-
participation triggers above apply and your lawyer will not agree to participate, your
money routes run through small claims or circuit court described in section 6, or
through the Client Security Fund in section 5 if the conduct involved dishonesty rather
than a plain billing disagreement.

The program's summary page, including current contact information and the application
form, is at <https://www.osbar.org/feedisputeresolution>. The program's actual governing
rules, including the participation exception and the filing deadline described above,
are published as a PDF at
<https://www.osbar.org/_docs/rulesregs/feedispute.pdf>.

## 4. Bar complaint process

Oregon's disciplinary process starts at the Oregon State Bar's Client Assistance Office
(CAO), which fields questions and complaints from the public about lawyer conduct. A
complaint the CAO cannot resolve informally, or that raises a possible rule violation,
moves to the bar's Disciplinary Counsel's Office (DCO) for investigation. From there, a
matter can go to the State Professional Responsibility Board (SPRB), which decides
whether formal disciplinary charges are warranted, and on to a formal trial panel. A
lawyer disciplined by a trial panel can seek review by the Oregon Supreme Court, which
reviews the matter de novo, meaning fresh, not just for error.

Bar complaints are not confidential; they are treated as a public record. You can file
online through the CAO's own channel, by email to cao@osbar.org, or by mail to Client
Assistance Office, PO Box 231935, Tigard, OR 97281-1935. The CAO can also be reached by
phone at (503) 620-0222 or, toll-free in Oregon, (800) 452-8260. This pack did not find
a stated deadline for filing a complaint with the CAO itself.

The bar's own public guidance is direct about what discipline will not do for you: it
explicitly tells complainants not to expect reimbursement for damages or loss through
the disciplinary process. Discipline can result in a reprimand, suspension, or
disbarment, but it is not a mechanism for getting your money back. Your money routes,
if you have one, run through the Client Security Fund in section 5, a civil claim, or
the FDRP process in section 3, not through the disciplinary case itself. This pack did
not find an Oregon disciplinary restitution mechanism comparable to the order-of-
restitution power some states give their discipline system; none was located in the
CAO's or the bar's public materials reviewed for this pack.

The Client Assistance Office's page, including how to submit a complaint, is at
<https://www.osbar.org/cao/>.

## 5. Client protection fund

Oregon has two separate lawyer-funded programs that this pack keeps deliberately
separate, because confusing them will send you to the wrong place.

The Client Security Fund (CSF), created in 1967 and funded by a mandatory annual
assessment on all active Oregon Bar licensees, is the program that can reimburse a
client for a lawyer's dishonest conduct. It is administered by the Oregon State Bar. To
qualify, the loss generally must involve dishonest conduct by the lawyer, and the claim
must also involve either a related criminal conviction, a related civil judgment
against the lawyer, or, for losses of $5,000 or less, bar discipline or the lawyer's
resignation; a hardship waiver of that additional requirement is available. A claimant
must generally attempt to recover the loss from the lawyer first. The filing deadline
is two years from the latest of the conviction, the judgment, or the discipline or
resignation date, or from when the claimant knew or should have known of the loss,
subject to an absolute outer limit of six years from the loss itself. The Fund
explicitly excludes losses from ordinary negligence or malpractice, losses from a
business or investment relationship with the lawyer including a loan to the lawyer,
consequential damages, interest, and the claimant's own attorney fees or costs of
recovery, and it does not cover fee reasonableness disputes at all. The per-claim
maximum is $100,000 for misconduct occurring on or after January 1, 2022, and $50,000
for misconduct before that date. Contact for the Fund is Sara Doherty, sdoherty@osbar.org,
(503) 431-6386. The Fund's page is at <https://www.osbar.org/csf>.

The Professional Liability Fund (PLF) is a different program entirely, and it is not a
fund you file a claim against as a client. Created in 1977 under ORS 9.080 and
operating since July 1, 1978, the PLF is mandatory professional liability, meaning
malpractice, insurance that every active Oregon Bar licensee in the private practice of
law with a principal office in Oregon must carry. It pays on the lawyer's behalf when
the lawyer becomes legally obligated to pay money damages because of a claim arising
from the lawyer's professional services, which is precisely a malpractice claim, not a
dishonesty claim you as a client would file. Oregon's own bar materials describe Oregon
as the only state that requires this coverage through a program the state bar itself
runs, rather than through commercial insurance the lawyer buys on the open market.
Coverage is $300,000 per claim and $300,000 in the aggregate per lawyer per year, plus a
$75,000 claims-expense allowance, with optional excess coverage available up to $10
million in the aggregate since 1991. Roughly half of Oregon's active licensees, those
in private practice, carry PLF coverage; lawyers who are not in private practice, for
example government or in-house lawyers, generally are not required to carry it. If you
believe your lawyer committed malpractice, the PLF is the insurance program that may
eventually respond to that claim, but this kit does not cover how to bring a malpractice
claim, and you do not contact the PLF the way you would contact a client protection fund.
The PLF's own page is at <https://osbplf.org/about/who-we-are.html>, and the Oregon
State Bar's page describing the requirement is at <https://www.osbar.org/plf/plf.html>.

In short: dishonesty by your lawyer is a Client Security Fund question. A lawyer's
malpractice is a Professional Liability Fund question for the lawyer's own insurer, not
a fund a client applies to directly. A plain fee dispute is neither.

## 6. Small claims or equivalent court

Oregon's small claims department sits within the circuit court, under ORS chapter 46.
The jurisdictional limit runs from $750 as a mandatory floor, meaning claims of $750 or
less generally must be brought there, up to $10,000 as an optional ceiling a plaintiff
can choose, under ORS 46.405. Class actions and certain suits by a person in state
custody are excluded from small claims. This pack found no county-level variation in
that dollar range; ORS 46.405 sets it as a single statewide figure.

Oregon's small claims department has an unusual attorney-appearance rule. Under ORS
46.415(4), an attorney may not appear for a party in the small claims department
without the consent of the judge. A non-natural-person party, for example a business,
may appear through a representative without an attorney under ORS 46.415(5). If you
bring a fee dispute against your own lawyer in Oregon small claims, expect the ordinary
posture to be self-represented on both sides unless the judge allows counsel in.

If the amount claimed exceeds $750, the defendant has a statutory right to demand a
jury trial under ORS 46.455(3) and 46.465(3), which moves the case out of the informal
small claims track. That right belongs to the defendant in the small claims case, not
to either side generally. A plaintiff who wants a jury trial from the start does not
"trigger" one inside a small claims filing; a plaintiff with a claim over $750 can
instead choose to sue in the regular circuit court in the first place, since small
claims jurisdiction above $750 is optional rather than mandatory under ORS 46.405(3). A
defendant who asserts a counterclaim exceeding $10,000 can force a transfer under ORS
46.461. A
defendant who demands a jury trial and loses can be ordered to pay the plaintiff's
reasonable attorney fees, capped at $1,000 unless a contract or statute provides
otherwise, under ORS 46.465(4)(a).

The filing fee under ORS 46.570 is $57 when the amount claimed is $2,500 or less, and
$102 when the amount claimed is more than $2,500, collected from the plaintiff on
filing and from the defendant on demanding a hearing. This pack found no rule specific
to suing a lawyer in Oregon small claims beyond the general attorney-appearance
restriction above.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A dispute over your fee agreement, what you owed or what should have been refunded, is
generally a contract claim. ORS 12.080(1) sets a six-year limitation period for an
action on a contract or a contract implied by law, subject to exceptions elsewhere in
chapter 12 that do not apply to an ordinary fee agreement.

Oregon's general catch-all period for a tort injury not arising on contract is two
years, under ORS 12.110(1). Oregon's official statutory annotations to that section
identify legal malpractice as a claim governed by this two-year period, and describe
the discovery rule that applies to it: the period begins to run when the client becomes
aware that the harm from an adverse resolution of the legal matter was caused by the
lawyer's conduct, not necessarily at the moment of the lawyer's error itself. The
annotations cite this rule to U.S. National Bank v. Davies, 274 Or 663, 548 P2d 966
(1976), and later cases applying it. One of those later cases, described in the same
annotations, holds that the limitation period does not begin to run until the case in
which the lawyer allegedly gave bad advice reaches its final disposition, so an action
commenced within two years of a final appellate decision can be timely even though more
than two years passed since the lawyer's original conduct. That case is Fliegel v.
Davis, 73 Or App 546, 699 P2d 674 (1985).

ORS 12.110 contains a separate subsection, (4), that sets a specific discovery-rule
period and a five-year statute of repose for medical, surgical, and dental malpractice.
By its own terms that subsection does not extend to legal malpractice, and this pack
found no separate statute of repose that applies specifically to a legal malpractice
claim in Oregon. Treat the two-year discovery-rule period above as the operative one,
with no confirmed outer repose limit beyond it.

Whether Oregon lets a client characterize a claim against their own lawyer as a contract
claim instead of a tort claim, and thereby reach the longer six-year period, was not
confirmed by a primary source within this pack's research. Do not assume you can elect
the six-year period for what is really a malpractice claim; this is exactly the
question a case-specific legal malpractice claim analysis has to answer, and that
analysis is outside what this kit covers.

None of this is legal advice about your own deadline. If you suspect malpractice rather
than a billing or communication problem, get a lawyer's advice quickly. These periods
are short, and the discovery rule does not give you an open-ended amount of time once
you have reason to know you were harmed.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

This pack searched the full text of the Oregon Rules of Professional Conduct, as
published by the Oregon State Bar, for a Preamble or Scope section stating whether a
rule violation creates a civil cause of action. Unlike the ABA Model Rules and unlike
some other states' own compiled rules, the ORPC as published begins directly at Rule
1.0 with no separate Preamble or Scope section at all. This pack found no Oregon-specific
statement, in the rules themselves, saying a rule violation does or does not create a
private civil claim. This is recorded here as an absence, not as an answer either way;
see the gap list in the verification file.

Two related and narrower rules were confirmed. ORPC 1.8(h)(3) requires informed client
consent, in a signed writing, before a lawyer can put a malpractice arbitration
agreement in place with a client. ORPC 1.8(h)(4) separately bars a lawyer from entering
any agreement with a client or former client that limits or purports to limit the
client's right to file or pursue a complaint before the Oregon State Bar. Whatever your
fee agreement says, it cannot lawfully stop you from filing a bar complaint.

Your practical money routes in Oregon, whatever the rules say about a private cause of
action, are the ones covered above: a contract claim over the fee itself, the Fee
Dispute Resolution Program if your lawyer agrees to it, the Client Security Fund if the
conduct was dishonest in the sense described in section 5, or a separate malpractice
claim if that applies, which is outside this kit.
