# Missouri

This pack is for a person in Missouri who has a fee, billing, communication, or file-return
dispute with their own lawyer. It does not cover legal malpractice as a claim to run yourself.
Retrieved 2026-08-20.

What a Missouri reader most needs to know, that a reader in another state does not:

- Missouri runs a statewide, no-cost Fee Dispute Resolution (FDR) Program through the Missouri
  Bar, offering facilitation, mediation, and binding arbitration. It is run by a Bar committee,
  not the courts, and it is voluntary for both sides, but Missouri's own ethics rule tells a
  lawyer to "conscientiously consider participating" when a client raises a dispute. Only the
  client (or someone who paid the fee) can start the process; a lawyer cannot. See Section 3.
- Missouri has its own file retention rule, Rule 4-1.22, with no counterpart in the ABA model
  rules at all. A lawyer must securely keep a client's file for six years after the
  representation ends unless the client agrees otherwise in writing, and must not destroy a
  file while certain claims or complaints are pending. If you are trying to get your file back,
  this is the rule to cite. See Section 2.
- Missouri splits the ABA model's safekeeping-property rule into three numbered subrules,
  4-1.145 (definitions), 4-1.15 (safekeeping property itself), and 4-1.155 (IOLTA accounts). A
  reader looking only for "Rule 1.15" will miss the definitions and the IOLTA-specific rule.
  See Section 1.
- The Client Security Fund, administered by the Missouri Bar rather than the courts, pays out
  only for a lawyer's dishonest taking of money or property, never for a fee dispute or for
  ordinary malpractice, and it generally requires the lawyer to already be dead, incapacitated,
  disbarred, or suspended before a claim can be paid. See Section 5.
- Missouri's small claims court limit is $5,000, and the statute does not bar lawyers from
  appearing the way some states do. It only says a party is not required to have one. See
  Section 6.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct, numbering and adoption

The Supreme Court of Missouri adopts Missouri's professional conduct rules as Rule 4 of a
larger set called the "Rules Governing the Missouri Bar and the Judiciary." Rule 4 is titled
"Rules of Professional Conduct." Cite an individual rule in the compound form the court itself
uses on its own rule pages, "Rule 4-1.5," not "RPC 1.5" and not a bare "Rule 1.5." A letter or
complaint that cites a rule as "Rule 1.5" without the "4-" prefix is missing part of Missouri's
actual citation form.

The official publication point is the Missouri Courts website's Clerk Handbook rule pages,
for example the fees rule at
<https://www.courts.mo.gov/courts/ClerkHandbooksP2RulesOnly.nsf/0/6ebe0456bcc45d6186256ca6005211df?OpenDocument=>.
Rule 4-1.5 itself states it was adopted August 7, 1985, effective January 1, 1986, and was
amended March 1, 2007 (effective July 1, 2007) and October 2, 2007 (effective January 1, 2008).

Most of the rules a client is likely to need sit at the numbers a reader familiar with the
model rules would expect: Rule 4-1.4 (communication), Rule 4-1.5 (fees), and Rule 4-1.16
(declining or terminating representation). Two numbering points do not match a simple 1-to-1
expectation. First, Missouri's safekeeping-property rule is not a single Rule 4-1.15. Effective
July 1, 2013, Missouri split it into three subrules: 4-1.145 (definitions for safekeeping
property and IOLTA accounts), 4-1.15 (the safekeeping-property requirements themselves), and
4-1.155 (IOLTA accounts). Second, Missouri has a stand-alone file retention rule, Rule 4-1.22,
adopted effective July 1, 2013 and revised effective July 1, 2016, that has no counterpart
anywhere in the ABA model rules. It is covered fully in Section 2.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

Rule 4-1.5(a) lists eight factors for the reasonableness of a fee, and they track the model
rule's eight factors closely: time and labor and difficulty of the questions; the likelihood
that taking the matter will preclude other work; the customary local fee; the amount involved
and results obtained; time limitations; the nature and length of the professional
relationship; the lawyer's experience, reputation, and ability; and whether the fee is fixed or
contingent. Missouri does not add a ninth factor to this list the way some states do, and the
rule's own comment states the factors are not exclusive and that not every factor is relevant
in every matter.

Missouri's real deviation is not in the factor list, it is in Rule 4-1.5(f), which has no
counterpart in the model rules at all: "When a fee dispute arises between a lawyer and a
client, the lawyer shall conscientiously consider participating in the appropriate fee dispute
resolution program." This does not apply where a fee is set by statute or by a court or agency
with authority to set it. This is a soft duty, it does not force a lawyer into arbitration, but
it means Missouri's ethics rules themselves point a lawyer toward the FDR Program described in
Section 3, and a reader can cite Rule 4-1.5(f) directly in a letter asking the lawyer to
participate.

Rule 4-1.5(b) tracks the model rule on communicating the basis or rate of the fee before or
within a reasonable time after the representation begins, with a duty to communicate later
changes. Rule 4-1.5(c) and (d) track the model rule on contingent fees: a signed writing
stating how the fee is calculated, a closing statement at the end of a contingent matter, and a
bar on contingent fees in domestic relations matters and in criminal defense. No
Missouri-specific contingent-fee percentage cap or sliding scale was found for ordinary civil
matters.

The other genuinely useful Missouri-specific deviation is Rule 4-1.22, File Retention, which
has no ABA model counterpart at any number. A lawyer must securely store a client's file for
six years after the representation ends, unless the lawyer and client agree otherwise in a
signed writing at any point during those six years. If the client does not request the file
within six years, the rule allows the lawyer to treat it as abandoned and destroy it, except
that the lawyer may not destroy a file while a related malpractice claim, a governmental
investigation, a Rule 5 disciplinary complaint, or other related litigation is pending, and
items of intrinsic value may never be destroyed. Files completed or terminated before July 1,
2016 are still governed by the prior 10-year retention period.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

Missouri runs a single statewide Fee Dispute Resolution (FDR) Program, administered by the
Missouri Bar, a voluntary bar association, not by the courts. The FDR Program offers
facilitation, mediation, and binding arbitration, and it serves the entire state. It is
overseen by the Fee Dispute Resolution Committee, seven members (four Missouri Bar members and
three members of the general public) appointed by the Missouri Bar's Board of Governors for
four-year terms.

Only the client can start the process. The client (or someone else who paid the fee, or
assumed responsibility for paying it) submits a petition form to the Committee; a lawyer cannot
initiate a case. The amount in dispute must be at least $500. There is no charge to either side
unless a party chooses to hire their own lawyer or a court reporter. The FDR Committee first
reviews a petition for jurisdiction; if it opens a case, and the amount in dispute is more than
$1,000, the client and the lawyer each get up to 30 days to consent to mediation, binding
arbitration, or both. Participation, including consenting to mediation or arbitration, is
voluntary on both sides; nothing in the confirmed material makes it mandatory on the lawyer,
though Rule 4-1.5(f), described in Section 2, tells a lawyer to conscientiously consider it.

If both the client and the lawyer sign a consent to binding arbitration, the arbitration
panel's decision may be enforced by a court under Missouri's general arbitration statute,
Chapter 435, RSMo. A mediation session produces an agreement only if both sides accept one; a
mediator does not decide the dispute.

No dollar ceiling on how large a dispute the program will hear was found. No specific filing
deadline, such as a number of years from the bill or the end of the representation, was found;
see the gap list. The average case takes about five months from filing to resolution, and where
arbitration is used, the panel is expected to issue a final decision within 30 days after the
hearing.

Missouri also runs a separate Lawyer-to-Lawyer Dispute Resolution Program under Supreme Court
Rule 5.10, for money and property disputes between lawyers, and a Complaint Resolution Program
for complaints the Office of Chief Disciplinary Counsel refers out of the formal disciplinary
track. Neither of those is the right forum for a client's own fee dispute; the FDR Program is.

The Missouri Bar's own page for the program, with the petition form and the current committee
contacts, is at
<https://missourilawyershelp.org/dispute-resolution-programs/fee-dispute-resolution-program/>.

## 4. Bar complaint process

The Office of Chief Disciplinary Counsel (OCDC) is an agency of the Supreme Court of Missouri,
not of the Missouri Bar, and it investigates and prosecutes allegations of lawyer misconduct
under Rule 5, Missouri's disciplinary rule. Anyone may file a complaint; you do not need a
lawyer to file. A complaint must be in writing, either as a letter, a downloadable form, or an
online fillable form, submitted at <https://mochiefcounsel.org/filing-a-complaint/> or mailed to
the OCDC's Jefferson City office. There is no cost to file.

A complaint should give the lawyer's name and address, the nature of the matter, any related
court case, and a detailed factual account of what happened. OCDC lists inadequate
communication, neglect, conflicts of interest, misuse of client funds, improper advertising,
breach of confidentiality, and improper communication with a represented party as examples of
what it reviews. A plain disagreement about the size of a bill is not on that list. OCDC's own complaint page
says so directly: it cannot recover money or attorney's fees for you, and if your complaint is
about the amount you were charged, it directs you to the Missouri Bar's Fee Dispute Resolution
Committees instead. A billing disagreement belongs with the FDR Program in Section 3, not a
formal disciplinary complaint. OCDC's own materials do not describe a separate informal
assistance track distinct from the formal complaint process the way some states do; every
complaint goes to Staff Counsel for the same initial screening.

Once filed, a complaint goes to a Staff Counsel who evaluates whether it could support a
disciplinary investigation, in the order complaints are received. OCDC aims to respond in
writing within about two to four weeks of receipt (OCDC's own pages state each figure in
different places). If a file is opened, it is investigated by OCDC's Jefferson City office or a
regional office in Kansas City, St. Louis, or Springfield, and the lawyer is typically notified
and given a chance to respond. Public discipline can range up to suspension or disbarment. No
deadline for filing an OCDC complaint was found; see the gap list. Discipline addresses a
lawyer's conduct and license, it is a separate track from getting money back, and no
disciplinary restitution mechanism that returns a client's money as part of the complaint
process itself was confirmed for this pack.

## 5. Client protection fund

The Client Security Fund is administered by the Missouri Bar, not by the courts. It was created
by resolution of the Missouri Bar's Board of Governors on September 8, 1965, and is overseen by
a six-person Client Security Fund Committee appointed by the Bar's President with the Board's
approval. Information is available through
<https://missourilawyershelp.org/dispute-resolution-programs/client-security-fund/>, and the
Committee's own governing rules are published at
<https://missourilawyershelp.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/CSF-Rules-Regs-approved-11-15-2018.pdf>.

What it covers: a pecuniary loss from a lawyer's "fraudulent or dishonest act," defined to
include embezzlement, wrongful taking of property, borrowing money from a client without intent
or reasonable ability to repay it, or failing to remit money or property owed to the client. The
loss must have arisen after January 1, 1966 and out of legal services substantially provided in
Missouri.

What it excludes: the Fund's own definition of a "fraudulent or dishonest act" expressly
excludes "malpractice, non-feasance or negligence." It also excludes losses from business or
personal investments outside the lawyer-client relationship, and consequential or incidental
damages such as lost interest or the cost of pursuing the claim.

Prerequisite and deadline: a formal claim must be filed within two years after the loss came to
the claimant's knowledge (the Committee has discretion to also accept a claim filed within two
years of the lawyer's death or the qualifying disciplinary event described next). Before a claim
can be recognized, the lawyer must have died, been adjudged or medically diagnosed as mentally
incapacitated and agreed with OCDC to permanently stop practicing, or been disbarred or
suspended, including a suspension stayed with probation. This is a stricter prerequisite than
simply having a disciplinary grievance on file; Missouri's Fund requires the disciplinary or
personal-incapacity event to have actually occurred.

Caps: no payment may exceed $50,000 per claim, and all claims arising from losses to a single
trust are capped at $50,000 in total. For a claim above $5,000, the payment is capped at $5,000
plus 80 percent of the amount above $5,000. No separate lifetime, multi-claim cap per lawyer
beyond the per-trust cap was found in the Committee's rules; see the gap list. Payment is
discretionary. The Committee's own rules call the Board's decision "a matter of grace and not a
matter of right," and no claimant has an enforceable right to a payment from the Fund. The Board
may also require the claimant to assign the Fund a right to recover from the lawyer, and any
surplus the Fund later recovers beyond what it paid out goes back to the claimant.

## 6. Small claims or equivalent court

Missouri's small claims forum is called "small claims court." It is not a separate court; it is
a docket that a circuit court's associate circuit judge maintains and presides over when hearing
these cases, under RSMo 482.300. The jurisdictional limit is $5,000, exclusive of interest and
costs, under RSMo 482.305. A plaintiff whose claim exceeds $5,000 may still file in small claims
court, but waives any recovery above $5,000 in that case or any later case between the same
parties over the same issues, under RSMo 482.315.

Under RSMo 482.310(1), parties may prosecute their claims and defenses without an attorney's
assistance, and a corporation or unincorporated association may appear through an officer or
authorized employee without that counting as the unauthorized practice of law. The statute does
not bar a lawyer from appearing for either side, unlike some states' small claims courts;
Missouri's rule is only that a party is not required to have one. No provision specific to suing
a lawyer in small claims court was found. Proceedings are informal, the formal rules of evidence
do not apply, no discovery is permitted, and the case is tried to the judge without a jury (RSMo
482.310).

A party unhappy with a small claims judgment, other than a judgment by consent, may seek a trial
de novo. Under RSMo 482.365, the application for a trial de novo must be filed with the small
claims court clerk within ten days of the judgment, and if the losing party wants to stay
enforcement of the judgment while that appeal is pending, they must also post a recognizance
with a solvent surety within that same ten-day window. No small claims judgment is a lien on
real estate. The filing fee itself was not found in the statutes reviewed for this pack; it is
generally set and collected locally, so confirm the amount with the circuit court clerk in the
county where you file.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A written promise to pay money or property, which includes most written attorney fee
agreements, has a ten-year limitation period under RSMo 516.110(1). A contract, obligation, or
liability that is not covered by that ten-year rule, which includes an oral fee agreement, has a
five-year period under RSMo 516.120(1). Under RSMo 516.100, a cause of action does not accrue
when the wrong occurs or a technical breach happens; it accrues when the resulting damage is
sustained and is capable of ascertainment, with the last item of damage starting the clock if
there is more than one.

Legal malpractice, a claim that the lawyer's own negligence caused you harm, needs its own
counsel rather than self-help. It is included here only so you know a clock exists. RSMo
516.100's own official annotations cite a 1984 Missouri Court of Appeals decision, Anderson v.
Griffin, Dysart, Taylor, Penner & Lay, P.C., as an example of the damage-capable-of-ascertainment
rule applied to a legal malpractice claim, which confirms the accrual rule reaches malpractice
claims. A commercial case-law mirror separately describes a Missouri Supreme Court decision,
Klemme v. Best, 941 S.W.2d 493 (Mo. banc 1997), as holding that a legal malpractice claim
sounding in negligence is governed by the five-year period in RSMo 516.120(4), the general
tort-style catch-all, rather than the fraud-specific five/ten-year rule in 516.120(5); that
specific holding characterization is flagged rather than confirmed, see
[verification_mo.md](verification_mo.md). No statute of repose for legal malpractice, an outer
deadline running regardless of discovery, was found in the material reviewed for this pack.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

Missouri's Rule 4 Preamble, in its Scope section, states directly that violation of a rule
"should not itself give rise to a cause of action against a lawyer" and should not create a
presumption that a legal duty was breached, and that "the Rules are designed to provide
guidance to lawyers and to provide a structure for regulating conduct through disciplinary
agencies. They are not designed to be a basis for civil liability." Missouri does not give a
client a private right of action for a bare rules violation.

Whether a rule violation is nonetheless admissible as evidence of the standard of care in a
Missouri legal malpractice case was not researched for this pack; the kit does not cover
malpractice strategy. Your actual money route runs through the other forums in this pack
instead: the Fee Dispute Resolution Program (Section 3), a contract claim in small claims court
or another court within the limitation period (Sections 6 and 7), or the Client Security Fund
where the lawyer's conduct was dishonest rather than merely a bad bill or ordinary negligence
(Section 5). Getting the lawyer disciplined (Section 4) is a separate goal from getting money
back.

## Before you rely on this

Confirm the current rule text against the Missouri Courts' own Rule 4 pages, starting at
<https://www.courts.mo.gov/page.jsp?id=198902>, confirm the FDR Program's current petition
process and any deadline directly with the Missouri Bar before assuming anything about timing,
and confirm any filing fee or local small claims procedure with the circuit court clerk in your
county before you act on it. See [verification_mo.md](verification_mo.md) for the source and
retrieval date behind every claim in this pack, including what could not be confirmed.
