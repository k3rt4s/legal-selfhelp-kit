# Illinois

This pack is for someone in Illinois trying to resolve a fee, billing, communication, or
file-return dispute with their own lawyer. It is not a guide to suing a lawyer for malpractice.
Retrieved 2026-08-20.

What an Illinois reader most needs to know, that a reader in another state does not:

- Illinois runs no statewide fee arbitration program. The Chicago Bar Association's Committee on
  Professional Fees hears fee disputes involving any lawyer practicing in Illinois, not just its
  own members, and a client can open a complaint without the lawyer's agreement; a binding
  arbitration award, however, requires both sides to agree to that track. A similar DuPage County
  program stopped accepting new cases on August 21, 2023.
- Illinois Rule 1.5(c) bans nonrefundable fees and nonrefundable retainers outright, and Rule
  1.5(d) requires a lawyer to use one of five defined fee-agreement categories, each with its own
  trust-accounting rule. If your agreement calls something a "retainer" without saying which
  kind, that alone is worth asking about.
- One regulator, the Attorney Registration and Disciplinary Commission (ARDC), handles both bar
  complaints and the state's client protection fund. There is no separate informal-versus-formal
  complaint track; every complaint goes into the same process, and ARDC's own materials say a
  disciplinary case can end in an order that the lawyer pay you back.
- Illinois's small claims limit is $10,000, and the state's Rules of Professional Conduct match
  ABA Model Rule numbering exactly for fees, communication, safekeeping property, and
  termination, so a rule cited as "1.5" here means the same rule you'd expect from the national
  baseline.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct, numbering and adoption

The Illinois Supreme Court adopts the Illinois Rules of Professional Conduct of 2010 (IRPC 2010)
as Article VIII of the Illinois Supreme Court Rules. Cite them as "Illinois Rule 1.5" or "IRPC
1.5." The official publication point is the Illinois Supreme Court's own rules page,
<https://www.illinoiscourts.gov/rules/supreme-court-rules?a=viii>, which links to each rule as a
PDF hosted at ilcourtsaudio.blob.core.windows.net, digitally signed by the Reporter of Decisions.
The rules took effect January 1, 2010, adopted July 1, 2009. Rule 1.5's black-letter
text was last amended effective July 1, 2023. Rule 1.16's black-letter text carries no amendment
date beyond its 2010 adoption; its Comment was separately amended July 6, 2023.

Illinois did not renumber its rules relative to the ABA Model Rules the way some states did. Rule
1.4 (Communication), Rule 1.5 (Fees), Rule 1.15 (Safekeeping Property), and Rule 1.16 (Declining
or Terminating Representation) all sit at the same numbers as the corresponding Model Rules. A
reader who has read the national baseline can look up the same rule number here and find the same
subject.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

Rule 1.5(a) lists eight factors for fee reasonableness, and they track the national baseline's
eight factors point for point, down to the final factor (whether the fee is fixed, contingent, or
some type of retainer). Illinois adds no ninth factor and drops none of the eight. The real
deviations sit in subsections (c) and (d), which the model rule does not have anything comparable
to.

Rule 1.5(c) prohibits nonrefundable fees and nonrefundable retainers outright, and also
prohibits any agreement that restricts a client's right to terminate the representation or that
unreasonably restricts the client's right to a refund of unearned or unreasonable fees. The
national baseline has no equivalent flat ban; this is Illinois-specific.

Rule 1.5(d) requires a lawyer to fit any advance payment into one of five defined categories,
each with its own ownership and trust-accounting rule, driven by the Illinois Supreme Court's
decision in Dowling v. Chicago Options Associates, Inc., 226 Ill. 2d 277 (2007):

1. Fixed fees: a flat sum for a described service, becomes the lawyer's property, may not go into
   a client trust account.
2. Contingent fees: must be in a signed writing stating the method of calculation and how
   expenses are deducted, with a required written closing statement at the end of the matter.
3. Engagement retainers: a fixed sum paid to secure the lawyer's availability, earned and the
   lawyer's property the moment it is paid, kept out of the trust account, and separate from
   payment for any actual work performed.
4. Security retainers: funds meant to secure future fees, remain the client's property, and must
   stay in a client trust account until the lawyer actually earns them.
5. Special purpose retainers (also called advance payment retainers): funds that become the
   lawyer's property immediately, used only when a security retainer will not accomplish the
   client's purpose, and only valid if the agreement is a signed writing using the term "special
   purpose retainer," explains the special purpose, discloses that the money is not held in
   trust, and states that any unearned or unused portion will be refunded.

If your fee agreement uses the word "retainer" without saying which of these it means, or without
the specific writing requirements attached to the category, that is worth raising directly.

No Illinois-specific statutory percentage cap on contingent fees was found in Rule 1.5 itself.
Illinois bars a contingent fee tied to securing a divorce or to the amount of alimony, support, or
a property settlement in a domestic relations matter, and bars a contingent fee for representing
a criminal defendant, matching the two bans in the national baseline with no added restriction.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

Illinois runs no statewide, court-administered fee arbitration program. What exists are local,
bar-association-run programs, and coverage is thinner than a reader might expect from a state
this size.

The Chicago Bar Association's Committee on Professional Fees, described on its own page at
<https://www.chicagobar.org/CBA/Legal_Help/Attorney_Fee_Dispute_Assistance/CBA/Legal_Help_for_the_Public/Attorney_Fee_Dispute_Assistance.aspx?hkey=82277848-de69-4e88-85dc-b5a830761565>,
describes its services as extending to "all attorneys who practice in Illinois," not only CBA
members, which makes it broader in reach than a typical county-bar program even though it is not
a state-run body. Its services are free, and no dollar limit on what the committee will hear was
stated on its page. Any client may file a
complaint on their own; the committee does not need the lawyer's agreement to open a file or hold
a hearing, though it has no subpoena power and cannot force either side to appear. A hearing can
end in a recommendation that the lawyer refund part of the fee, or a referral to the ARDC, but that
recommendation does not bind the lawyer. Binding resolution is a separate, opt-in track: if the
client and the lawyer both agree to arbitrate instead of (or after) a hearing, the resulting award
is legally binding and enforceable in court. If a petition for fees or other court action over the
fee was filed before the complaint reached the committee, the committee will dismiss the
complaint. After the committee receives the lawyer's written answer to a complaint, it mails a copy
to the client and gives the client ten days to reply in writing before closing the file; that ten-day
window runs to the client, not to the lawyer. No statute or rule found in this research makes
participation mandatory on the lawyer.

The DuPage County Bar Association ran a comparable fee dispute arbitration program but, per its own
FAQ page at <https://www.dcba.org/page/FAQFee>, stopped accepting new requests effective August 21,
2023; a dispute already in progress before that date continues through the association's office.
No confirmed program was found for Lake County, Will
County, or other Illinois county bar associations during this research; this list of local
programs should be treated as incomplete, not exhaustive, and it is worth calling your own county
bar association directly to ask.

No requirement that an Illinois lawyer notify a client of a right to arbitrate before suing for
fees was found in the rules or in any of the local program materials reviewed.

## 4. Bar complaint process

The Attorney Registration and Disciplinary Commission (ARDC), an arm of the Illinois Supreme
Court, investigates complaints against Illinois lawyers and can bring formal disciplinary charges
that the Supreme Court ultimately decides. Its own "File a Complaint" page,
<https://www.iardc.org/Home/FileComplaint>, explains that a complaint can be filed by email, mail,
fax, or the ARDC's online complaint form; there is no cost to file, and no requirement to use the
ARDC's own form rather than a plain letter.

Illinois does not run a separate informal-assistance track alongside a formal complaint the way
some states do. Every complaint goes into the same intake process: an ARDC lawyer reviews it and
decides, typically within about two weeks, whether to investigate. ARDC's own public materials
say plainly that many fee-related complaints stem from general dissatisfaction with the amount
charged rather than a rule violation, and that this kind of dispute "ordinarily" does not support
disciplinary charges; ARDC will investigate a fee itself only where the fee or the agreement
appears improper under the ethics rules, violates a court order or statute, is fraudulent, or
involves keeping an unearned advance fee. A pure disagreement over the size of a bill for work
actually done is more likely to close without action than to result in discipline.

ARDC states there is no formal time limit or statute of limitations on filing a complaint, though
it may weigh how old the alleged conduct is in deciding whether and how to act on it.

Discipline is not automatically a refund, and this pack does not treat it as one. But ARDC's own
public materials state that if an investigation shows a lawyer wrongfully took money or property
and this is proven in a formal disciplinary case, the discipline imposed may include a requirement
that the lawyer make restitution to the client. This is a case-by-case outcome within a
disciplinary proceeding, not a separate standing restitution rule with its own numbered
procedure the way some other states have.

## 5. Client protection fund

The Client Protection Program (CPP), described at
<https://www.iardc.org/Lawyer/ClientProtectionProgram>, is administered by the ARDC under the
authority of Supreme Court Rule 780 and ARDC Commission Rules 501 through 512, and reimburses
clients for losses caused by a lawyer's intentional dishonesty, or for unearned and unrefunded
fees where the lawyer died or was moved to disability inactive status. It is funded by lawyer
registration fees, not tax money.

The Program's own materials list what it does not cover: losses from negligence or malpractice
rather than intentional dishonesty; a fee dispute; a personal loan to a lawyer; an ordinary
debtor-creditor relationship; lost interest, profits, or consequential damages; conduct that
occurred before January 1, 1984; and a claim from a spouse, child, parent, grandparent, sibling,
partner, or associate of the lawyer who caused the loss. A claim also generally requires that the
lawyer already be disciplined or deceased before it will be paid, though the ARDC may begin
investigating a claim before that point.

A claim must be filed within three years of when the client knew of the loss, or within one year
after the lawyer was disciplined or died, whichever is later.

The Program may reimburse up to $150,000 for a single loss, with total payouts tied to any one
lawyer's conduct capped at $1,500,000. These figures come from an amendment adopted April 4,
2025, effective January 1, 2026, so they are the current figures as of this pack's retrieval date;
an older or cached summary may still show the prior, lower caps. There is no cost to file a claim,
and the amount, timing, and conditions of any payment are left to the Commission's discretion.

## 6. Small claims or equivalent court

Illinois small claims cases are governed by Illinois Supreme Court Rules 281 through 289, which
apply statewide rather than varying by county. Rule 281 sets the jurisdictional limit at $10,000,
exclusive of interest and costs, for a civil action based on tort or contract. This limit was last
raised, from $5,000, effective January 1, 2006, and the rule was further amended effective
January 1, 2022 to remove tax-collection cases from the small claims definition; the dollar limit
itself did not change in that 2022 amendment.

An individual may bring or defend a small claim without a lawyer. A corporation generally needs a
lawyer to appear as a plaintiff, claimant, assignee, subrogee, or counterclaimant under Illinois
Supreme Court Rule 282, but may defend a claim brought against it without a lawyer, appearing
through an officer, director, manager, department manager, or supervisor.

Illinois does not provide a trial de novo appeal from a small claims judgment. An appeal follows
the state's general civil appeal rule, Supreme Court Rule 303: a notice of appeal must be filed
within 30 days of the final judgment, and the case goes to the Illinois Appellate Court for review
on the record, not a full new trial.

Filing fees for small claims cases are set and can vary by county; this pack does not state a
figure because none was confirmed against a primary source for this research. Confirm the current
fee with the circuit clerk in the county where you would file. No rule specific to suing a lawyer
in small claims court, as opposed to any other defendant, was found.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A claim on a written fee agreement is a contract claim. Illinois Code of Civil Procedure Section
735 ILCS 5/13-206 sets a ten-year limitation period for actions on written contracts. Section 735
ILCS 5/13-205 sets a five-year period for actions on unwritten (oral or implied) contracts, and
also covers several other categories including damage to property and civil actions not covered
by a more specific statute. Both sections were read directly from the Illinois General Assembly's
own compiled statutes site (ilga.gov).

Legal malpractice against an attorney, whether pleaded in tort, contract, or otherwise, has its
own dedicated statute: 735 ILCS 5/13-214.3. Under subsection (b), the claim must be brought within
two years of when the client knew, or reasonably should have known, of the injury. Under
subsection (c), the claim is also subject to a six-year statute of repose running from the date of
the act or omission, regardless of when the client discovered it, except where subsection (d)'s
narrow exception applies: if the injury does not surface until after the client's death, the claim
may be brought within two years of the death unless an estate proceeding or probate was opened
sooner. Because Illinois has a dedicated malpractice statute, a client cannot extend the
malpractice period by pleading the same claim as an ordinary breach-of-contract claim instead.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

The Illinois Rules of Professional Conduct say directly, in the Preamble's Scope section at
paragraph [20], that violation of a rule does not itself create a cause of action against a
lawyer and does not create a presumption that a legal duty was breached. The same paragraph
states that the rules are not designed to be a basis for civil liability, but that a violation
may nonetheless be evidence of a breach of the applicable standard of conduct, which matters only
in a malpractice case, not in the kind of billing or communication dispute this kit covers.

A client's actual money routes in Illinois are: a contract claim on the fee agreement itself, the
Chicago Bar Association's Committee on Professional Fees (a hearing recommendation that does not
bind the lawyer, or a binding arbitration award if the lawyer agrees to that track), the Client
Protection Program for losses from intentional dishonesty, and case-by-case restitution ordered as
part of a formal ARDC disciplinary proceeding.

## Before you rely on this

Confirm the current text of Rule 1.5 and Rule 1.16 directly through the Illinois Supreme Court's
own rules pages at courts.illinois.gov before citing a rule number in a letter, since amendment
dates can move. Confirm the Chicago Bar Association's current fee arbitration process and any
other county program directly with that bar association before relying on it, since none of the
programs reviewed publish a written filing deadline and details can change without notice.
Confirm the Client Protection Program's current caps directly at iardc.org, since the $150,000 and
$1,500,000 figures in this pack took effect January 1, 2026 and older sources may still show the
prior amounts. See [verification_il.md](verification_il.md) for every claim's source.
