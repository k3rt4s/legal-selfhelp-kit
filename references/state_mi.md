# Michigan

This pack is for someone in Michigan trying to resolve a fee, billing, communication, or
file-return dispute with their own lawyer. It is not a guide to suing a lawyer for malpractice.
Retrieved 2026-08-20.

What a Michigan reader most needs to know, that a reader in another state does not:

- Michigan has a statewide fee arbitration mechanism, but it only runs if both you and your
  lawyer agree to it in writing. It is not something you can force on an unwilling lawyer, and
  it is administered by the same office that prosecutes discipline cases, not by the state bar.
- Michigan splits attorney regulation across three separate bodies with three separate jobs.
  The State Bar of Michigan is the membership organization; it does not investigate or
  discipline lawyers. The Attorney Grievance Commission (AGC) is the investigative and
  prosecuting arm. The Attorney Discipline Board (ADB) is the adjudicative arm that holds
  hearings and decides cases the AGC brings. Sending a complaint to the wrong one wastes time.
- Contingent fees in personal injury, wrongful death, and no-fault cases are capped at
  one-third of the net recovery, but the cap comes from a court rule, MCR 8.121, not from the
  ethics rules or a statute.
- Michigan has a rule the ABA model does not: MRPC 1.19, effective September 1, 2022, bars a
  lawyer from putting an arbitration clause covering future disputes, including fee disputes, in
  a representation agreement unless the client gave informed written consent, either after being
  told in writing what the clause gives up or by being independently represented in making the
  agreement.
- The small claims division of Michigan's district court caps claims at $7,000. A lawyer,
  including the lawyer you are suing, cannot be represented by another attorney in that
  division; an attorney may only appear there on their own behalf.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct, numbering and adoption

The Michigan Supreme Court adopts the Michigan Rules of Professional Conduct (MRPC) under its
constitutional authority to regulate the practice of law. Cite them as "MRPC 1.5," not "Model
Rule 1.5" or "Rule 1.5" alone. The rules themselves state their own citation form at MRPC
1.0(a): "these are the Michigan Rules of Professional Conduct. The form of citation for this
rule is MRPC 1.0."

The official publication point is the Michigan Supreme Court's own PDF, published at
courts.michigan.gov and currently posted at
<https://www.courts.michigan.gov/492c94/siteassets/rules-instructions-administrative-orders/rules-of-professional-conduct/michigan-rules-of-professional-conduct.pdf>.
The document's own footer states it is "Updated with MSC order(s) effective on 1/1/2026," so
the version retrieved for this pack reflects amendments current through January 1, 2026.

Michigan's numbering tracks the ABA Model Rules directly for the rules this kit cares about:
Communication is Rule 1.4, Fees is Rule 1.5, Safekeeping Property is Rule 1.15, and Declining
or Terminating Representation is Rule 1.16, all matching the model numbers. Unlike some states,
there is no general numbering offset to watch for here.

Michigan does add a rule the model set does not have at all: MRPC 1.19, restricting a mandatory
arbitration clause covering future disputes, including fee disputes, in a representation
agreement unless the client gave informed consent in writing. See Section 2.

The comment to MRPC 1.0 says the rules were "largely drawn" from the ABA Model Rules, with
changes made first by the State Bar and then by the Supreme Court, "with such substantive
changes as appeared proper to the Court." The specific administrative order number under which
the Court first adopted the MRPC was not confirmed; see the gap list.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

MRPC 1.5(a) does not use the model's plain "unreasonable fee" standard. It bars a lawyer from
entering into an agreement for, charging, or collecting an illegal or "clearly excessive" fee,
defined as one where, after review of the facts, "a lawyer of ordinary prudence would be left
with a definite and firm conviction that the fee is in excess of a reasonable fee." That is a
higher bar to prove for discipline purposes than a bare unreasonableness standard.

The eight factors listed under MRPC 1.5(a) match the national baseline's eight factors word for
word in substance: time and labor and difficulty of the questions; preclusion of other
employment; the customary local fee; amount involved and results obtained; time limitations;
the nature and length of the relationship; the lawyer's experience and reputation; and whether
the fee is fixed or contingent. Michigan does not add a ninth factor and does not omit any of
the eight.

Where Michigan does deviate is in the contingent-fee writing requirement. MRPC 1.5(c) requires
a contingent-fee agreement to be in writing and to "state the method by which the fee is to be
determined," and requires a written closing statement at the end of the matter showing the
outcome and, if there was a recovery, how it was calculated. It does not separately require, as
the model rule does, that the agreement state the percentage separately for settlement, trial,
and appeal, or spell out whether litigation expenses are deducted before or after the
contingent fee is calculated. Michigan's rule text is shorter on this point than the model's.

MRPC 1.5(d)(1) also widens the domestic-relations contingent-fee ban beyond the model's
divorce-and-support language, additionally barring a fee tied to "the lawyer's success, results
obtained, value added, or any factor to be applied that leaves the client unable to discern the
basis or rate of the fee." One carve-out: a contingent fee for collecting a postjudgment balance
already due under a support or alimony order is allowed, since collecting money already ordered
does not raise the same conflict-of-interest concern.

The Michigan-specific contingent fee cap the pack asked to verify is real and is a court rule,
not a statute or an ethics rule. MCR 8.121(B) caps a contingent fee in a claim or action
brought for damages for personal injury, wrongful death, or under the no-fault act at
one-third of the amount recovered. The one-third is computed on the net sum recovered after
deducting the disbursements properly chargeable to pursuing the claim, such as filing fees and
expert costs. Taxed court costs and any interest included in a judgment are treated as part of
the amount recovered rather than deducted from it, so "net" here means net of the case's
out-of-pocket disbursements, not net of every cost associated with the case. MRPC 1.5(c) itself
cross-references this, telling the reader to "see also MCR 8.121 for additional requirements
applicable to some contingent-fee agreements." MCR 8.121 also requires the fee agreement to be
in writing and requires the lawyer to provide the client a closing statement.

The rule the pack was asked to check for specifically is MRPC 1.19, Lawyer-Client
Representation Agreements: Arbitration Provisions, adopted effective September 1, 2022 (ADM
File No. 2021-07), which has no counterpart anywhere in the national baseline. It bars a lawyer
from entering into an agreement for legal services that requires any dispute with the client,
including a fee dispute, to go to arbitration, unless the client gave informed consent in
writing to the arbitration provision. The rule allows two ways to satisfy that: the client was
reasonably informed in writing about the scope, advantages, and disadvantages of arbitration, or
the client was independently represented by another lawyer in making the agreement. It applies
both to a clause signed at the start of the representation and to one added later. The rule's
comment lists what a written disclosure should cover when the lawyer relies on the first path,
among them: that the client is waiving the right to a jury trial, potentially giving up some
right to take discovery, waiving or limiting the right to appeal, agreeing to help pay the
arbitrator's fees, whether the clause covers a future malpractice claim against the lawyer, and
who would administer the arbitration if named. If your fee agreement has an arbitration clause
and you were never told what you were giving up, and you did not have your own lawyer look at
the agreement before you signed it, that consent question is itself worth raising, separately
from whatever the underlying dispute is about.

The comment to MRPC 1.5 also speaks directly to fee disputes: "if a procedure has been
established for resolution of fee disputes, such as an arbitration or mediation procedure
established by the bar, the lawyer should conscientiously consider submitting to it." That is
comment language, not a rule requirement, and it does not make participation mandatory. See
Section 3 for what that procedure actually is.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

Michigan runs one statewide mechanism, and it is not mandatory on the lawyer. Under MCR
9.130(A), "on written agreement between an attorney and his or her client," the Attorney
Grievance Commission's Grievance Administrator, or an attorney the administrator assigns, may
arbitrate the fee dispute and enter an award. Both sides have to agree in writing before it can
happen; a client cannot invoke it unilaterally, and a lawyer who does not want to arbitrate
cannot be forced into it by this rule. The rule text is published at
<https://www.courts.michigan.gov/siteassets/rules-instructions-administrative-orders/michigan-court-rules/court-rules-book-ch-9-responsive-html5.zip/Court_Rules_Book_Ch_9/Court_Rules_Chapter_9/Court_Rules_Chapter_9.htm>.

The arbitration itself, once both sides agree, is governed procedurally by MCR 3.602,
Michigan's general arbitration rule, except as MCR 9.130 provides otherwise. The award, along
with a motion to enter it as an order or judgment, is filed in the court that has jurisdiction
under MCR 8.122, which is Michigan's circuit court summary-proceeding rule for client money
claims against attorneys. One notable feature: if the arbitration award recommends discipline
of the attorney, MCR 9.130(A) requires that it also be treated as a request for investigation,
meaning an arbitrated fee dispute can trigger a separate disciplinary review on its own.

MCR 9.130(A) states no dollar ceiling or floor on what this arbitration will hear, no filing fee,
and no filing deadline running from the bill, the payment, or the end of the representation. It
also does not require a lawyer to notify a client of any right to arbitrate before suing for
fees. None of these were found stated anywhere in the rule text, and no separate administrative
order setting them was located; see the gap list.

One practical caveat: the AGC's own public complaint-intake materials do not mention this
arbitration option at all. A reader trying to use it should ask for it directly, by rule
number, MCR 9.130, rather than expect to find a link to it on the AGC's general complaint page.

MCR 9.130(B) adds a second, different remedy: if the administrator finds that filing a lawsuit
under MCR 8.122 would be a hardship for the client, and the client may have a meritorious
claim, the administrator may file that lawsuit on the client's behalf and prosecute it to
completion at no cost to the client. This is not arbitration; it is the AGC's own office
bringing a client money claim into circuit court. It is a narrower, discretionary option, not a
guaranteed right.

## 4. Bar complaint process

Michigan's regulatory structure is genuinely three separate bodies, and this pack names them
precisely because getting the wrong one wastes a complaint. The State Bar of Michigan is the
statewide membership organization; MCR 9.107(B) states plainly that "a local bar association
may not conduct a separate proceeding to discipline an attorney," and the State Bar itself has
no disciplinary authority. The Attorney Grievance Commission (AGC) is, in the rule's own words
at MCR 9.108, "the prosecution arm of the Supreme Court." The Attorney Discipline Board (ADB)
is, at MCR 9.110, "the adjudicative arm of the Supreme Court." Investigation and prosecution
happen at the AGC; hearings and decisions happen at the ADB.

A complaint against a lawyer, called a "request for investigation" under MCR 9.112, is filed
with the AGC's administrator. It must be in writing, describe the alleged misconduct with its
approximate time and place, and be signed. Use of the AGC's own form is not required. The AGC's
own instructions for filing are posted at
<https://www.agcmi.org/for-the-public#file-request>.

Michigan does not split the intake process into a client-chooses informal-versus-formal track
the way some states do. Under MCR 9.112(C), the administrator either tells the complainant and
attorney the request is inadequate, or serves it on the attorney for an answer; MCR 9.114 then
governs dismissal, admonishment, contractual probation, or referral toward a formal complaint
filed with the ADB. The complainant does not pick a track; the administrator and commission
decide the disposition. No separate "client assistance" intake program, run by the State Bar as
opposed to the AGC, was confirmed to exist for fee and communication problems; see the gap
list.

No deadline for filing a request for investigation was found in MCR 9.112 through 9.114. A
respondent's answer is due within 21 days of service, but that is the attorney's deadline to
respond, not the complainant's deadline to file. This reads as a genuine absence of a filing
deadline on the complainant's side, based on a full reading of the relevant rules, not a search
that stopped early.

Discipline is not a refund, and this pack says so plainly. But Michigan does have a disciplinary
restitution mechanism, which is not universal. MCR 9.106 lists disbarment, suspension,
reprimand, and probation as the forms of discipline, then adds, at MCR 9.106(5), "requiring
restitution, in an amount set by a hearing panel, the board, or the Supreme Court," as something
that can be imposed as a condition of an order of discipline, rather than a standalone sanction
on its own. A formal disciplinary proceeding, not the arbitration in Section 3, is the route
that can produce this kind of order, and it depends on the case actually reaching a panel and
the panel choosing to order it.

## 5. Client protection fund

The Client Protection Fund is a State Bar of Michigan program, established by the State Bar's
Board of Commissioners as authorized by the Michigan Supreme Court, and administered by State
Bar staff, not by the AGC. Its own overview, at
<https://www.michbar.org/client/protectionfund>, states its purpose directly: to reimburse
clients "who have been victimized by lawyers who violate the profession's ethical standards and
misappropriate funds entrusted to them."

What it covers, in its own words: losses caused by a lawyer who misappropriates estate and
trust assets, settlement funds, and unearned fees. Its exclusions are stated explicitly and
matter here: "the Fund will not reimburse losses attributable to legal malpractice, fee
disputes, or consequential damages." An ordinary disagreement over whether a bill was too high
does not qualify; this fund is for dishonest conduct, not billing arguments.

The Fund publishes caps with figures, not a vague ceiling. No more than $150,000 can be paid to
one claimant, or to a group of claimants who share a common interest in the reimbursement, and
no more than $375,000 total can be paid out for all losses caused by one attorney or group of
attorneys acting together. If claims against a single lawyer exceed that $375,000 aggregate
cap, the Fund may pay each claimant only a percentage of their actual loss rather than the full
amount.

There is a filing deadline with a discovery-rule overlay: a claimant must apply within two years
after the loss occurred, or six months after discovering it, whichever is later, or within one
year after an Attorney Discipline Board or Attorney Grievance Commission determination
regarding the attorney, or the attorney's death, whichever applies, under CPF Rule 7(B). Filing
a request for investigation with the Attorney Grievance Commission is itself part of the Fund's
claim process, not an optional extra step: the Fund's own claims procedure states that when a
request for investigation has been filed, the AGC's or the Attorney Discipline Board's decision
on it must be complete before the claim moves to the Fund's Standing Committee for review.

## 6. Small claims or equivalent court

Michigan's version is the small claims division of the district court, a division within the
district court rather than a separate court. It is governed both by Chapter 84 of Michigan's
Revised Judicature Act, starting at MCL 600.841, and by MCR 4.301 through 4.306.

The jurisdictional limit is $7,000, set under MCL 600.8401's stepped schedule of increases,
which reached $7,000 beginning January 1, 2024. This limit is set by statute statewide; no
county-level variation in the dollar limit was found, unlike states that set the limit locally.

Lawyers are restricted here in a way directly relevant to this kit. MCL 600.8408(1) states that
"an attorney at law, except on the attorney's own behalf... shall not take part in the filing,
prosecution, or defense of litigation in the small claims division." A lawyer cannot represent
someone else's case there, but can appear on their own behalf. If you sue your own lawyer in
small claims, that lawyer can defend personally but cannot bring in a different attorney to
represent them. A business party may instead be represented by an officer or employee with
direct personal knowledge of the facts, under MCL 600.8408(2).

Neither side may appeal a small claims judgment; MCR 4.305(B) says so directly. What either side
can do instead is demand removal to the district court's regular trial docket before or at the
hearing, under MCR 4.306, which puts the case back into ordinary civil procedure, including the
right to have a lawyer. This is Michigan's functional equivalent of a trial de novo mechanism,
though the rule calls it removal rather than an appeal.

The filing fee for a small claims case was not confirmed against a specific statewide figure;
Michigan district court filing fees are set by MCL 600.8371 and related fee statutes, and this
pack did not independently verify the current schedule. Confirm the current fee with the
district court where you would file.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A claim on a written fee agreement is a contract claim. MCL 600.5807(9) sets a residual six-year
limitation period for "an action to recover damages or money due for breach of contract" that
is not covered by one of the other, more specific contract periods listed earlier in that same
section.

Legal malpractice in Michigan has its own dedicated statute. MCL 600.5805(8) sets a two-year
general limitation period for malpractice claims. MCL 600.5838 governs when that period starts
for a claim against a Michigan-licensed professional, including an attorney: the claim accrues
at the time the professional discontinues serving the client in the matter giving rise to the
claim, regardless of when the client actually discovers the problem. Separately, MCL
600.5838(2) lets the client instead file within six months of discovering, or reasonably should
have discovered, the existence of the claim, whichever is later than the two-year period,
though the client bears the burden of proving that later discovery. In practice this often
gives more time than a flat two years from when the representation ended.

Michigan does have a statute of repose for legal malpractice. MCL 600.5838b caps a legal
malpractice action against an attorney or a law firm at whichever comes first: the expiration
of the otherwise-applicable limitation period described above, or six years after the act or
omission that is the basis for the claim. That six-year period runs from the act or omission
itself, not from when the representation ended or when the client discovered the problem, so it
can cut off a claim earlier than the discovery rule in MCL 600.5838(2) would otherwise allow.
MCL 600.5838a, by contrast, is written specifically for medical malpractice against licensed
health care professionals and facilities; its own text does not extend to legal malpractice or
attorneys, which confirms it is the wrong section to rely on for a lawyer's repose period.

Legal malpractice in Michigan sounds in tort, not contract, even where the client also had a
written fee agreement. Michigan's Court of Appeals has held that a claim against an attorney is
governed by the malpractice limitation period unless the client can point to a breached
"special agreement," meaning a contract to perform a specific, defined act, separate from the
lawyer's general duty to provide competent representation. Brownell v Garber, 199 Mich App 519,
524-525; 503 NW2d 81 (1993), restated in Broz v Plante & Moran, PLLC, 326 Mich App 528; 928
NW2d 292 (2018). Absent that kind of special agreement, a client cannot get a longer clock by
relabeling a malpractice claim as a breach of contract claim.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

MRPC 1.0(b) states this directly: "the rules do not... give rise to a cause of action for
enforcement of a rule or for damages caused by failure to comply with an obligation or
prohibition imposed by a rule." A rule violation is a basis for the disciplinary process, not
a lawsuit by itself.

On whether a violation can still be used as evidence in some other kind of case, Michigan's
rule is more neutral than the model comment on this point. It says only that "in a civil or
criminal action, the admissibility of the Rules of Professional Conduct is governed by the
Michigan Rules of Evidence and other provisions of law." Unlike the model rule's own comment,
which affirmatively states a violation may be used as evidence of a breach of the standard of
care in a malpractice case, MRPC 1.0(b) defers the question to ordinary evidence law instead of
taking a position on it. This kit does not cover malpractice strategy, so that question was not
researched further.

A client's actual money routes in Michigan are: a contract claim on the fee agreement, the
MCR 9.130 fee arbitration if the lawyer agrees to participate, the Client Protection Fund for
dishonest conduct, and disciplinary restitution under MCR 9.106(5) where a formal proceeding
results in an order for it.
