# Wyoming: handling a dispute with your own lawyer

This section is for a person in Wyoming who has a problem with their own lawyer over
fees, billing, communication, getting their file back, or an advance payment that was
never earned or never refunded. It does not cover legal malpractice claims or how to run
one yourself. Retrieved August 20, 2026.

What is different about Wyoming:

- Wyoming's contingent fee agreements are governed by a separate, stand-alone rule set,
  the Rules Governing Contingent Fees for Members of the Wyoming State Bar, not by the
  numbered Rule 1.5 alone. That separate rule set carries its own presumptive-reasonable
  percentage schedule and its own fee-review process, a Committee on the Resolution of
  Fee Disputes, distinct from the general fee arbitration program in section 3. Read
  section 2.
- Wyoming's statewide fee arbitration program is mandatory for the lawyer once a client
  timely starts it, but voluntary for the client. A lawyer cannot refuse to arbitrate a
  client-initiated request. There is no fee to file. Read section 3.
- A Wyoming lawyer may withhold specific documents a client has not paid for under the
  fee agreement, and separately, a Wyoming statute gives a lawyer a lien on client papers
  and money once the lawyer gives proper notice. These are two different legal
  mechanisms with different scope. Read section 2.
- Wyoming's small claims appeal to district court is a review on the record, not a new
  trial. A current court rule sets the fee paid to the circuit court clerk when you file
  the notice of appeal at $100, separate from the $10 circuit court filing fee, and a
  second rule lists a $100 docketing fee at the district court; it is unclear whether both
  apply to the same appeal, so confirm the total with the clerk. Read section 6.
- Wyoming's legal malpractice limitation period sits inside a general "professional or
  health care services" statute that also covers doctors. A 2020 Wyoming Supreme Court
  decision applied it to bar a claim against an attorney and a law firm. Read section 7.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct: numbering and adoption

Wyoming's lawyer conduct rules are adopted by the Wyoming Supreme Court and published
under the title "Rules of Professional Conduct for Attorneys at Law." The rule numbering
tracks the ABA model rules directly: Rule 1.5 is the fee rule, Rule 1.4 is communication,
Rule 1.16 covers declining or terminating representation, all as you would expect from
another state that follows the model numbering. Cite them in a letter as "Rule 1.5,
Wyoming Rules of Professional Conduct" or the shorter "Wyo. R. Prof. Cond. 1.5."

One numbering difference to know: Wyoming inserted a rule with a letter suffix, Rule
1.15A, "Client Files," between the trust-account rule (1.15) and the termination rule
(1.16). This rule does not exist in the ABA model numbering at all. It is the rule that
answers what counts as "the file" and when a lawyer must hand it over, covered in section
2 below.

The current published version of the Rules of Professional Conduct carries an effective
date of September 21, 2026. The rules are published as a PDF by the Wyoming Judicial
Branch at
<https://www.wyocourts.gov/app/uploads/2026/06/WY-Rules-Prof-Conduct-Eff.-9-21-26.pdf>,
reached from the court's own rules index at <https://www.wyocourts.gov/court-rules/>.
Wyoming's rule PDFs move to a new URL with each effective-date amendment, so confirm you
are reading the current file from the index page, not a bookmarked link.

Contingent fee agreements are separately governed by the Rules Governing Contingent Fees
for Members of the Wyoming State Bar, a rule set with its own numbering (Rule 1 through
Rule 6), published at
<https://www.wyocourts.gov/app/uploads/2025/01/RULES_GOVERNING_CONTINGENT_FEES_FR_MEMBERS_OF_THE_WYOMING_STATE_BAR-1.pdf>.
Fee arbitration is governed by yet another separate rule set, the Wyoming Rules for Fee
Arbitration, covered in section 3. Lawyer discipline is governed by the Wyoming Rules of
Disciplinary Procedure, a rule set separate from the Rules of Professional Conduct,
covered in section 4. A reader who only knows "Rule 1.5" should know that several of the
practical answers in this pack live in these other, separately numbered rule sets, not in
the numbered conduct rules themselves.

## 2. How Wyoming's fee rule differs from the model, and file and lien rules

Rule 1.5(a) lists the same eight reasonableness factors as the ABA model rule, in the
same order: time and labor required, the novelty and difficulty of the questions, and the
skill required; whether taking the matter would preclude other employment; the customary
local fee; the amount involved and the results obtained; time limits imposed by the
client or circumstances; the nature and length of the professional relationship; the
lawyer's experience, reputation, and ability; and whether the fee is fixed or contingent.
Wyoming adds nothing to this list and removes nothing from it.

The real deviation is in Rule 1.5(b). For an ordinary hourly or flat fee, Rule 1.5(b) only
"preferably" requires a written fee basis, matching the model rule's permissive language.
But the same paragraph, Rule 1.5(b), also requires every contingent fee agreement to be in
writing and to comply with the separate Rules Governing Contingent Fees for Members of
the Wyoming State Bar. (Rule 1.5(c) itself tracks the ABA model's contingent-fee content,
the required percentage terms and the written closing statement, without repeating the
cross-reference to the separate rule set.) That separate rule set is the real deviation
from the model: its
Rule 5 sets a presumptive-reasonable percentage schedule, 33 1/3 percent of a recovery
settled within 60 days of filing suit, 40 percent if settled later or after a verdict,
and 30 percent of any amount recovered above one million dollars. A fee within this
schedule is presumed reasonable; the parties remain free to contract for a different
percentage, but a fee above the schedule invites the reasonableness review described
below. This kit did not find a Wyoming rule specifically labeled "nonrefundable fee" or
"earned upon receipt" fee; an advance fee is still governed by the ordinary trust-account
and refund-on-termination rules described next.

Rule 1.16(d) requires a lawyer, on termination, to take steps reasonably practicable to
protect the client's interests, including giving reasonable notice, allowing time to
find new counsel, surrendering papers and property the client is entitled to, and
refunding any advance payment of a fee or expense that has not been earned or incurred.

What "surrendering papers" means is spelled out in Rule 1.15A, a rule unique to Wyoming's
numbering (see section 1). Rule 1.15A(a) defines "the client's file" broadly: everything
the client supplied, all correspondence, all filed pleadings, investigatory and discovery
materials, and any intrinsically valuable document such as an original will, deed, or
security. Rule 1.15A(b) requires the lawyer to make that file available to the client
within a reasonable time of a request, but carves out two exceptions: the lawyer may keep
copies at the lawyer's own expense, and, most relevant to a fee dispute, Rule 1.15A(b)(2)
says the lawyer is not required to turn over documents the client is obligated to pay for
under the fee agreement but has not paid for. This is a narrow exception tied to specific
unpaid items, not a blanket right to hold the whole file hostage over any unpaid balance,
and it does not reach intrinsically valuable trust property, which Rule 1.15A(d) requires
be delivered regardless.

Separately, Wyoming has a statutory attorney's lien. W.S. 29-9-102 gives a lawyer a lien
for compensation due, attaching to any papers or money of the client that have come into
the lawyer's possession, or to money the client is owed that is in an adverse party's
possession. The lien takes effect once the lawyer gives notice by certified mail, return
receipt requested. This lien is broader than the narrow document-withholding exception in
Rule 1.15A(b)(2): once proper notice is given, it can reach the whole file and any client
money the lawyer holds, not just specific unpaid-for items. A lawyer asserting the lien
without giving the required notice is not using it correctly.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

Wyoming runs a single statewide program, established directly by the Wyoming Supreme
Court: the Wyoming Rules for Fee Arbitration, administered by the Fee Arbitration
Committee and a Wyoming State Bar staff Administrator. The current rules are published at
<https://www.wyocourts.gov/app/uploads/2025/01/WYOMING-RULES-FOR-FEE-ARBITRATION-1.pdf>.
This is a different body from the Committee on the Resolution of Fee Disputes in section
2, which reviews only contingent fee agreements under a separate rule set; this program
covers "any and all fees and/or costs paid, charged, or claimed for professional services
by lawyers," contingent or not.

Rule 1(c) states this directly: "Fee arbitration pursuant to these rules is voluntary for
clients and mandatory for lawyers if timely commenced by a client in accordance with
these rules." A client can start the process on their own initiative and the lawyer
cannot refuse to participate. The reverse is not true; a lawyer cannot force a client
into arbitration.

The decision automatically becomes binding on both sides unless a party seeks a trial de
novo, under the Wyoming Rules of Civil Procedure, within 30 days of service of the
decision, or unless all parties have separately agreed in writing to be bound, in which
case no one can withdraw from that agreement without everyone's consent.

There is a dollar floor: Rule 1 excludes disputes where the amount in dispute is $500.00
or less. This kit did not find a dollar ceiling on what the program will hear; the dollar
amount instead determines the size of the panel, three arbitrators, one of them a
non-lawyer, for disputes of $10,000 or more, and a single lawyer-arbitrator for disputes
under that amount.

There is a filing deadline: a request for arbitration is barred if filed more than one
year after the lawyer-client relationship ended, or more than one year after the client
received the final billing, whichever is later. This kit did not find a fee to file a
petition anywhere in the current Wyoming Rules for Fee Arbitration, after searching the
full text of the rules for any filing-fee provision.

There is a notice requirement that works in the client's favor. Before or at the time a
lawyer sues a client to collect fees, Rule 1(g) requires the lawyer to serve the client,
by certified mail return receipt requested, with written notice of the client's right to
arbitrate, including a warning that the client must file a petition within 30 days of
that notice or waive the right. Failure to give this notice is grounds for dismissal of
the lawyer's own fee collection suit. Once a client has filed a petition, the lawyer must
stop any non-judicial collection activity on the disputed fees while arbitration is
pending.

## 4. Filing a bar complaint

Wyoming's discipline system runs under the Wyoming Rules of Disciplinary Procedure, a
rule set separate from the Rules of Professional Conduct, published at
<https://www.wyocourts.gov/app/uploads/2025/01/WYOMING-RULES-OF-DISCIPLINARY-PROCEDURE-1.pdf>.
Complaints are received and investigated by the Office of Bar Counsel, and formal cases
are heard by the Board of Professional Responsibility (BPR), all operating under the
Wyoming Supreme Court's supervision.

A complaint against a lawyer must be filed within four years of when the complainant
discovered, or reasonably should have discovered, the misconduct. There is no statute of
limitations for misconduct alleging fraud, conversion, conviction of a serious crime, or
an offense whose discovery the lawyer concealed.

This kit did not find a separate informal, non-disciplinary consumer-assistance track
inside the Wyoming Rules of Disciplinary Procedure; a complaint to Bar Counsel is the
mechanism this kit found. Discipline is not a refund: the process protects the public and
the profession, not the complainant's money, and a straightforward billing disagreement,
without more, is unlikely to result in a formal charge.

Wyoming does have a limited disciplinary restitution mechanism. Restitution can be
ordered as a condition of probation when a lawyer is placed on probation as a
disciplinary sanction, and a lawyer's failure to pay court-ordered restitution can itself
support revoking that probation. This is discretionary, tied to an actual disciplinary
sanction, and not something a complainant can request directly or count on receiving.

## 5. Client protection fund

Wyoming's fund is the Client Protection Fund of the Wyoming State Bar, administered by a
nine-member Client Protection Fund Committee, one member from each of Wyoming's nine
judicial districts, appointed by the Wyoming Supreme Court. The current governing rules
are published at
<https://www.wyocourts.gov/app/uploads/2025/01/RULES-FOR-THE-CLIENT-PROTECTION-FUND-1.pdf>.

The Fund reimburses losses caused by a lawyer's "dishonest conduct," defined to include
theft, embezzlement, and other wrongful taking or conversion of money, property, or other
things of value, and the rule's own definition specifically includes a lawyer's failure
to refund fees received in advance as required by Rule 1.16 of the Rules of Professional
Conduct. This means an unearned-advance-fee loss can be covered, but only where the
failure to refund rises to dishonest conduct, not a routine billing dispute over how much
was actually earned.

A claim must be filed no later than four years after the claimant knew, or should have
known, of the lawyer's dishonest conduct. Excluded losses include those of the lawyer's
own spouse, children, parents, grandparents, siblings, partners, associates, and
employees; losses already covered by a bond, surety agreement, or insurance contract;
losses recoverable under a banker's blanket bond; losses through a business entity the
lawyer or those insiders control; losses of a governmental entity or agency; and losses
from business or personal investments outside the lawyer-client relationship. The
Committee may recognize an otherwise-excluded claim in a case of extreme hardship or
special circumstances.

This kit did not find a fixed rule-text cap on the per-claim or aggregate reimbursement
amount. The rule instead gives the Committee discretion to set the maximum amount payable
from time to time, and separately bars paying any one claimant more than $15,000.00 in a
calendar year without prior approval from the Wyoming State Bar's Board of Officers and
Commissioners. That $15,000 figure is an approval threshold in the rule text, not a
stated hard cap; the rule states plainly that reimbursement is discretionary, that no
person has a legal right to it, and that there is no appeal from a Committee decision.

## 6. Small claims: circuit court

Wyoming's small claims procedure runs in circuit court, under W.S. 1-21-201 through
1-21-205. The jurisdictional limit is $6,000, exclusive of costs. This is a single
statewide statutory figure; this kit did not find any county-level variation in the
dollar limit.

A party does not need a lawyer to appear. W.S. 1-21-202(b) allows the state, a
governmental entity, a natural person, or an organization to litigate in person or
through an authorized employee, with or without an attorney. If one side appears with an
attorney, the statute entitles the other side to a continuance to obtain an attorney of
its own. The filing fee for a small claims action is $10.00, under W.S. 5-9-135, which
otherwise sets a $70.00 combined filing, automation, and indigent-legal-services fee for
ordinary circuit court civil cases.

An appeal from a circuit court civil judgment goes to district court, and W.S. 5-9-141
states plainly that "a judge of a district court shall review the case on the record on
appeal." This is a review on the existing record, not a trial de novo, a new full trial.
The Wyoming Rules of Appellate Procedure (WRAP) govern the mechanics of that appeal, and
WRAP Rule 1.02 confirms its scope reaches appeals from a circuit court, a type of "trial
court," to a district court, a type of "appellate court" for this purpose.

Under WRAP Rule 2.01(a), a notice of appeal must be filed with the clerk of the circuit
court within 30 days from entry of the appealable order. On a showing of excusable
neglect, the circuit court can extend that deadline, but the application for the
extension must itself be filed before 45 days have passed from entry of the order.

The true total cost of that appeal is more than the small claims filing fee you already
paid. WRAP Rule 2.09(a) requires the appellant, when filing the notice of appeal, to
deliver to the circuit court clerk both a docket fee for the district court and a separate
fee for the circuit court clerk to prepare the record, both collected under W.S. 5-3-205,
W.S. 5-9-135, and court rule. Two specific, currently effective court rules each state a
dollar figure: Rule 8 of the Rules for Fees and Costs for Circuit Courts, effective July
1, 2024, sets the "appellate and docketing fees for the circuit courts" at $100.00, and
Rule 1(C) of the Rules for Fees and Costs for District Courts lists $100.00 "for docketing
an appeal from a circuit court or municipal court." This kit could not tell from the rule
text whether these describe the same $100.00 fee from each court's side, or two separate
charges totaling $200.00. Confirm the exact total with the circuit court clerk before
filing, and budget separately for any court reporter fee for a transcript.

This kit did not find any Wyoming rule specific to suing your own lawyer in small claims
court; an ordinary fee dispute proceeds the same as any other small claims case.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A dispute over a fee agreement is ordinarily a contract claim. W.S. 1-3-105(a)(i) gives a
written contract, agreement, or promise a 10-year limitation period. W.S. 1-3-105(a)(ii)
gives a contract not in writing, express or implied, an 8-year period.

Wyoming's legal malpractice period sits inside a general professional-services statute,
W.S. 1-3-107, titled "Act, error or omission in rendering professional or health care
services." By its own terms this statute covers "licensed or certified professional or
health care services," language broad enough to reach a licensed attorney and not
written to name any single profession. The period is two years from the date of the
alleged act, error, or omission. A discovery rule extends that period: if the claimant
can show the act, error, or omission was not reasonably discoverable within the initial
two years, or that the claimant failed to discover it despite due diligence, the claimant
may bring the action up to two years after actual discovery. If the act is discovered
during the second year of the original two-year period, the filing period is extended by
an additional six months. This kit did not find a separate statute of repose specific to
legal malpractice, nor a codified continuous-representation tolling rule, inside W.S.
1-3-107 or elsewhere in Wyoming Statutes Title 1, Chapter 3.

The Wyoming Supreme Court has applied W.S. 1-3-107(a)(i) to bar a claim against a lawyer.
In Foltz v. Oblasser, 2020 WY 51, 461 P.3d 417 (Wyo. 2020), a client sued his former
criminal defense attorney and the attorney's law firm to recover a retainer; the court
held the two-year professional malpractice limitation period in W.S. 1-3-107(a)(i) barred
the claim because it arose from the professional relationship with the attorney. This
kit read that holding from a commercial case-law mirror republishing the court's opinion,
not from the Wyoming Judicial Branch's own site, so it is flagged rather than confirmed in
references/verification_wy.md; see that file for the retrieval detail.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

Wyoming's Rules of Professional Conduct carry a Scope section, paragraph [20], that
closely tracks the ABA model language: "Violation of a Rule should not itself give rise
to a cause of action against a lawyer nor should it create any presumption in such a case
that a legal duty has been breached." The same paragraph adds that a rule violation does
not necessarily warrant a nondisciplinary remedy such as disqualification, that the Rules
are designed to provide guidance to lawyers and a structure for disciplinary regulation
rather than a basis for civil liability, and that the Rules are not meant to be invoked by
opposing parties as procedural weapons.

Wyoming's version keeps the model rule's final substantive point but rewords it slightly.
Where the ABA model says a lawyer's violation of a Rule "may be evidence of breach of the
applicable standard of conduct," Wyoming's paragraph [20] says only that "the Rules may be
evidence of the applicable standard of conduct," dropping the explicit words "a lawyer's
violation of a Rule" and "breach of." The practical meaning is the same: a rule violation
is not itself a claim, but it can still be used as evidence of what a reasonable lawyer's
conduct should look like in a case that is otherwise a recognized claim, such as a
malpractice action.

In practical terms, filing a rule-violation complaint against your lawyer does not by
itself put money in your hands. Your actual money routes are the ones covered above: a
contract claim over the fee itself under section 7, the fee arbitration program in
section 3, the client protection fund in section 5 if the conduct was dishonest in the
sense described there, or a separate malpractice claim if that applies to you, which is
outside what this kit covers.
