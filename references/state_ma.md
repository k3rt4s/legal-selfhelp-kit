# Massachusetts

This pack is for someone in Massachusetts who has a dispute with their own lawyer, usually
about a fee, and wants to know where to take it. Retrieval date for this pack is 2026-08-20.

What a reader here most needs to know that a reader in another state does not:

- Massachusetts uses a "clearly excessive" fee standard, not the model's "unreasonable"
  standard. See Section 2.
- The Massachusetts Bar Association runs a statewide fee arbitration program, but it is
  voluntary on both sides. A lawyer cannot be forced into it. See Section 3.
- The Clients' Security Board says plainly that it has no filing deadline and no published cap
  on what it will pay a claimant. That is unusual; most states cap both. See Section 5.
- Massachusetts attorney malpractice claims have a 3-year limitation period and no statute of
  repose at all, unlike the state's own medical malpractice claims, which share the same
  statute but carry a 7-year repose. See Section 7.
- A billing dispute with a lawyer can sometimes be brought as a Chapter 93A unfair-practices
  claim, which carries multiple damages, but only in narrow circumstances. See Section 8.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct, numbering and adoption

The Massachusetts Rules of Professional Conduct are adopted by the Supreme Judicial Court
(SJC) as S.J.C. Rule 3:07. Cite them as "Mass. R. Prof. C." followed by the rule number, for
example Mass. R. Prof. C. 1.5. The numbering tracks the ABA Model Rules: 1.4 is Communication,
1.5 is Fees, 1.15 is Safekeeping Property, 1.16 is Declining or Terminating Representation. A
reader looking for those numbers in Massachusetts will find them in the same place as the
model.

The official publication point is the SJC's rules page at mass.gov, under
"Massachusetts Rules of Professional Conduct," which publishes each rule as its own page, for
example Rule 1.4 (Communication) at
<https://www.mass.gov/supreme-judicial-court-rules/rules-of-professional-conduct-rule-14-communication>,
Rule 1.5 (Fees) at
<https://www.mass.gov/supreme-judicial-court-rules/rules-of-professional-conduct-rule-15-fees>,
Rule 1.15 (Safekeeping Property) at
<https://www.mass.gov/supreme-judicial-court-rules/rules-of-professional-conduct-rule-115-safekeeping-property>,
and Rule 1.16 (Declining or Terminating Representation) at
<https://www.mass.gov/supreme-judicial-court-rules/rules-of-professional-conduct-rule-116-declining-or-terminating-representation>.
mass.gov itself blocks automated retrieval (see verification_ma.md), so these four pages were
each confirmed this pack's most recent retrieval date through the newest available Internet
Archive snapshot of the same official mass.gov page, and each snapshot's page title carries the
expected rule number. This pack no longer cites the Massachusetts School of Law's own uploaded
PDF copy of the rules; that copy is dated 2020, is not an official publisher, and predates the
2022 Preamble and Scope revision described below, so it is not used here even for numbering.
The Preamble and Scope section was rewritten and replaced in its entirety by SJC order effective
October 1, 2022, at
<https://web.archive.org/web/20250901032843/https://www.mass.gov/doc/order-amending-sjc-rule-307-effective-october-1-2022/download>
(mass.gov itself blocks automated retrieval, so this pack cites an Internet Archive snapshot of
the same official order; see verification_ma.md), along with several other comment-level changes
across the rules that year. Rule 1.5 itself was last amended with an effective date of September
1, 2018. A reader relying on a printed or downloaded copy of the rules should check that it
reflects at least the 2022 Preamble and Scope changes, since older mirrors of the rules in
circulation predate that revision.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

This is the most important difference in the Massachusetts rule. Mass. R. Prof. C. 1.5(a)
does not use the model's "unreasonable" fee standard. It says a lawyer shall not enter into an
agreement for, charge, or collect an illegal fee or a **clearly excessive** fee. "Clearly
excessive" is a higher bar for a client to clear than "unreasonable": the fee has to be
excessive by more than a fair margin, not merely on the high side of reasonable. The current
official text of Rule 1.5(a) does not itself define "clearly excessive" beyond the factor list
below; an earlier draft of this pack included a definitional sentence ("a lawyer of ordinary
prudence would be left with a definite and firm conviction...") that could not be found
anywhere in the official rule text or comments and has been removed. See the gap list in
verification_ma.md.

The list of factors bearing on that judgment tracks the model's eight factors in substance.
Massachusetts does not add extra numbered factors the way some states do. The deviation here
is in the standard itself, not in the factor count.

Mass. R. Prof. C. 1.5(b) goes further than the model on the writing requirement. Where the
model rule prefers a written fee communication, Massachusetts requires it: when a lawyer has
not regularly represented the client, the scope of the representation and the basis or rate of
the fee and expenses must be communicated to the client, **in writing**, before or within a
reasonable time after starting the representation. There are three exceptions to the writing
requirement: a fee for services rendered in a single consultation, a fee the lawyer reasonably
expects to be less than $500, or a fee for representing a criminal defendant that is fixed by
appointment of a court.

Contingent fees carry more Massachusetts-specific detail than the model rule has at all.
Mass. R. Prof. C. 1.5(c) requires the contingent fee agreement to be in writing, signed by both
the lawyer and the client, and prepared in duplicate, with the lawyer giving the client a
signed copy. The rule as currently published on mass.gov (effective September 1, 2018)
requires the lawyer to retain their duplicate copy for six years after the matter concludes.
An older, undated mirror of the rules found during this pack's research states seven years;
that figure is not used here because it does not carry a confirmed current effective date.

Massachusetts also lists, by rule, at least eight items the contingent fee writing must
contain, more than the three items the model rule specifies: the name and address of each
client and of the lawyer or firm, the nature of the claim, how the contingency is defined,
the percentage method (including any provision that a different percentage applies at
different stages, with the client owing the lesser or greater of two calculations), how
litigation expenses are handled, whether and how the lawyer may claim a fee if discharged
before the contingency occurs, and how a fee is divided if the client later hires successor
counsel. If a fee is claimed on termination before the contingency occurs, the lawyer must
have told the client in the engagement agreement that this claim was reserved.

On termination of a contingent-fee representation, or on the client's written request before
the contingency occurs, the rule requires the lawyer to give the client a written itemized
statement, within a reasonable time and in any event within 20 days of the request, unless the
lawyer waives in writing any claim to a fee or expenses.

Mass. R. Prof. C. 1.5(d) prohibits contingent fees in the same two situations the model rule
does: a fee contingent on securing a divorce or on the amount of alimony, support, or a
property settlement, and any contingent fee in a criminal defense. No deviation was found
there.

One further deviation worth naming: the comment to Mass. R. Prof. C. 1.5 states a lawyer should
conscientiously consider mediation or fee arbitration where a fee dispute arises, but is bound
to use such a procedure only where it is required by law or by the lawyer's own agreement with
the client. There is no rule-based duty in Massachusetts requiring a lawyer to submit to fee
arbitration. See Section 3.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

The Massachusetts Bar Association (MBA) runs a statewide Legal Fee Arbitration Board (FAB),
in operation since 1974, at <https://www.massbar.org/public/fee-arbitration-board>. It is a
public service program of the MBA, not a government agency. The FAB's current rules and fee
schedule are published at
<https://www.massbar.org/docs/default-source/fee-arbitration-board/fab-petitions-and-rules/fabrules.pdf>.

Participation is voluntary on both sides. The Board's own rules state it does not have
authority to compel either an attorney or a client to submit to its jurisdiction. If a lawyer
declines to arbitrate, the client's only path is small claims or District Court. This is a
real difference from states that make a fee arbitration program mandatory on the lawyer once
the client asks for it; Massachusetts does not.

If both sides agree to submit, the decision is final and binding, subject only to the narrow
grounds for vacating an arbitration award under the Massachusetts Uniform Arbitration Act,
G.L. c. 251. There is no ordinary right to a trial afterward once both sides have agreed to
arbitrate.

A single arbitrator decides matters where the total fees in dispute are $10,000 or less; a
three-member panel decides matters above $10,000. There is no stated dollar ceiling on the
program itself; disputes of any size may be filed, but the panel size changes at the $10,000
mark.

The filing fee schedule depends on who is disputing what. A $125/$250 fee tied to MBA
membership status applies only to disputes between two attorneys, not to a client's dispute
with their own lawyer. For "any other dispute," which is the category a client's fee dispute
falls into, the petitioner pays an initial $25 filing fee, then a balance that rises with the
total amount of fees at issue: roughly $25 more up to $2,500 in dispute, $50 for $2,500 to
$5,000, $125 for $5,000 to $25,000, and $200 above $25,000, for a total filing cost of roughly
$50 to $225 depending on the size of the dispute. A fee waiver is available on written request.
The FAB's own rules do not set a fixed filing deadline; they require filing "within a
reasonable time" together with the filing fee, without further defining that phrase.

No Massachusetts rule found in this research makes a notice of the right to arbitrate a
precondition to a lawyer's own suit for fees.

No separate statewide or Boston-specific fee arbitration program run by the Boston Bar
Association was confirmed. The BBA operates its own lawyer referral service, but this pack
did not find a primary source confirming that BBA-referred fee disputes are routed to the MBA
board, so that claim is not made here. See the gap list in verification_ma.md.

## 4. Bar complaint process

Attorney discipline in Massachusetts is handled by the Board of Bar Overseers (BBO) and its
staff arm, the Office of Bar Counsel, at massbbo.org. The BBO's current live complaints page is
at <https://www.massbbo.org/s/complaints>. That page was checked again for this pack's most
recent retrieval date and, like the rest of the BBO's current site, it is a JavaScript-rendered
Salesforce Community page that returns no usable text to automated retrieval, whether fetched
directly or through the newest available Internet Archive snapshot of that same URL; that is a
retrieval limit, not a content problem, and it holds for the modern site regardless of how
recently it is checked (see verification_ma.md). The most recent snapshot of the BBO's older,
plain-HTML complaints page that still returns readable content is
<https://web.archive.org/web/20211006135248/https://www.massbbo.org/Complaints>; every capture
of that page after that date is only a redirect to the unreadable modern site, so this remains
the newest usable snapshot, not merely the most convenient one. ACAP is the informal, first-line
intake track: it takes a written complaint, contacts the lawyer, and tries to resolve the
problem informally, including by mediating simple fee and communication issues, before any
complaint becomes a formal disciplinary matter.

A complaint that raises only a fee disagreement, with no dishonesty or misconduct beyond the
amount charged, usually stays in this informal track or is closed without discipline. The BBO
process exists to protect the public and to discipline lawyers. It does not have the power to
order a lawyer to refund money, and this pack does not represent that filing a complaint will
produce a refund. A client seeking money back should look to Section 3 (fee arbitration) or
Section 6 (small claims), not to a bar complaint.

This research did not find a stated filing deadline in the BBO's own published disciplinary
rules for when a complaint must be filed after the underlying conduct. That is a negative
finding, not a confirmed "no deadline" holding backed by a court decision; the search stopped
at the BBO's own rules document.

Formal discipline, when imposed, ranges from a private admonition, to a public reprimand, to
suspension, to disbarment. A complainant is immune from civil liability for filing a complaint
in good faith, and complaints are kept confidential unless formal disciplinary proceedings are
initiated or the lawyer consents to disclosure.

## 5. Client protection fund

Massachusetts has a Clients' Security Board (CSB), established under S.J.C. Rule 4:04, with
its current governing rules effective September 1, 2019 under S.J.C. Rules 4:04 through 4:09,
at <https://www.masscsb.org/resources-rules>. The Board is made up of seven bar members
appointed by the SJC, and it is funded entirely from a portion of the annual registration fees
paid by Massachusetts lawyers, not from public funds.

The fund covers losses caused when a lawyer, while providing legal services within a
lawyer-client relationship, steals, embezzles, or misappropriates money or property that
rightfully belongs to the client. It explicitly excludes ordinary fee disputes and claims that
a lawyer's work was negligent, incompetent, or inadequate. Malpractice and overbilling do not
qualify; only dishonest taking of client money or property does.

A claimant cannot bring a claim until the underlying lawyer has already been disbarred,
indefinitely or fixedly suspended, resigned as a disciplinary sanction, or died. A pending
complaint against a still-practicing lawyer is not enough by itself.

The CSB states directly on its own site that the Board has no statute of limitations. Its FAQ
says the same thing in plainer terms, and then adds the warning that matters more: if you wait
several years after discovering the loss, you risk losing the cancelled checks, fee agreements,
and letters you need to prove the theft, and the Board may treat that delay as negligence that
disqualifies you from a reimbursement. So there is no deadline, but waiting still costs you.
File as soon as you discover the theft.

The site also states that the Board has no caps on reimbursements to an individual claimant.
That is unusual; most states publish a per-claim cap. Note what the site does not say: it makes
no statement about an aggregate cap on what the Board will pay out for one lawyer's misconduct.
It does report having reimbursed more than $2.8 million for the thefts of a single disciplined
lawyer, and more than $1 million each for six others, so large aggregate payouts clearly happen,
but that is a record of what was paid, not a published rule about what will be.

Since 1974 the Board reports paying more than $57 million to more than 2,500 law clients. That
history shows the program pays real amounts. It is not a promise about what any individual claim
will recover. The Board describes each reimbursement as a matter of grace, not right. It also
reimburses only the amount actually stolen, not interest on it and not punitive or consequential
damages.

## 6. Small claims

Massachusetts runs small claims sessions inside its District Court, the Boston Municipal
Court, and the Boston Housing Court, rather than in a separate small claims court.

The jurisdictional limit is $7,000, set by G.L. c. 218, § 21. That cap does not apply to
certain claims for damage caused by a motor vehicle, which can be brought in the small claims
session regardless of amount. Cities and towns may also use the small claims session to
collect unpaid personal property taxes for any amount.

Lawyers may appear in a small claims session for either side. The 2002 Trial Court Small
Claims Standards, the official guidance for how these sessions are run, and the case
McLaughlin v. Municipal Court of the Roxbury Dist., 308 Mass. 397, 405 (1941), both address a
lawyer's role in these informal sessions; the standards direct judges to keep the session
informal even where a lawyer appears for one side. A corporation may appear through a
corporate officer or authorized employee rather than through a lawyer.

A case can be transferred out of the small claims session, for example to a related civil
docket, under the small claims transfer rule. Only a party who appeared, or a defaulting
plaintiff, may claim an appeal; a defendant who never appeared and was defaulted cannot appeal
that default. An appeal must be filed within 10 days of the written notice of the small claims
decision, requires posting a bond (commonly cited at $100 for an individual party), and
results in a new trial rather than a review of the small claims record. The filing fee for a
small claims statement of claim varies with the dollar amount sought, on a published District
Court fee schedule.

No small claims rule specific to suing a lawyer, as opposed to any other defendant, was found.
A claim against a lawyer for money owed, such as an unreturned retainer, can be brought in
small claims like any other contract claim, subject to the $7,000 limit.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A claim for breach of a written or oral contract, including a dispute over a fee agreement,
must generally be brought within six years, under G.L. c. 260, § 2.

Legal malpractice claims, whether framed in contract or in tort, must be brought within three
years after the cause of action accrues, under G.L. c. 260, § 4. That same section separately
gives medical malpractice claims a three-year period with a seven-year statute of repose. The
repose clause is written for physicians, surgeons, dentists, and similar medical providers,
not for attorneys; this research found no statute of repose that applies to a Massachusetts
attorney malpractice claim. That absence is worth knowing, since it means the three-year clock
is the only outer limit, once it starts.

Massachusetts applies a discovery rule generally, meaning the clock on a claim starts when the
plaintiff actually discovered, or reasonably should have discovered, both the harm and its
cause, not necessarily on the date of the underlying conduct. This description of the general
discovery-rule test comes from a 2025 Boston Bar Association Journal discussion of the
Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court's recent restatement of the rule in a different kind of
case; it was not confirmed against a case applying the rule specifically to attorney
malpractice, so treat it as the general Massachusetts approach rather than an attorney-specific
holding.

A claim under G.L. c. 93A, the state's consumer protection statute, discussed in Section 8,
carries its own four-year limitation period under G.L. c. 260, § 5A.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

Massachusetts follows the same general approach as the national baseline: violating a Rule of
Professional Conduct is not, by itself, an actionable claim against a lawyer. This is stated
directly in the current Scope section of the Massachusetts rules, adopted by SJC order
effective October 1, 2022, which says a violation of a rule should not itself give rise to a
cause of action against a lawyer, and should not create a presumption that a legal duty was
breached, citing Fishman v. Brooks, 396 Mass. 643, 649 (1986). That same comment goes on to
say that if a plaintiff can show a particular rule was meant to protect someone in their
position, a violation of that rule can be used as some evidence of the lawyer's negligence in
an otherwise recognized claim such as malpractice.

A separate, genuinely Massachusetts-specific path exists outside the rules of professional
conduct: G.L. c. 93A, the state's unfair and deceptive practices statute. Massachusetts courts
have generally held that the rendering of legal advice and legal services itself is not "trade
or commerce" for purposes of c. 93A, so a straightforward claim that a lawyer gave bad legal
advice does not support a c. 93A claim. Tetrault v. Mahoney, Hawkes & Goldings, 425 Mass. 456
(1997). But billing practices are treated differently: where a lawyer's fee is not adequately
substantiated or explained, or the billing itself is unfair or deceptive, at least one federal
appeals court applying Massachusetts law has allowed a c. 93A claim to proceed against a lawyer
over the bill, which can carry multiple damages and attorney's fees for the client. Sears,
Roebuck & Co. v. Goldstone & Sudalter, P.C., 128 F.3d 10 (1st Cir. 1997) (applying
Massachusetts law). A further case sometimes cited for this line of reasoning could not be
independently verified this session and has been left out; see the gap list in
verification_ma.md. This is a narrower path than a general
malpractice or billing complaint, and it turns on whether the fee itself, not the underlying
legal work, was inadequately explained or unsubstantiated.

For most readers, the practical money routes remain: a contract claim in small claims or
District Court (Section 6), the MBA's voluntary Fee Arbitration Board if the lawyer will agree
to it (Section 3), and the Clients' Security Board only where the loss involves dishonest
taking of money or property, not a fee dispute (Section 5).
