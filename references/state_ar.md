# Arkansas: handling a dispute with your own lawyer

This section is for a person in Arkansas who has a problem with their own lawyer over fees,
billing, communication, getting their file back, or an advance payment that was never earned
or never refunded. It does not cover legal malpractice claims or how to run one yourself.
Retrieved August 20, 2026.

What is different about Arkansas:

- Arkansas requires every lawyer trust account, IOLTA or not, to carry automatic overdraft
  notification to the disciplinary office. The bank itself must report any instrument presented
  against insufficient funds, whether or not the bank honors it, directly to the Executive
  Director of the Office of Professional Conduct. Read section 2. This is the closest thing
  Arkansas has to a built-in check on a lawyer mishandling client money, including an unearned
  advance a lawyer refuses to return.
- Arkansas has no statewide, client-triggered fee arbitration program. The only arbitration
  option is a discretionary referral the disciplinary office's Executive Director can make
  after a complaint is already filed, and only for conduct minor enough to fall short of
  formal discipline. Read section 3 before counting on arbitration as a forced remedy.
- Arkansas disciplinary complaints are exempt from any statute of limitations, and a
  disciplinary sanction can include restitution to a person the lawyer financially injured,
  on top of costs and a fine of up to $25,000. Read section 4.
- A commonly repeated claim that Arkansas Code section 16-17-606 bars attorneys from small
  claims division is out of date: that section was repealed. The attorney-representation
  rule is not in the statute and it is not spelled out in Rule 10 itself either. Rule 10(f)
  of the Arkansas District Court Rules just points to Supreme Court Administrative Order
  Number 18, and it is Order 18 that carries the actual rule. Read section 6, and treat any
  source that cites 16-17-606, or that cites Rule 10 alone without Order 18, as incomplete.
- An appeal from a small claims judgment costs more than the original small claims filing
  fee. A second filing fee applies in circuit court when the appeal is filed there. Read
  section 6 before assuming the small claims filing fee is the only cost of a full appeal.
- The Arkansas Code of 1987, the state's official statute text, sits behind a login-gated
  Lexis portal for the general public. Every statute citation in this pack was confirmed
  against a Justia mirror copy, not the state's own site, because the state's own site could
  not be fetched without a login. Flagged accordingly throughout.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct: numbering and adoption

Arkansas's lawyer conduct rules are the Arkansas Rules of Professional Conduct, adopted by
the Arkansas Supreme Court as the Model Rules of Professional Conduct of the American Bar
Association, as amended. The rule numbering tracks the ABA model numbering directly: the fee
rule is "Rule 1.5," communication duties are "Rule 1.4," safekeeping property is "Rule 1.15,"
and duties on termination are "Rule 1.16," with no added prefix or renumbering the way some
states use.

The Arkansas Supreme Court originally adopted the Model Rules by per curiam order on
December 16, 1985, effective January 1, 1986. In re: Matter of the Arkansas Bar Association,
No. 83-187, 287 Ark. 495, 702 S.W.2d 326 (1985). The current published text of the Rules and
their Procedures carries a "current" status as of June 12, 2025.

Two different bodies matter here, both created under the authority of the Arkansas Supreme
Court, and they are not the same body:

- The Arkansas Supreme Court Committee on Professional Conduct is the body that actually
  adjudicates formal disciplinary complaints. It is composed of two seven-member panels
  (Panel A and Panel B), each with five lawyer members and two lay members, appointed by the
  Supreme Court for six-year terms.
- The Office of Professional Conduct (OPC) is the staff office, led by an Executive Director,
  that receives and investigates complaints, prosecutes them before the Committee, and
  administers day-to-day disciplinary functions. When you file a complaint, you file it with
  OPC, not directly with the Committee.
- A separate body, the Arkansas Client Security Fund Committee, handles reimbursement claims
  for a lawyer's dishonest conduct. It is a distinct committee from the Committee on
  Professional Conduct, also appointed under the Arkansas Supreme Court, covered in section 5.

The Arkansas Bar Association (ArkBar), a voluntary membership organization, explicitly does
not handle attorney discipline; its own website states plainly that it does not handle
disciplinary actions and points the public to the Arkansas Judiciary's site instead. Do not
confuse ArkBar with OPC or the Committee.

The current Rules, Procedures, and related materials are published by the Arkansas Judiciary
(the Administrative Office of the Courts) at <https://arcourts.gov/professional-conduct>,
with the current Rules of Professional Conduct and current Procedures each linked as PDFs
from that page. Note for anyone re-checking this: the Arkansas Judiciary reorganized its
site between this pack's first draft and this verification pass, and the older
courts/supreme-court/office-of-professional-conduct path now returns a dead page. Use the
URL above.

## 2. How Arkansas's fee rule differs from the model

Rule 1.5(a) lists the same eight reasonableness factors as the ABA model rule, in the same
order: time and labor required, novelty and difficulty of the questions, and skill required;
whether the matter will preclude other employment; the customary local fee; the amount
involved and results obtained; time limitations imposed by the client or circumstances; the
nature and length of the professional relationship; the lawyer's experience, reputation, and
ability; and whether the fee is fixed or contingent. Arkansas adds nothing to this list and
removes nothing from it. Rule 1.5(b) and (c), covering communication of the fee basis and
contingent fee agreement requirements, also track the model rule closely, including the same
flat bans on a contingent fee in a domestic relations matter and in a criminal defense.

Where Arkansas goes beyond the model rule is in Rule 1.15, safekeeping property and trust
accounts. Rule 1.15(b)(5) requires every lawyer trust account, whether an IOLTA account or a
separate non-IOLTA account, to carry automatic overdraft notification to the OPC Executive
Director. The reporting requirement is not limited to instruments the bank refuses to honor;
it also applies "in the case of instruments that are presented against insufficient funds
but which instruments are honored," meaning the disciplinary office can learn about a
trust-account shortfall even if the bank quietly covers it and the client never notices.
This is a structural check most states do not put directly into the black-letter rule text
the same way. Rule 1.15(b)(3) also caps at $500 the amount of a lawyer's own money that may
sit in a client trust account, and only to cover bank service charges, consistent with the
usual model-rule commingling ban.

Rule 1.16(d), on termination of representation, matches the model rule: a lawyer must take
reasonably practicable steps to protect the client, including "surrendering papers and
property to which the client is entitled and refunding any advance payment of fee or expense
that has not been earned or incurred." This kit did not find a Arkansas-specific addition to
this duty. If your former lawyer is holding your file, this is the rule to cite; it does not
give the lawyer a right to hold the file hostage over an unrelated fee dispute, though the
lawyer may retain papers "to the extent permitted by other law," a qualifier this kit did not
research further because it depends on facts outside this pack's scope.

This kit looked specifically for a stand-alone Arkansas statute or rule banning
"nonrefundable" or "earned upon receipt" fee language, or a general statutory cap on
contingent fees outside specific practice areas, and did not find one. An unearned portion of
any advance fee remains the client's money under Rule 1.15(b)(2) and Rule 1.16(d) regardless
of how the fee agreement labels it.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

Arkansas does not have a statewide, client-triggered fee arbitration program the way some
states do. This is an absence finding, not an oversight in this kit: this pack looked at the
OPC's own governing Procedures document, the Arkansas Bar Association's "Professional
Conduct" and "Alternative Dispute Resolution" section pages, and general web search for any
Arkansas fee-dispute committee, and found none of the three describing a standing program a
client can invoke on their own.

The only arbitration mechanism this kit found is narrower and not client-initiated. Under
Procedures Section 5(A)(2), once a complaint has already been filed with OPC, the Executive
Director has discretion to refer a matter involving "lesser misconduct" (conduct that would
not warrant suspending or revoking a law license) to an alternatives-to-discipline program
approved by the Supreme Court, which may include "programs for fee arbitration, arbitration,
mediation, law office management assistance, psychological counseling, continuing education,
and ethics." This is a diversion option inside the disciplinary pipeline, decided by OPC's
Executive Director, not a right the client can invoke directly, and it is not described
anywhere as producing a binding award enforceable like a judgment.

ArkBar's own "Professional Conduct" page states directly that the Bar Association does not
handle discipline. ArkBar's "Alternative Dispute Resolution" section page describes itself as
a member section for lawyers practicing in the ADR field generally, with no mention of a
client fee-dispute process.

Because there is no standing program, if your dispute is only about the amount or
reasonableness of a fee and not about dishonest conduct, your practical routes are small
claims or district court (section 6) for money owed either way, or raising the issue directly
with OPC per section 4 if the underlying conduct also looks like a rule violation.

Sources checked for this absence finding: the current Procedures document at
<https://arcourts.gov/professional-conduct> (Section 5(A)(2)), ArkBar's pages at
<https://www.arkbar.com/?pg=professional-conduct> and <https://www.arkbar.com/?pg=adrsection>,
and a search for a county-level fee-dispute program (the Pulaski County Bar Association, the
state's largest county bar, was checked specifically and its public site describes no such
program). This kit did not find a county or local bar fee-dispute program anywhere in
Arkansas, but it also could not check every one of Arkansas's roughly 75 county bar
associations individually, most of which have no independent website; treat the county-level
absence finding as reasonably thorough rather than exhaustive.

## 4. Filing a bar complaint

Arkansas's disciplinary regulator is the Office of Professional Conduct (OPC), an office of
the Administrative Office of the Courts that investigates and prosecutes complaints against
lawyers before the Committee on Professional Conduct described in section 1. OPC provides
both a printable mail-in grievance form and a fillable form that can be completed, saved, and
emailed, both published from the OPC page above.

Arkansas disciplinary complaints are exempt from any statute of limitations. Procedures
Section 4(D) states plainly: "The institution of disciplinary actions pursuant to these
Procedures shall be exempt from all statutes of limitations." There is no deadline by which
you must file a complaint, unlike most of the other deadlines in this pack.

OPC receives and dockets every complaint, and the Executive Director may initiate a complaint
on the office's own authority. If a complaint does not set out sufficient grounds for a
formal complaint but "contains information indicative of a misunderstanding or controversy
between an attorney and a client," OPC has a separate, less formal track for that kind of
dispute, distinct from the alternatives-to-discipline referral described in section 3.

The disciplinary process itself does not exist to get you money back. Its purpose is to
sanction the lawyer, ranging from a private warning up to disbarment; a complaint is not a
mechanism for the OPC or the Committee to award you damages directly. That said, Arkansas's
restitution mechanism is broader than in many states. Under Procedures Section 18, whenever a
panel of the Committee imposes any disciplinary sanction, including a consent warning, it may
also assess the lawyer the costs of the proceedings, impose a fine of not more than $25,000,
and "order restitution to persons financially injured by the conduct." This restitution
authority is not something a complainant can demand or count on, it is discretionary and tied
to whether a sanction is imposed at all, but it is a real mechanism, broader than a
probation-only restitution condition, that exists inside the Arkansas disciplinary process.

OPC's staff office is at One Capitol Mall, Suite 3D-101, Little Rock, AR 72201-1049, phone
501-376-0313, toll-free 800-506-6631. The current Rules, Procedures, grievance forms, and
annual reports are all published from <https://arcourts.gov/professional-conduct>.

## 5. Client protection fund

Arkansas's fund is administered by the Client Security Fund Committee, appointed by the
Arkansas Supreme Court and distinct from the Committee on Professional Conduct described in
section 1. The purpose of the Fund, as described by the Arkansas Supreme Court, is to protect
clients from losses caused by the dishonest conduct of an attorney.

Under Rule 4(A) through (C) of the Rules of the Client Security Fund Committee, as quoted
directly in a 2022 Arkansas Supreme Court opinion:

- The loss "must be caused by the dishonest conduct of the lawyer and shall have arisen out
  of and by reason of a lawyer-client relationship or a fiduciary relationship between the
  lawyer and the claimant."
- The claim "shall have been filed no later than three years after the claimant knew or
  should have known of the dishonest conduct of the lawyer."
- "Dishonest conduct" is defined as "wrongful acts committed by a lawyer in the nature of
  theft or embezzlement of money or the wrongful taking or conversion of money, property, or
  other things of value. A dispute over the reasonableness of a lawyer's fee is not an
  eligible claim."

That last sentence matters directly for this kit's scope: an ordinary fee dispute, even a
serious one, is not what this Fund covers. The Fund is for the lawyer stealing or converting
your money or property, not for overcharging you or refusing to negotiate. The
three-year filing deadline is strict and was enforced against a claimant who filed
approximately three and a half years after he knew of the alleged dishonest conduct; the
Arkansas Supreme Court affirmed denial of that claim as untimely. Curran v. Ark. Client
Security Fund, 2022 Ark. 217 (December 8, 2022).

This kit located the Fund's own published rules document, the "Rules of the Client Security
Fund Committee," directly on the Arkansas Judiciary's site. Rule 4 there states: "No claim
shall be allowed for an amount in excess of $100,000," with that dollar figure "amended and
effective by per curiam order October 3, 2019." That is the current per-claim cap.

The same rules also carry exclusions worth knowing about before you count on this route.
Under Rule 4(D), the Fund does not reimburse: a loss to the lawyer's own spouse, child,
parent, grandparent, sibling, partner, associate, or employee; a loss already covered by a
bond, surety, or insurance; a loss recoverable under a bank's own blanket bond; a loss to a
business entity the lawyer controls; or a loss to a government entity. The Committee has
discretion to waive some of these exclusions in a case of extreme hardship (Rule 4(E)), and
discretion to deny a claim where the claimant unreasonably or knowingly contributed to the
loss or would be unjustly enriched (Rule 4(F)). No claim is paid until the Committee on
Professional Conduct has certified that the lawyer was disbarred, suspended, resigned and
surrendered their license, or died before one of those could happen.

This kit looked for a separate annual or aggregate cap on total payouts across all claimants
in a year and did not find one anywhere in the Fund's own rules; the rules describe only the
$100,000 per-claim cap above, funded by $10 of each attorney's annual license fee credited to
the Fund. That does not mean an aggregate limit could never come up in practice if the Fund
runs short of money in a given year, only that this kit found no rule stating one. Confirm
the current caps directly with the Committee before relying on this route, since a rule
figure like this can be moved by a later per curiam order that this pack's retrieval date
would not catch.

The Client Security Fund Committee is listed among the Arkansas Judiciary's boards and
committees at <https://www.arcourts.gov/boards-committees/client-security-fund>. The
Committee's own Rules document above is confirmed against a primary arcourts.gov source,
listed in the verification file; the Curran opinion described next is a separate,
corroborating source for the same Rule 4 language, reached through a commercial mirror and
flagged accordingly.

## 6. Small claims

Arkansas's small claims division sits inside the district court system. District courts
generally have civil jurisdiction up to $25,000; the small claims division specifically is
limited to matters "involving contracts, damage to personal property and recovery of
personal property where the amount in controversy does not exceed $5,000.00," per the
Arkansas Judiciary's own description of the district courts, current as of January 1, 2025.

Arkansas is unusual on attorney representation in the small claims division: a person cannot
be represented by an attorney there. This is not, however, set out in Arkansas Code section
16-17-606, a section some secondary sources still cite; that section was repealed and its
current text reads simply "[Repealed.]" as confirmed directly against a mirror of the current
Arkansas Code. District Court Rule 10(f) itself is only a cross-reference: it points to
"Administrative Order Number 18 for restrictions on the participation of attorneys and
prohibitions against certain parties initiating small claims actions." The actual rule lives
in Administrative Order Number 18, section 4(a): "No attorney-at-law or person other than the
plaintiff and defendant shall take part in the filing, prosecution, or defense of litigation
in the small claims division. When any case is pending in the small claims division of any
district court and the judge of the court determines that an attorney is representing any
party in the case, the case shall immediately be transferred to the civil docket." The same
section adds a proviso: this restriction "shall not be construed to abridge in any way the
rights of persons to be represented by legal counsel," meaning a person can still hire a
lawyer, but doing so moves the case out of small claims and into the regular civil docket,
with its higher filing fee and formal procedure. Order 18, section 4(b) also bars certain
entities from filing in small claims division at all: a collection agency, collection agent,
or assignee of a claim, and any business primarily or secondarily engaged in lending money at
interest.

Business entities appearing in small claims division are more restricted than a plain
"officer or authorized agent" description suggests. Under Order 18, section 4(c), only a
closely held corporation may sue or be sued there at all: one organized under Arkansas law
with no more than three stockholders, or one where at least 85 percent of the voting stock is
held by relatives within the third degree, or a closely held corporation acting by unanimous
shareholder vote. Where a corporation does qualify, it "shall be represented in the
proceedings by an officer of the corporation," not by an authorized agent generally and not
by an outside attorney.

There is no jury trial in district court; Arkansas Code section 16-17-703 states there "shall
be no jury trials in district court," and to preserve the right to a jury trial, "all appeals
from judgment in district court shall be de novo to circuit court," meaning a full new
hearing rather than a review of the first one.

The filing fee for initiating a case in the small claims division of district court is
$50.00, versus $65.00 for the civil division generally, per Arkansas Code section 16-17-705.
No portion of the filing fee is refunded.

An appeal costs more than that original filing fee. Because the district court appeal goes de
novo to circuit court, it is filed as a new case there, and circuit court has its own,
separate filing fee. Arkansas Code section 21-6-403(b)(1) sets the circuit court filing fee,
"including appeals," at $150.00. That is on top of, not instead of, the $50.00 already paid
in small claims division; a person budgeting for a full appeal should expect roughly $200
total in filing fees alone, before any cost of service or other court costs. This kit reached
section 21-6-403 through a commercial mirror, not the state's own gated statute portal, so it
is flagged rather than confirmed, but the two-fee structure itself follows directly from the
de novo appeal process described in Arkansas Code section 16-17-703.

On venue, Arkansas Code section 16-17-706(b) sets out small-claims-specific venue rules tied
to Arkansas Constitution Amendment 80, section 7: generally, where the defendant contracted
to perform an obligation, where the defendant resides, or where an injury to person or
property occurred. This kit did not find county-by-county variation in the dollar limit
itself; the $5,000 figure is a statewide limit for the small claims division wherever it is
established.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A dispute over what you owed your lawyer, or what your lawyer owed you back, under your fee
agreement is a contract claim, and Arkansas splits the period by whether the agreement was
written or not. Arkansas Code section 16-56-111 gives a written contract, obligation, or
instrument five years, with the period restarting on a partial payment or a written
acknowledgment of the debt. Arkansas Code section 16-56-105(3) gives an action founded on "any
contract or liability, express or implied," not in writing and not under seal, three years.
An ordinary attorney fee agreement that was never reduced to writing would fall under this
three-year period; a signed written fee agreement would fall under the five-year period.

This kit also looked for how Arkansas treats a legal malpractice claim specifically, since
that is a different question from an ordinary fee dispute and this pack does not cover how to
bring a malpractice claim. The Arkansas Supreme Court has held that a legal malpractice claim
carries a three-year limitation period, and that the period runs from the date of the
lawyer's negligent act, not from when the client discovered it. In Chapman v. Alexander, 307
Ark. 87, 817 S.W.2d 425 (1991), the court applied what it called the "occurrence rule" and
expressly rejected three alternative accrual rules a client might expect: the discovery rule,
a separate "date of injury" rule, and a rule tolling the clock until the attorney-client
relationship ends. Under Arkansas's occurrence rule, the clock starts when the lawyer's
negligent act or omission happened, even if the client had no way to know about it yet,
unless the lawyer concealed the wrongdoing. This is a real trap: a client who does not
discover a lawyer's error until years later can find the three-year period has already run.

This finding is flagged rather than confirmed under this kit's sourcing standard, because it
was reached through CourtListener's public opinion page rather than the Arkansas Judiciary's
own site, and CourtListener's own text is an OCR scan that may contain typos. It is also
fact-specific: a later case with different facts, or a written engagement agreement with its
own terms, could change the analysis, and this pack did not attempt a full survey of every
Arkansas malpractice opinion since 1991. If you believe you may have a malpractice claim as
opposed to a billing dispute, treat the clock as already running from when the lawyer's
conduct happened, not from when you found out about it, and get your own lawyer's advice
quickly rather than estimating the deadline yourself from this pack.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

The Arkansas Rules of Professional Conduct carry the same Scope commentary language as the
ABA model rule, in paragraph [20] of the Scope section that precedes the numbered rules:
"Violation of a Rule should not itself give rise to a cause of action against a lawyer nor
should it create any presumption in such a case that a legal duty has been breached." The
same paragraph adds that the Rules "are not designed to be a basis for civil liability,"
though a violation "does not necessarily warrant any other nondisciplinary remedy," and the
Rules exist to "provide a structure for regulating conduct through disciplinary agencies."

In practical terms, filing a rule-violation complaint against your lawyer does not by itself
put money in your hands. Your money routes are the ones covered above: a contract claim over
the fee itself under section 7, the discretionary alternatives-to-discipline referral in
section 3 if OPC chooses to make one, the Client Security Fund in section 5 if the conduct was
dishonest in the sense described there, the disciplinary restitution mechanism in section 4 if
a sanction is imposed, or small claims or district court under section 6 for a straightforward
money claim.
