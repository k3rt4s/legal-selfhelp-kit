# Kansas

This pack is for someone in a dispute with their own lawyer in Kansas over fees, billing,
communication, getting their file back, or an unearned advance. It is not for a malpractice
claim, though the malpractice limitation period is in section 7 so you know a clock exists.
Retrieval date for this pack is 2026-08-20. Read
[national_baseline.md](national_baseline.md) first.

What is different about Kansas:

- Kansas gives a client an affirmative right, on the client's own application, to have a fee
  contract reviewed by the court for reasonableness, with the court empowered to set and allow
  a reasonable fee if the contract is not reasonable. This is not in the ABA model rule.
- Kansas has no statewide attorney fee arbitration or fee dispute program. The disciplinary
  office's own complaint form says so directly and points instead to two county-level fee
  dispute committees, Sedgwick County and Topeka Bar Association (Shawnee County), that do not
  cover the rest of the state.
- Kansas small claims (the small claims procedure act) bars either side from being represented
  by an attorney before judgment, with a narrow exception, and caps any one person to 20 small
  claims filings in the same court in a calendar year.
- Kansas renumbered its rules of professional conduct. Older material may cite "Rule 226."
  The current rule is Rule 240. If you search for "Rule 226" today you will land on an
  unrelated attorney-discipline-hearing rule, not the conduct rules.
- Kansas's Scope section for its rules of professional conduct does not include the sentence,
  present in the current ABA model, saying a rule violation may be used as evidence of a
  breach of the standard of conduct in an otherwise recognized claim such as malpractice.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct, numbering and adoption

The Kansas Supreme Court adopts the Kansas Rules of Professional Conduct (KRPC) by supreme
court rule, published in the "Attorney Discipline" section of the court's own rules
publication. The current rule number is Rule 240, and the fee rule within it is commonly cited
as "Rule 240, 1.5" or, informally in correspondence, "KRPC 1.5." The official publication
point is the Kansas judicial branch's rules and orders page,
<https://www.kscourts.gov/Rules-Orders/Rules> , which links to individual rule pages such as
<https://www.kscourts.gov/Rules-Orders/Rules/1-5-Client-Lawyer-Relationship-Fees> for Rule 240,
1.5.

A citation trap worth naming plainly: material written before a January 1, 2021 renumbering
cites the conduct rules as sitting inside "Rule 226." That rule number is still in use today,
but for something else entirely, a rule governing final hearing reports in attorney discipline
proceedings. A reader following an older citation to "Rule 226" expecting the fee rule will
land on the wrong page. Use Rule 240 and its lettered subrules (1.5, 1.4, 1.15, 1.16, and so
on) for anything current.

Kansas otherwise follows the model numbering for the individual conduct rules themselves,
1.4, 1.5, 1.15, 1.16, and so on, so a reader who has the right top-level rule number (240) will
find the familiar decimal numbering underneath it.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

Kansas's Rule 240, 1.5(a) lists the same eight reasonableness factors as the ABA model rule, in
the same order and substance: time and labor and difficulty, whether taking the case
foreclosed other work, the customary local fee, the amount involved and results obtained, time
limitations, the length of the relationship, the lawyer's experience and reputation, and
whether the fee is fixed or contingent. No added or reworded factor was found on this point.

Where Kansas departs is in the surrounding structure of the rule, not the factor list:

- Rule 240, 1.5(b) keeps the model's communication requirement (basis or rate of the fee,
  preferably in writing, before or within a reasonable time after the representation begins).
- Rule 240, 1.5(c) is a Kansas-specific clause with no model-rule counterpart: a court's
  determination that a fee is not reasonable is not, by itself, presumptive evidence of a
  disciplinary violation. This separates the civil reasonableness question from the
  disciplinary question in a way the model rule does not.
- Rule 240, 1.5(d) and (f) carry the model's contingent fee writing and disclosure
  requirements, and the model's two flat prohibitions (a fee contingent on securing a divorce
  or on the amount of alimony, support, or a property settlement, and a contingent fee for
  representing a criminal defendant), plus a third: a contingent fee in any other matter where
  a statute precludes one.
- Rule 240, 1.5(e) is the pack's headline finding: on the client's own application, all fee
  contracts are subject to review and approval by the court having jurisdiction of the matter,
  and the court has authority to determine whether the contract is reasonable. If the court
  finds it is not, the court sets and allows a reasonable fee in its place. This is a direct,
  named path to challenge a fee agreement in court that the model rule does not give a client
  at all. The right is a real one in practice, not just rule text: the Kansas Supreme Court has
  called 1.5(e) "a vehicle for clients to seek court intervention in attorney fee contract
  disputes." Ryder v. Farmland Mut. Ins. Co., 248 Kan. 352, 807 P.2d 109 (1991). The same court
  has also held that a client is not required to use the 1.5(e) procedure before pursuing a fee
  dispute another way. Gerhardt v. Harris, 261 Kan. 1007, 934 P.2d 976 (1997). So 1.5(e) is an
  option on top of your other paths in this pack, not a mandatory first step and not your only
  one.

No express nonrefundable-fee rule was found in Rule 240, 1.5 itself. This is not confirmed
absent; it means the text of 1.5 as fetched does not use that term, and no other provision
naming nonrefundable fees was located in the material checked for this pack.

On whether a lawyer can hold your file for unpaid fees: Kansas's discharge rule, Rule 240,
1.16(d), tracks the model rule (notice, time to get new counsel, surrendering papers and
property the client is entitled to, refunding unearned advance fees), and adds the same
qualifier the model rule has, that papers may be retained "to the extent permitted by other
law." Kansas's "other law" is K.S.A. 7-108, a statutory attorney's lien: a lawyer has a lien
for a general balance of compensation on client papers that came into the lawyer's possession
in the course of the representation, and on money in the lawyer's hands belonging to the
client, but only from the time the lawyer gives the client written notice of the lien. Read
together, a Kansas lawyer cannot simply keep your file for nonpayment without first giving you
that written lien notice, and Comment 9 to Rule 1.16 confirms retention is allowed only to the
extent the law (here, the lien statute) permits.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

Kansas has no statewide fee arbitration or fee dispute program. This is stated directly by the
Office of the Disciplinary Administrator's own complaint form: "Please be advised that we do
not settle fee disputes... There is no state-wide fee dispute committee." Source:
<https://www.kscourts.gov/KSCourts/media/KsCourts/Attorney%20Discipline/attorney-complaint-form.pdf> .

In its place, two local, county-limited fee dispute committees were named on the same form:

- Sedgwick County Fee Dispute Committee, (316) 263-2251. This is the Wichita Bar Association's
  Fee Dispute Complaint process; the phone number on the complaint form is the WBA's own main
  line. The WBA publishes an intake form (nature of the representation, the fee agreement, the
  amount in dispute, the reason for the dispute) that you send to the WBA at 225 N. Market,
  Suite 200, Wichita, KS 67202. Source: <https://www.wichitabar.org/page/FeeDisputeForm> . The
  WBA's own materials, as fetched, do not state whether the process is mandatory on the lawyer,
  whether the outcome binds either side, any dollar ceiling, any filing deadline, or any fee to
  file, so those points stay unconfirmed; see the gap list in
  [verification_ks.md](verification_ks.md).
- Topeka Bar Association Fee Dispute Committee, (785) 233-3945. This is the TBA's own "Fee
  Dispute Resolution Panel," made up of volunteer TBA members, for Shawnee County matters.
  The TBA's own intake form states the panel's process directly: it "receive[s], hear[s], and
  make[s] confidential non-binding recommendations on client complaints concerning fee
  disputes or expenses charged by an attorney." So this one is confirmed non-binding on both
  sides: it is a recommendation, not a decision either party has to accept. Source:
  <https://www.topekabar.com/page-18143> . No dollar ceiling, filing deadline, or filing fee
  was found; those stay unconfirmed.

Treat both as a real process you can start, not a court-style hearing with fixed rules. If you
are outside Sedgwick or Shawnee County, this pack found no local Kansas bar running a
comparable fee dispute process for you to use. Your remaining paths are the court review right
under Rule 240, 1.5(e) (section 2 above), small claims (section 6), or a regular civil contract
claim (section 7).

## 4. Bar complaint process

Kansas's disciplinary regulator is the Office of the Disciplinary Administrator (ODA), part of
the Kansas judicial branch. A complaint must be in writing, contain the complainant's name and
address, and be signed by the complainant; one complaint form per attorney. The current
complaint form and its instructions are published at
<https://www.kscourts.gov/KSCourts/media/KsCourts/Attorney%20Discipline/attorney-complaint-form.pdf> .
General information is at
<https://www.kscourts.gov/Attorneys/Office-of-Disciplinary-Administration/Office-of-the-Disciplinary-Administrator> .

The complaint form states plainly, in the same fee disputes paragraph cited in section 3, that
the ODA does not settle fee disputes; it directs a fee complaint toward the two local
committees named above instead of investigating it as discipline. Filing a billing complaint
as a formal disciplinary complaint is unlikely to produce a refund; it can lead to
investigation and, if warranted, discipline of the lawyer, which is a separate outcome from
getting money back.

No confirmed filing deadline for a bar complaint was found in the material checked; nothing
located stated a time limit, and nothing located stated affirmatively that none exists, so this
is left as a gap rather than a claim either way.

At the time of this pack's retrieval, the ODA's own page noted an office relocation in
progress: the office was working remotely during construction and expected to move into a new
address, Kansas Judicial Center, 301 SW 10th Ave., Topeka, KS 66612, later in the year. Its
phone number, 785-435-8200, was stated to remain in service throughout the transition.
Confirm the current mailing address before mailing anything.

Kansas's Rule 212 Attorney Diversion Program is an alternative to a full disciplinary
proceeding for a lawyer facing a complaint. The rule text fetched for this pack
(<https://www.kscourts.gov/Rules-Orders/Rules/Rule-212>) describes the
program's structure and eligibility but contains no restitution or client-fee-refund term. This
pack did not find current authority for the diversion program ordering a fee refund to a
client as one of its conditions. A single older case annotation, from before the 2021 rule
renumbering, described restitution ordered as a form of discipline in one historical matter,
which is not the same thing as a current diversion-program term and is not relied on here; see
the gap list.

## 5. Client protection fund

Kansas's Lawyers' Fund for Client Protection is administered by the Client Protection Fund
Commission and governed by Supreme Court Rule 241. Program information:
<https://www.kscourts.gov/Attorneys/Office-of-Disciplinary-Administration/Lawyer-s-Fund-for-Client-Protection> ;
the rule itself: <https://www.kscourts.gov/Rules-Orders/Rules/Rule-241> .

What it covers: economic loss caused by the dishonest conduct of an active Kansas-licensed
lawyer. Rule 241(l)(3) defines "dishonest conduct" to include the wrongful taking or
conversion of client money or property, refusal to refund unearned fees where the lawyer
performed no services or so insignificant a portion that the refusal amounts to a wrongful
taking, borrowing client money without intent or a reasonable ability to repay it, and any
other act of intentional dishonesty proximately causing the loss.

What it excludes: the fund's own public FAQ states directly that the commission does not have
authority to discipline lawyers, resolve fee disputes, or determine legal malpractice claims,
so an ordinary billing disagreement or a malpractice claim, standing alone, is not a fund
claim. Rule 241(l)(4) lists further exclusions from an otherwise eligible dishonest-conduct
claim: a loss suffered by the lawyer's own spouse, child, parent, grandparent, sibling,
partner, associate, or employee; a loss already covered by a bond, surety agreement, or
insurance policy, to the extent covered; a loss suffered by a business entity the lawyer
controls; a loss suffered by a government entity or agency; and interest and other incidental
or out-of-pocket expenses. The Commission has narrow discretion under Rule 241(l)(5) to
consider an otherwise-excluded claim in a case of extreme hardship or special and unusual
circumstances.

Caps: not to exceed $125,000 for any one claimant, with an aggregate limit of $350,000 for all
claims against any one lawyer. Confirmed identically in both the rule text and the program's
FAQ page.

Deadline: under Rule 241(l)(2), a claim must be filed no later than one year after the
claimant knew or should have known of the lawyer's dishonest conduct. No requirement that a
disciplinary complaint be on file first was found in the rule text as fetched.

## 6. Small claims or equivalent court

Kansas's small claims court operates under the Small Claims Procedure Act, K.S.A. 61-2701 and
following, heard in the district court.

Dollar limit: a "small claim" is a claim for money or personal property where the amount
claimed or the property's value does not exceed $10,000, exclusive of interest, costs, and
certain statutory damages. K.S.A. 61-2703(a). This pack did not confirm a separate, lower
limit in any particular county.

Attorney representation, a Kansas-specific restriction: under K.S.A. 61-2707(a), the trial of
a small claims case is to the court, and except as provided in K.S.A. 61-2714, no party may be
represented by an attorney prior to judgment. A party may instead appear through a full-time
employee, officer, or other nonattorney representative. K.S.A. 61-2714 creates a mutual
escalation exception: if one party uses a person who is or was an attorney to represent them,
or a party who is themselves an attorney appears self-represented, every other party in that
case becomes entitled to have their own attorney appear on their behalf.

Filing cap, a Kansas-specific limit: K.S.A. 61-2704(b) caps any one person to no more than 20
small claims filed in the same court during the same calendar year.

Filing fee: K.S.A. 61-2704(b) also sets the docket fee to file a small claim: $35 if the claim
does not exceed $500, or $55 if it exceeds $500, unless the judge waives the fee for good
cause shown. This is stated as the only fee for filing.

Appeal, and its real cost: under K.S.A. 61-2709(a), either side may appeal any small claims
judgment by filing a notice of appeal with the clerk of the district court within 14 days of
entry of judgment. The appeal is tried and determined de novo before a different district
judge than the one who heard the small claims case. The same subsection makes K.S.A. 60-2001
applicable to the appeal. That is the general district court docket fee statute, and it currently
sets that fee at $173 to file or appeal any case in district court. In other words, the appeal
is not filed for the small claims docket fee again; it is treated as a new district court
filing and carries the much larger $173 general docket fee, not the $35 or $55 small claims
fee. Confirm the current figure before filing, since this fee is set by statute and can
change.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

Contract, written: an action on a written agreement, contract, or promise must be brought
within five years. K.S.A. 60-511(1).

Contract, oral or implied: an action on a contract, obligation, or liability that is not in
writing must be brought within three years. K.S.A. 60-512(1). A dispute over an oral fee
arrangement, or a fee arrangement never reduced to writing at all, falls here rather than
under the five-year written-contract period.

Legal malpractice: Kansas's general tort catch-all, K.S.A. 60-513(a)(4), covers "an action for
injury to the rights of another, not arising on contract, and not herein enumerated," and
carries a two-year period. This governs a legal malpractice claim framed in tort.

Kansas case law does allow a malpractice claim against your own attorney to be framed as a
contract claim in some circumstances, so which period applies can depend on what exactly went
wrong. The Kansas Supreme Court has explained the line this way: if what you are complaining
about is a breach of a specific promise your lawyer made (for example, a clear agreement to
file something by a certain date, and the lawyer simply did not do it), that can be a contract
claim, subject to the three-year period for an unwritten agreement (K.S.A. 60-512(1)) or the
five-year period if the promise was in writing (K.S.A. 60-511(1)). If instead your complaint is
about your lawyer's general professional duty of care, the ordinary case, that is a tort claim
under the two-year period above. Pancake House, Inc. v. Redmond, 239 Kan. 83, 716 P.2d 575
(1986), citing Bowman v. Doherty, 235 Kan. 870, 686 P.2d 112 (1984), and Juhnke v. Hess, 211
Kan. 438, 506 P.2d 1142 (1973). This is a real distinction with real consequences, but it turns
on the specific facts of what your lawyer did or promised, so do not rely on this pack to sort
your own situation into one category or the other; that judgment call is exactly the kind of
thing to bring to a malpractice attorney, not decide yourself from a self-help guide. This
holding was reached only through a source this pack treats as less reliable than an official
publication (see the note in verification_ks.md), so treat the general two-year tort period as
the one to plan around unless a lawyer tells you your facts support the contract theory.

Discovery rule: K.S.A. 60-513(b) provides that the causes of action listed in 60-513(a),
malpractice among them, do not accrue until the act first causes substantial injury, or, if
the fact of injury is not reasonably ascertainable until sometime after the act, until the
fact of injury becomes reasonably ascertainable to the injured party.

Statute of repose: the same subsection, K.S.A. 60-513(b), caps this discovery-rule extension:
in no event may the action be commenced more than ten years beyond the act giving rise to the
claim. Nothing found in this subsection describes any tolling of that ten-year outer limit.

Because getting your file back or your unearned fee refunded is what this kit is for, and
because a malpractice claim is a different and more serious step, these periods are given so
you know a clock is running, not as a basis to evaluate or run a malpractice claim yourself.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

Kansas's Rule 240 Scope section, paragraph [20], states that violation of a rule should not
itself give rise to a cause of action against a lawyer, and should not create any presumption
that a legal duty was breached; it adds that a violation does not necessarily warrant any
other nondisciplinary remedy either, such as disqualifying a lawyer in pending litigation.
Source: https://www.kscourts.gov/Rules-Orders/Rules/Scope-1 .

A textual difference from the current ABA model worth naming: the ABA model's Scope section
adds a further sentence saying a rule violation may nonetheless be used as evidence of a
breach of the applicable standard of conduct in an otherwise recognized claim, such as
malpractice (see [national_baseline.md](national_baseline.md), section 8). The Kansas Scope
text as fetched for this pack does not contain that sentence. This pack does not conclude from
its absence that Kansas categorically forbids using a rule violation as evidence in a
malpractice case; it states only that the qualifying sentence itself is not present in the
Scope section as currently published, which is a narrower and directly sourced finding.

Your actual money routes in Kansas, per the sections above, are: the court's fee-contract
review right under Rule 240, 1.5(e); a county-level fee dispute committee if you are in
Sedgwick or Shawnee County; small claims court up to $10,000; a regular contract claim within
the periods in section 7; or a claim to the Lawyers' Fund for Client Protection if the loss
involves dishonest conduct as that term is defined in Rule 241.
