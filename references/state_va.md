# Virginia

This pack is for a person in Virginia who has a fee, billing, communication, or file-return
dispute with their own lawyer. It does not cover legal malpractice as a claim to run yourself.
Retrieved 2026-08-20.

What a Virginia reader most needs to know, that a reader in another state does not:

- Virginia does run a statewide fee dispute program, the Fee Dispute Resolution Program (FDRP),
  organized through a Circuit Committee on the Resolution of Fee Disputes (CCRFD) in each
  circuit but governed by one set of statewide rules. It is voluntary on both sides. If the
  lawyer declines to sign the Agreement to Participate, the file is simply closed, so this is
  not a program you can force your lawyer into.
- Virginia's Rule 1.5(g) flatly bans nonrefundable advanced legal fees, something the ABA model
  rule does not address at all. A true retainer paid only to secure the lawyer's availability is
  treated differently and is earned when paid, but any fee that is really an advance payment for
  future work must stay refundable until earned.
- On termination, Rule 1.16(e) requires the lawyer to return your original documents and give
  you copies of your file, including work product, whether or not you have paid what you owe.
  The lawyer can bill you for copying costs but cannot use your unpaid bill as a reason to
  withhold the file.
- Virginia has no discovery rule for a contract-based claim. The clock starts when the breach
  happens, not when you find out about it, and Virginia treats a legal malpractice claim as a
  contract claim for this purpose.
- Virginia's General District Court has more than one dollar threshold that matters: $4,500 for
  exclusive jurisdiction, $50,000 for jurisdiction it shares with circuit court, and $5,000 for
  its small claims division specifically. Lawyers cannot appear in the small claims division
  except pro se, but a defendant can remove the case out of small claims court at any time
  before decision and hire a lawyer once removed.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct, numbering and adoption

Virginia adopts the Rules of Professional Conduct as Part Six, Section II of the Rules of the
Supreme Court of Virginia, on petition from the Virginia State Bar and by order of the Supreme
Court of Virginia. Cite them as "Rule" followed by the number, for example "Rule 1.5," the same
form the Court's own orders and the Virginia State Bar's own compiled edition use. Virginia does
not renumber the rules a client is most likely to need: Rule 1.4 (communication), Rule 1.5
(fees), Rule 1.15 (safekeeping property), and Rule 1.16 (declining or terminating
representation) sit at the same numbers a reader would expect from the model rules.

The official publication point is the Virginia State Bar's compiled *Virginia Rules of
Professional Conduct*, at
<https://vsb.org/common/Uploaded%20files/docs/rules-rpc-complete.pdf>, a document the Bar
updates as rules change and that carries a cover date of the most recent amendment incorporated.
The copy retrieved this session is dated July 27, 2026, reflecting an amendment to Rule 1.9.
Rule 1.5 was itself last amended effective July 15, 2025, by Supreme Court order, published at
<https://www.vacourts.gov/static/courts/scv/amendments/part%20six_sect_ii_rule_1_5.pdf>. Both
documents were fetched directly and read for this pack; neither is a summary.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

Virginia's Rule 1.5(a) lists eight factors for whether a fee is reasonable, and the list tracks
the ABA model factors one for one, in the same order, with no added or removed factor. That is a
real finding, checked directly against the current rule text, not assumed from the model.

Two things around the factor list do deviate, and they matter more than the list itself:

- Rule 1.5(b) requires that a lawyer's fee be adequately explained to the client, a distinct
  affirmative duty the model rule's text does not use those words for. Where the lawyer has not
  regularly represented the client, the amount, basis, or rate of the fee must also be
  communicated, preferably in writing, before or within a reasonable time after the
  representation begins. Unlike the ABA model rule, Virginia's Rule 1.5(b) does not separately
  state that a later change in the basis or rate must also be communicated to the client. If your
  rate changed and you were never told, Virginia's fee rule does not spell that out as its own
  violation the way some states' rules do; you would be arguing it under the reasonableness
  requirement in 1.5(a) instead.
- Rule 1.5(g) prohibits nonrefundable advanced legal fees outright. The rule's own comments
  explain the distinction the Bar draws: a retainer paid solely to secure the lawyer's
  availability, and not as prepayment for future work, is earned when paid and is not an advance
  fee. Anything that is really prepayment for services not yet performed must stay refundable
  until the lawyer earns it, and keeping an unearned nonrefundable fee after being discharged is
  treated as a violation of the reasonableness requirement in 1.5(a) as well. If your engagement
  letter calls a payment "nonrefundable" and it was meant to cover future work rather than the
  lawyer's mere availability, that label may not hold up under Rule 1.5(g).

Rule 1.5(c) requires a contingent fee agreement to be in writing, stating how the fee is
calculated including the percentages that apply at settlement, at trial, and on appeal, which
expenses come out of the recovery, and whether those expenses are deducted before or after the
fee itself is calculated. At the end of the matter the lawyer must give the client a written
statement showing the outcome and, if there was a recovery, how the amount paid to the client was
determined.

Contingent fees are barred in a domestic relations matter and in representing a defendant in a
criminal case. Virginia's rule allows an exception only "in rare instances," a vague standard
with no defined categories of matter it covers. Treat a contingent domestic relations fee as
presumptively improper rather than assuming any particular exception applies.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

Virginia runs the Fee Dispute Resolution Program (FDRP) statewide, overseen by a Special
Committee on the Resolution of Fee Disputes at the Virginia State Bar, with a Circuit Committee
on the Resolution of Fee Disputes (CCRFD) handling cases in each judicial circuit under one
uniform manual. This is a single statewide program administered locally, not a patchwork of
independent local bar programs. See <https://vsb.org/Site/Site/legal-help/fee-dispute.aspx> and
the *Fee Dispute Resolution Program Manual*, 2024 edition, at
<https://www.vsb.org/common/Uploaded%20files/docs/pub-rfd-rules.pdf>.

The program is voluntary for both sides. A party who wants to use it files an Agreement to
Participate and pays a one-time, non-refundable $20 administrative fee. The CCRFD chair sends the
signed agreement to the other party; if that party declines to sign, the chair closes the file
and the program cannot proceed. It is not mandatory on the lawyer, which is the single most
important fact about it: if your lawyer will not agree to participate, this program is not
available to you, and the realistic path is a written demand followed by General District Court
(Section 6).

Before mediation or arbitration, the manual gives the CCRFD chair a first, informal option called
Conciliation, described as the program's "First Resolution Option." The chair, acting as
conciliator, talks with both sides and carries proposals back and forth to see whether the dispute
settles without going to mediation or arbitration. Conciliation is not automatic and not
mandatory: the chair conciliates only where the chair decides it looks workable, or where both
parties jointly ask for it. If it produces an agreement, the file closes. If it does not, the chair
may move the case into mediation or arbitration as the parties agreed on the Agreement to Participate
form. The manual states no deadline for conciliation. Confirmed against the *Fee Dispute
Resolution Program Manual*, 2024 edition, at
<https://www.vsb.org/common/Uploaded%20files/docs/pub-rfd-rules.pdf>; the program's public web
page does not mention conciliation at all, so rely on the manual for this step.

Beyond conciliation, the program offers mediation, binding arbitration, or mediation followed by
arbitration if mediation does not resolve the dispute. A party may not move from arbitration back
to mediation.
A mediated agreement, once signed, is enforceable as any other written contract. An arbitration
award is binding and is enforceable by the circuit court; it cannot be revised or revoked except
on grounds that would apply to any arbitration award. No dollar ceiling limits which disputes the
program will hear; the amount in controversy only changes the panel size, with a single
lawyer-arbitrator usually assigned at $25,000 or below and a three-arbitrator panel typically
assembled above that figure. Mediation is scheduled within 30 days of the mediator's appointment
and arbitration hearings within 45 days of the arbitrator's appointment, both are goals stated in
the manual rather than fixed deadlines the manual describes as jurisdictional. The manual does
not state a filing deadline or window for bringing a case to the program, and no requirement that
the lawyer notify the client of the right to use the program before suing for fees was found in
the manual or on the program's public page.

The program cannot take a fee dispute that a court has already decided, and generally cannot take
one that is currently pending in court, though if both sides agree to participate and the court
matter is nonsuited or stayed, the program can proceed. Confirmed at
<https://vsb.org/Site/Site/legal-help/fee-dispute.aspx>, retrieved this session.

## 4. Bar complaint process

The Virginia State Bar's Office of Bar Counsel, through its Intake Office, is Virginia's
disciplinary body, at <https://vsb.org/Site/Site/legal-help/misconduct-claim.aspx>, phone (804)
775-0570 or toll-free (866) 548-0873. Complaints must be in writing, on the Bar's complaint form
or as a letter, and must include your contact information, the individual lawyer's name and
address (the VSB does not accept complaints about a firm, only an individual lawyer), a
description of the facts, and your signature. Source: *Virginia State Bar Complaints About
Lawyers*, at <https://www.vsb.org/common/Uploaded%20files/docs/pub-intake-brochure.pdf>.

The VSB explicitly will not open a disciplinary case over a pure fee complaint. Its own public
brochure directs a reader with an unresolved fee dispute to the Fee Dispute Resolution Program
(Section 3) instead, at the Fee Dispute Resolution Coordinator, (804) 775-9423. If your complaint
is really about the size of a bill rather than dishonest or unethical conduct, the disciplinary
process is the wrong forum from the outset, not merely a slow one.

What the process will and will not produce: the Intake Office reviews every complaint for whether
it fits within an ethics rule; if not, it is closed and you are notified, usually within a few
weeks. If it might state a rule violation, an active file is opened, a staff attorney is assigned,
and the process can take two months to a year. Possible outcomes are an admonition or reprimand,
suspension of up to five years, or revocation (disbarment). None of these produce a refund. The
brochure states directly that the disciplinary process will not recover money damages, will not
force the lawyer to take any action you want, and will not substitute for a civil remedy.

No filing deadline for a disciplinary complaint was found, either in Part Six, Section IV,
Paragraph 13 of the Rules of the Supreme Court of Virginia, the rule governing disciplinary
procedure (read in full at
<https://www.courts.state.va.us/static/courts/scv/amendments_tracked/para_13_interlineated_2023_1128.pdf>),
or in the VSB's own public complaint materials. Neither source states a time limit, and neither
states affirmatively that none exists; the pack reports the absence found rather than asserting a
rule that was not located.

If the underlying problem looks like the lawyer withholding your file over unpaid fees, Rule
1.16(e) is worth knowing about directly: on termination, the lawyer must return your original
documents, and must give you copies of your file, including work product such as drafts, research,
and internal correspondence with third parties, whether or not you have paid the fees and costs
you owe. The lawyer may bill you for the cost of copying, but your refusal or inability to pay
that copying cost cannot be used as a reason to withhold the materials. The lawyer only has to
provide this once per request, not on an ongoing basis during the representation. Confirmed
against the Bar's own compiled rules text, Rule 1.16(d) and (e).

## 5. Client protection fund

The Virginia State Bar Clients' Protection Fund (CPF) reimburses losses caused by a Virginia
lawyer's dishonest conduct, at <https://vsb.org/Site/Site/legal-help/cpf.aspx>, phone (804)
775-9426. It expressly excludes losses caused by negligence or malpractice rather than dishonest
conduct, and it is intended as a remedy of last resort, so a claimant must first pursue other
sources of recovery. Full rules at
<https://vsb.org/EN/Site/about/rules-regulations/cpf-rules.aspx>.

A reimbursable loss must be caused by the lawyer's dishonest conduct, must arise from a
lawyer-client or fiduciary relationship, must be documented, and the lawyer must be effectively
unreachable for recovery: disbarred, suspended, transferred to a disciplinary class of
membership, resigned, deceased, adjudicated incompetent, has discharged the debt in bankruptcy, or
cannot be located after reasonable efforts. There is no requirement that a disciplinary complaint
be on file first, unlike some states' funds.

Exclusions, stated in the Fund's own rules: losses of the lawyer's close relatives, partners,
associates, employers, or employees; losses by a business entity the lawyer controls; losses of
any governmental entity; losses covered by a bond, surety, or insurance policy to the extent
covered; claims for interest, late fees, penalties, or consequential and incidental damages; and
fees paid for a matter that never resulted in a settlement or judgment, where the claim is
essentially for the outcome rather than a wrongful taking.

For an unearned-fee claim specifically, the CPF rules draw a real distinction: if the lawyer
performed no legal services, or only insignificant services, and would not refund the fee, the
Board may reimburse up to 100 percent of the fees paid, treating it as a wrongful taking. If the
lawyer performed more than insignificant work but did not fully earn the entire fee and would not
refund the unearned portion, the Board may reimburse up to 50 percent of the fees paid instead.
This distinction is set out in the Fund's own Rules of Procedure.

The per-petitioner cap is $100,000 for a loss incurred on or after July 1, 2021 (lower caps of
$75,000, $50,000, and $25,000 applied to losses before that date, on a schedule set out in the
rules). No payment on account of any one lawyer or firm can exceed 15 percent of the Fund's net
worth at the time the first claim against that lawyer is filed. Confirmed at
<https://vsb.org/Site/Site/news/rules-news/20210527-cpf-rules.aspx> and
<https://vsb.org/EN/Site/about/rules-regulations/cpf-rules.aspx>.

The filing deadline is seven years from when the petitioner knew or should have known of the
lawyer's dishonest conduct, or one year after the first occurrence of a qualifying event
(disbarment, suspension, resignation, death, and the others listed above), whichever date is
later. Awards are entirely discretionary; the Board has complete discretion over whether to pay, in
what order, and how much, and establishing the Fund does not itself create any legal right to
payment.

## 6. Small claims or equivalent court

Virginia's small claims equivalent is the General District Court (GDC), and within it a Small
Claims Division that every GDC has been required to establish since 1999. Va. Code
§ 16.1-122.1, <https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title16.1/chapter6/section16.1-122.1/>.

More than one dollar figure matters here:

- The GDC has exclusive civil jurisdiction up to $4,500, and jurisdiction concurrent with
  circuit court from above $4,500 up to $50,000, both figures exclusive of interest and
  attorney fees. Va. Code § 16.1-77(1),
  <https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title16.1/chapter6/section16.1-77/>.
- The Small Claims Division specifically has jurisdiction, concurrent with the GDC generally,
  only up to $5,000, exclusive of interest. Va. Code § 16.1-122.2,
  <https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title16.1/chapter6/section16.1-122.2/>.

No county-level variation in either figure was found; both are set by statute statewide.

In the Small Claims Division, parties represent themselves. A business entity may be represented
by an owner, officer, member, or employee, but not by an attorney acting in a representative
capacity (a lawyer may appear only pro se, for their own claim). A defendant has the right to
remove the case to the regular GDC docket at any point before the judge's decision, and once
removed, may be represented by an attorney. Va. Code § 16.1-122.4,
<https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title16.1/chapter6/section16.1-122.4/>. If you sue your
former lawyer in small claims court, expect the possibility that the lawyer removes the case and
then appears with counsel.

Appeals from the Small Claims Division follow the same route as any other GDC civil appeal: a
right of appeal to circuit court, which hears the matter fresh (a trial de novo), if noted within
10 days of the judgment. Va. Code § 16.1-106,
<https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title16.1/chapter6/section16.1-106/>.

The filing fee was not confirmed as a single statewide figure; it varies by claim type and amount
under a fee schedule. Use the General District Court's own fee calculator at
<https://www.vacourts.gov/gdfees_calc_app> or ask the clerk in the county where you plan to file.

No rule specific to suing a lawyer in General District Court, beyond the ordinary jurisdictional
rules above, was found.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A fee agreement dispute, such as a lawyer's failure to refund an unearned fee, is a contract
claim. Virginia's statute of limitations for a written contract signed by the party to be charged
is five years; for an unwritten contract, express or implied, it is three years. Both run from
when the cause of action accrues. Va. Code § 8.01-246(2) and (4),
<https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title8.01/chapter4/section8.01-246/>.

Virginia has no discovery rule for a contract claim. Under Va. Code § 8.01-230, a contract
action's limitation period runs from the date the breach occurs, not from when the damage is
discovered, except for a short list of statutory exceptions this pack did not find to apply to a
fee dispute. <https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title8.01/chapter4/section8.01-230/>. If you
did not learn about a problem with your bill or your lawyer's conduct until years after it
happened, the clock in Virginia still generally started running at the time of the act, not at
discovery.

Legal malpractice in Virginia sounds in contract, not tort, for limitations purposes, even though
the underlying conduct is a lawyer's negligence. The Supreme Court of Virginia held in *Oleyar v.
Kerr*, 217 Va. 88, 225 S.E.2d 398 (1976), that an attorney's negligence in performing
professional services, while sounding in tort, is treated as a breach of contract because no duty
would exist but for the attorney-client relationship, and is governed by the contract limitations
period rather than the two-year personal injury period. Read at
<https://law.justia.com/cases/virginia/supreme-court/1976/750876-1.html>. That means legal
malpractice carries the same three-year (oral) or five-year (written) periods as any other
contract claim, under the same Va. Code § 8.01-246 cited above, rather than the two-year period
in Va. Code § 8.01-243(A) that applies to ordinary personal injury and fraud claims.

A continuing-representation rule tolls the clock, but only for the particular undertaking, not
indefinitely for the whole relationship. The Supreme Court of Virginia held in *MacLellan v.
Throckmorton*, 235 Va. 341, 367 S.E.2d 720 (1988), applying the rule from *Keller v. Denny*, 232
Va. 512, 352 S.E.2d 327 (1987), that when malpractice is claimed with respect to a particular
undertaking, the clock starts when the lawyer's services on that specific undertaking end, not
when the general attorney-client relationship ends and not when work on unrelated matters for the
same client stops. Read at
<https://law.justia.com/cases/virginia/supreme-court/1988/850761-1.html>.

Both statutes above (Va. Code § 8.01-246, § 8.01-230, § 8.01-243) were read directly at
law.lis.virginia.gov, Virginia's own official code site, not a commercial mirror. The two court
decisions are a weaker citation than the statutes are. Virginia's own courts site publishes
opinions only back to the mid-1990s, so both were read on a commercial reproduction site and
both are flagged rather than confirmed in
[verification_va.md](verification_va.md). The statutory periods are solid. If you are relying on
the malpractice clock in particular, confirm those two cases against an official reporter or ask
a lawyer, because that is the part of this section resting on the weakest source.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

The Rules of Professional Conduct are a disciplinary code. Virginia's own Scope section, in the
Bar's compiled rules, states directly that violation of a rule should not itself give rise to a
cause of action and should not create any presumption that a legal duty was breached, and that
the rules "are not designed to be a basis for civil liability." It further states that nothing in
the rules should be read to expand any substantive legal duty a lawyer already has under other
law. Confirmed against the compiled rules text, Scope section.

Whether a proven rule violation is nonetheless admissible as evidence of the standard of care in a
Virginia legal malpractice case was not researched for this pack; the kit does not cover
malpractice strategy, and this pack stops at the limitation periods a reader needs to get counsel
in time (Section 7).

Your actual money route runs through the other forums in this pack: a contract claim in General
District or Circuit Court (Section 6), the Fee Dispute Resolution Program if your lawyer will
agree to it (Section 3), or the Clients' Protection Fund where the loss involved dishonest conduct
rather than a bad bill (Section 5). Getting the lawyer disciplined (Section 4) is a separate goal
from getting money back, and it runs through a separate forum that, by its own terms, will not
produce a refund.
