# Texas

This pack is for someone in Texas trying to resolve a fee, billing, communication, or file-return
dispute with their own lawyer. It is not a guide to suing a lawyer for malpractice. Retrieved
2026-08-20.

What a Texas reader most needs to know, that a reader in another state does not:

- Texas runs no statewide fee arbitration program. Fee dispute committees exist only in some
  local bar associations (Dallas, Austin, San Antonio, and others), and participation is
  voluntary on both sides. Your lawyer does not have to agree to arbitrate.
- The Texas Disciplinary Rules of Professional Conduct (TDRPC) were renumbered effective
  October 1, 2024, when a new Rule 1.10 was inserted and several later rules shifted up one
  number. A separate amendment effective March 7, 2025 touched two unrelated rules and did not
  change any numbering. Fee rules did not move in either amendment, but if you are working from
  an older printout, check the current numbering before you cite a rule in a letter.
- A bar grievance in Texas is not a path to a refund. The Client-Attorney Assistance Program
  (CAAP) can help reopen communication with your lawyer, but it cannot recover money for you.
  Texas grievances also carry a four-year filing window tied to when the misconduct occurred,
  unlike states with no deadline at all.
- Texas's small-claims-equivalent court is Justice Court, with a $20,000 jurisdictional limit,
  and either side can appeal to a full new trial in county court.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct, numbering and adoption

The Supreme Court of Texas adopts the Texas Disciplinary Rules of Professional Conduct (TDRPC)
under its rulemaking authority. Cite them as "Rule 1.04" or "TDRPC 1.04," not "Model Rule 1.5."
The official publication point is the Supreme Court's own PDF, published at txcourts.gov and
currently posted at
<https://www.txcourts.gov/media/1459244/texas-disciplinary-rules-of-professional-conduct-pdf.pdf>,
and the current consolidated edition is dated effective March 7, 2025.

Texas does not track the ABA Model Rules number for number. Fee-related rules sit one number
behind the model: Texas Rule 1.03 (Communication) corresponds to Model Rule 1.4, and Texas Rule
1.04 (Fees) corresponds to Model Rule 1.5. This offset has been stable for years and a reader
looking for "Rule 1.5" in a Texas rule book should look for Rule 1.04 instead.

An amendment effective October 1, 2024 (Supreme Court of Texas, Misc. Docket No. 24-9054)
inserted a new Rule 1.10 (Imputation of Conflicts of Interest), which pushed several later rules
up by one number. As a result, two rules that used to also sit one number behind the model now
match it exactly: Safekeeping Property is Rule 1.15 (matching Model Rule 1.15), and Declining or
Terminating Representation is Rule 1.16 (matching Model Rule 1.16). Before this amendment, both
were one number lower. A separate amendment effective March 7, 2025 (Misc. Docket No. 25-9012)
touched only Rule 1.05 (Confidentiality of Information) and Rule 3.05 (Maintaining Impartiality
of Tribunal); it did not touch the numbering, so the renumbering itself is about five months
older than the current edition's March 7, 2025 cover date. A reader relying on an older summary
of Texas's numbering pattern should not assume the offset is uniform across the whole rule set.

Older secondary sources describe the TDRPC's adopting instrument as "Article X, Section 9 of the
State Bar Rules, effective January 1, 1990." The State Bar's own official publication of its
Rules, as amended through June 2018, shows Article X was repealed effective May 1, 1992. That
citation form is out of date. The precise current article or order number under which the TDRPC
sits was not confirmed; see the gap list.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

Rule 1.04(b) lists eight factors for determining whether a fee is reasonable, closely tracking
the eight factors in the national baseline. Texas does not add a ninth or tenth factor the way
some states do.

The real deviation is the disciplinary standard itself. Rule 1.04(a) does not discipline a
lawyer for charging an unreasonable fee. It disciplines a lawyer for an illegal or an
unconscionable fee, and defines an unconscionable fee as one "a competent lawyer could not form
a reasonable belief" was reasonable. The rule's own commentary explains why: a bare
reasonableness standard was seen as too vague and uncertain to support discipline, so Texas
adopted a narrower, harder-to-meet standard for disciplinary purposes only. In practice this
means a fee can be on the high side of reasonable, or even debatable, without crossing into
something the disciplinary system will act on. The reasonableness factors in 1.04(b) still
matter for other purposes, including a fee dispute, just not as the direct disciplinary test.

Writing requirements: for a client the lawyer has not regularly represented, the basis or rate of
the fee should "preferably" be communicated in writing, but this is not a hard requirement with
a dollar threshold. A contingent fee agreement, by contrast, must be in writing under Rule
1.04(d), with no dollar floor: it must state how the fee is calculated, state each percentage
separately if the percentage changes at settlement, trial, or appeal, describe how expenses are
deducted, and be followed at the end of the matter by a written closing statement showing the
result and how any recovery was calculated.

Contingent fees are barred outright only in one setting: representing a defendant in a criminal
case, under Rule 1.04(e). Texas does not have a rule-text prohibition on contingent fees in
divorce, custody, or other family law matters. The rule's own commentary discourages them there
in strong language, calling them "rarely justified" because family law usually creates no fund
("res") to pay a percentage fee from and because a percentage stake can create a conflict between
lawyer and client over how marital assets are valued. But that is commentary, not a flat rule
ban, and a reader should not assume a family-law contingent fee agreement is automatically
improper in Texas the way it would be in a state that bans it by rule.

No statutory percentage cap on contingent fees was found within Rule 1.04 itself. A commonly
cited cap on contingency fees in workers' compensation matters could not be confirmed against a
primary source; see the gap list. No Texas-specific "nonrefundable fee" rule, separate from the
general reasonableness and writing requirements above, was found in Rule 1.04.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

Texas runs no statewide fee arbitration program. The State Bar of Texas describes fee dispute
resolution as a local matter, handled through participating local bar associations and dispute
resolution centers, not through a single statewide body, at
<https://www.texasbar.com/Content/NavigationMenu/ForThePublic/ProblemswithanAttorney/ResolvingFeeDisagreements/default.htm>.
Programs were confirmed directly for
the Dallas Bar Association, the Austin Bar Association, the San Antonio Bar Association, and the
Houston Bar Association. Other local bars may run similar programs; confirm with your own local
bar before assuming its process matches the ones described here.

Participation is voluntary on both sides, not just the client. The State Bar's own description of
the process states plainly that it is "entirely voluntary" and that "your lawyer must agree to
participate." This is the opposite of states where a lawyer is required to arbitrate once the
client asks. In Texas, you cannot force your lawyer into the process; you can only ask, and the
programs are built around the lawyer's separate consent.

Where both sides agree to arbitrate rather than mediate, local program materials describe the
result as binding, with a party able to go to court to enforce the award if the other side does
not pay. No source located during this research stated a right to a trial after a binding
arbitration award, which is consistent with what "binding" ordinarily means, but that is an
inference from the programs' own language rather than a rule stating it directly.

No dollar ceiling or floor on what these local programs will hear was found in any of the
program materials reviewed. No statewide, Austin-specific, San Antonio-specific, or
Houston-specific filing deadline was found either; the programs' own pages describe eligibility
and process but not a time window running from the bill or the end of the representation. The
Austin Bar Association and the Houston Bar Association each state their program runs at no cost
to the parties. A filing fee for the Dallas Bar Association's or the San Antonio Bar
Association's program was not stated on its own page either way.

No requirement that a Texas lawyer notify a client of a right to arbitrate before suing for fees
was found. This is consistent with the voluntary, no-statewide-program structure: because there
is no mandatory program to notify a client of, there is nothing built into Rule 1.04 or the
disciplinary rules requiring that notice as a precondition to a fee suit.

## 4. Bar complaint process

The Office of the Chief Disciplinary Counsel, part of the State Bar of Texas, investigates
grievances against lawyers. A client can file a grievance online through the Office's own portal,
at <https://sbotservices.texasbar.com/>. The Grievance Information Helpline, (800) 932-1900, is the starting
point; it connects to the Client-Attorney Assistance Program (CAAP), a separate, voluntary,
confidential program aimed at re-establishing communication between a client and lawyer. CAAP's
own page, at
<https://www.texasbar.com/Content/NavigationMenu/ForThePublic/ProblemswithanAttorney/CAAP/default.htm>,
states it cannot recover fees or refunds from your attorney, and cannot collect money on
your behalf.

Texas does not use a simple formal-versus-informal split. Under Rule 2.10 of the Texas Rules of
Disciplinary Procedure, the Chief Disciplinary Counsel classifies every grievance within thirty
days into one of three tracks: an Inquiry (no Professional Misconduct alleged, or dismissed on
review, appealable to the Board of Disciplinary Appeals), a Complaint (proceeds to a formal
investigation of alleged Professional Misconduct), or a Discretionary Referral (referred out,
commonly to CAAP). A pure billing disagreement, where the lawyer did the work and the dispute is
only about the amount or a communication breakdown, is more likely to be classified as an Inquiry
or referred to CAAP than pursued as a Complaint. Filing it as a formal grievance does not, by
itself, produce a refund.

There is a filing window. Rule 17.06 of the Texas Rules of Disciplinary Procedure bars discipline
for Professional Misconduct that occurred more than four years before the grievance is received,
with an exception where fraud or concealment delayed discovery, in which case the clock does not
start until the client discovered, or reasonably should have discovered, the misconduct.

Discipline is not a refund. But Texas does have a disciplinary restitution mechanism, which not
every state has: under Rules 2.19 and 3.11 of the Texas Rules of Disciplinary Procedure, where
the proof establishes misconduct involving money or property, a judgment disbarring or suspending
the lawyer must require restitution, and a suspension remains in effect until satisfactory
restitution is shown.

## 5. Client protection fund

The Client Security Fund is administered by the State Bar of Texas, described at
<https://www.texasbar.com/AM/Template.cfm?Section=Client_Security_Fund1&Template=%2FCM%2FHTMLDisplay.cfm&ContentID=69888>.
Applications are reviewed by
the Client Security Fund Subcommittee, a standing subcommittee of the State Bar's Board of
Directors, and the Office of the Chief Disciplinary Counsel serves as the Fund's administrator
and legal counsel. A client applies through the same Office of Chief Disciplinary Counsel
portal used to file a grievance, at <https://sbotservices.texasbar.com/>. It compensates clients for losses from a lawyer's dishonest conduct: theft or
conversion of client money or property, and the failure to refund an unearned advance fee where
the contracted-for work was not performed. It does not cover an ordinary disagreement over
whether a bill for work actually done was reasonable.

An award ordinarily requires that a disciplinary judgment first establish the wrongdoing, unless
the lawyer has already been disbarred, has resigned in lieu of discipline, or has died. A claim
must be filed within eighteen months of the date of the disciplinary judgment establishing the
misconduct.

No published per-claim or aggregate per-lawyer cap could be confirmed. Two figures found during
research (a $40,000 cap attributed to a State Bar Article XI publication, and $50,000/$85,000
figures attributed to a Texas Administrative Code section) both traced back to unrelated
programs, not the Client Security Fund: the first is the Texas Equal Access to Justice
Program's IOLTA rules, and the second is the Texas Real Estate Recovery Trust Account for
licensed real estate agents. Neither applies to this fund. Whether awards are paid in full or at
a discretionary percentage of the loss was also not confirmed against the fund's own published
rules; see the gap list.

## 6. Small claims or equivalent court

Texas has no separate "small claims court." Small claims cases are one of several case types
heard in Justice Court, alongside debt claim cases, repair-and-remedy cases, and eviction cases.
Rule 500.1(a) of the Texas Rules of Civil Procedure defines a small claims case as a suit for
money damages, civil penalties, personal property, or other relief allowed by law, and caps the
claim at $20,000, excluding statutory interest and court costs but including attorney fees.

An individual may represent themselves or be represented by an attorney. A corporation or other
entity may appear through a non-attorney employee, owner, officer, or partner, or through an
attorney, under Rule 500.3.

Either side may appeal a Justice Court judgment to county court within 21 days of the judgment
(or of the denial of certain post-judgment motions), by filing a bond, a cash deposit, or a
Statement of Inability to Afford Payment of Court Costs, under Rule 506.1. The appeal is not a
review of the Justice Court record. Rule 506.3 requires the case to be tried de novo in county
court, meaning an entirely new trial as if the first one had not happened.

There is no single statewide filing fee for Justice Court cases; fees are set at the county level.
Confirm the current fee with the specific Justice of the Peace court where you would file. No
rule specific to suing a lawyer in Justice Court, as opposed to any other defendant, was found.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A claim on a written fee agreement is a contract claim. Texas Civil Practice and Remedies Code
Section 16.004 sets a four-year period for a suit on a debt, and Section 16.051, the state's
residual limitations statute, also sets four years for a cause of action not covered by another
specific statute. Both were reviewed only through a commercial reproduction of the Texas Civil
Practice and Remedies Code (codes.findlaw.com), not the official Texas statutes site directly;
see the flagged table.

Legal malpractice in Texas has a two-year limitation period under Civil Practice and Remedies
Code Section 16.003(a), the general personal-injury period. The Texas Supreme Court has held that
a legal malpractice claim sounds in tort for limitations purposes regardless of how it is pleaded,
so a client cannot get the longer contract period by framing the claim as a breach of the fee
agreement instead. This comes from Willis v. Maverick, 760 S.W.2d 642 (Tex. 1988), read via a
law.justia.com reproduction of the opinion rather than the official reporter; see the flagged
table.

Willis v. Maverick also applies a discovery rule to legal malpractice: the clock does not start
until the client discovers, or in the exercise of reasonable diligence should discover, the facts
giving rise to the claim. A separate Texas Supreme Court decision, Hughes v. Mahaney & Higgins,
821 S.W.2d 154 (Tex. 1991), tolls the malpractice limitations period while the client is still
pursuing an appeal in the underlying matter the malpractice claim grew out of.

No Texas statute of repose specific to legal malpractice was found. This is a negative finding,
not a confirmed absence across all of Texas law; the search stopped once no repose provision
turned up in the sources reviewed for this pack. Do not treat this as a guarantee that no outer
time limit exists.

Whether the Texas Deceptive Trade Practices Act applies to claims against a lawyer for
professional services is unsettled; Willis v. Maverick itself notes the question without
resolving it. This pack does not state a DTPA period for that reason; see the gap list.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

The TDRPC's own Preamble says so directly, at paragraph 15: a violation of a rule does not
give rise to a private cause of action, and does not create any presumption that a legal duty to
a client has been breached. Whether a rule violation is nonetheless admissible as some evidence
in a malpractice case was not researched further, because this kit does not cover malpractice
strategy.

A client's actual money routes in Texas are: a contract claim on the fee agreement, a local bar
fee dispute program if the lawyer agrees to participate, the Client Security Fund for dishonest
conduct, and disciplinary restitution under Rules 2.19 or 3.11 where a disbarment or suspension
judgment orders it.

Confirm the current TDRPC text and rule numbers directly at txcourts.gov before citing a rule in
a letter, since the October 2024 renumbering is recent and many older summaries online have not
caught up. Confirm the Civil Practice and Remedies Code sections in Section 7 directly at
statutes.capitol.texas.gov, since this pack relied on a commercial reproduction for that text.
Confirm any local bar fee dispute program's current rules directly with that bar association
before filing, since none of the three confirmed programs publish a filing deadline and program
