# Connecticut: handling a dispute with your own lawyer

This section is for a person in Connecticut who has a problem with their own lawyer
over fees, billing, communication, getting their file back, or an advance payment that
was never earned or never refunded. It does not cover legal malpractice claims or how
to run one yourself. Retrieved August 20, 2026.

What is different about Connecticut:

- Connecticut has no mandatory statewide bar association. Its rules of professional
  conduct live inside the Connecticut Practice Book, adopted by the judges of the
  Superior Court, not by a bar association or by the legislature. The Connecticut Bar
  Association is a voluntary organization, and it happens to be the body that runs the
  fee arbitration program described in section 3.
- Connecticut's fee arbitration program is voluntary for both sides by default, but it
  becomes effectively mandatory on the lawyer, not the client, in two situations tied to
  the grievance process. Read section 3 before you assume you cannot get to
  arbitration.
- A grievance in Connecticut is not sent to a bar association. It goes to a centralized
  Statewide Bar Counsel, who screens it first, and only a complaint that survives that
  screening is forwarded to one of thirteen judicial-district grievance panels for
  investigation. Read section 4 for who actually looks at what you file and when.
- Connecticut has a statute that caps contingency fee percentages on a sliding scale in
  personal injury, wrongful death, and property damage cases. This is a real dollar
  ceiling written into state law, not just a factor in a reasonableness rule. Read
  section 2.
- Connecticut's legal malpractice claims run on a strict three-year clock tied to when
  the lawyer's conduct happened, not to when you found out about it. There is no
  statutory discovery rule for legal malpractice here. Read section 7 before you wait to
  act.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct: numbering and adoption

Connecticut's rules of professional conduct for lawyers are adopted by the judges of
the Superior Court and published as part of the Connecticut Practice Book, the same
book that carries the state's civil, criminal, and appellate procedure rules. There is no
separate, standalone volume just for the conduct rules. The rules follow the same
numbering as the ABA Model Rules: Rule 1.5 on fees, Rule 1.4 on communication, Rule
1.15 on safekeeping property, and Rule 1.16 on ending representation all sit at those
same numbers in Connecticut. A reader looking for those numbers will find them there.

Cite these rules in a letter as "Rule 1.5 of the Connecticut Rules of Professional
Conduct" or simply "Rule 1.5," the same short form used elsewhere. Connecticut's own
Practice Book text refers to them this way, with no separate prefix like Wisconsin's
"SCR" form.

The version of Rule 1.5 in effect carries an amendment dated June 14, 2013, effective
October 1, 2013. The Practice Book itself is republished each year; the edition used
here is the 2026 edition, published on the Connecticut Judicial Branch's own site. The
full Practice Book, including the Rules of Professional Conduct, is published at
<https://www.jud.ct.gov/Publications/PracticeBook/PB.pdf>.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

Rule 1.5(a) in Connecticut lists the same eight reasonableness factors as the ABA
model rule, in the same order and substance: the time and labor required and the
difficulty of the questions involved, the skill needed, whether taking the case
precluded other work, the customary local fee, the amount involved and the results
obtained, any time limitations imposed, the nature and length of the professional
relationship with the client, the lawyer's experience and reputation, and whether the
fee is fixed or contingent. Connecticut does not add or remove a factor from this list,
and does not add a general writing requirement to Rule 1.5(a) or 1.5(b) beyond the
model's own limited-appearance addition described below.

Where Connecticut departs from the model is not in the reasonableness factors
themselves, but in a separate word that shows up in the grievance process described
in section 4: a pure fee dispute, one that does not allege a "clearly excessive or
improper fee," can be dismissed from the disciplinary track and diverted toward fee
arbitration instead. That word does not appear in Rule 1.5(a) itself, which still uses
"unreasonable," the same as the model rule. It appears in the grievance-screening
standard, and it matters because it can decide whether your complaint is investigated
as misconduct or routed to arbitration.

Connecticut also has a statute the model rules do not have at all: General Statutes
section 52-251c caps contingency fee percentages on a sliding scale in personal injury,
wrongful death, and property damage cases. As read directly from the statute, the cap
runs 33 1/3 percent of the first $300,000 recovered, 25 percent of the next $300,000,
20 percent of the next $300,000, 15 percent of the next $300,000, and 10 percent of
any amount over $1,200,000. A claimant, not a court, can waive this cap, and the
statute does not require a court petition or court approval to do it. Before a claimant
signs a fee agreement above the cap, the lawyer must explain the cap and the reason
the lawyer wants more, tell the claimant about the right to find another lawyer who
will honor the cap, and give the claimant time to consider that. The waiver itself must
be in writing, must spell out the capped fee schedule in full, must carry a specific
boldface disclosure sentence set out in the statute itself, and must be signed and
acknowledged before a notary. Even with a valid waiver, the total fee can never exceed
33 1/3 percent of what the claimant actually recovers, and the claimant does not have
to repay the lawyer's litigation costs if there is no recovery. If your dispute is over a
contingency fee in one of these case types, this statute, not just Rule 1.5, sets the
outer limit on what your lawyer could lawfully take. The statute is published at
<https://www.cga.ct.gov/current/pub/chap_901.htm>.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

Connecticut runs a single statewide program, but it is not run by a court or by a state
agency. It is the Lawyer-Client Fee Dispute Resolution Program administered by the
Connecticut Bar Association, a voluntary membership organization, under the
Program's Rules of Procedure, approved by the CBA's Board of Governors on May 22,
2023.

By default, participation is voluntary for both sides. Under the Program's current Rules
of Procedure, the Program has jurisdiction over a fee dispute only if the lawyer and the
client, or a third party who agreed in writing or was ordered to pay the fee, have either
consented in writing to use the Program, or have been ordered to use it by a court, the
Statewide Grievance Committee, or another administrative body. Absent consent or an
order, neither side can be forced into the Program. Both mediation and arbitration are
offered, and the Program will only proceed once all parties have agreed, or been
ordered, to use the same specific process.

Connecticut's asymmetry runs through the grievance process, not through the fee
arbitration rules themselves, and it falls on the lawyer, not the client. Two situations
make arbitration effectively mandatory for the lawyer, and Practice Book section
2-37(b) and section 2-32(a)(3) each attach the same cost penalty to a lawyer who is
referred this way and refuses to arbitrate:

1. As a disciplinary sanction. Under Practice Book section 2-37(a)(6), a reviewing
   committee or the Statewide Grievance Committee, after finding misconduct, can
   order a lawyer to submit a fee dispute to CBA fee arbitration. Section 2-37(b) then
   states that a party who refuses to use the CBA's no-cost fee arbitration service
   must pay the cost of the arbitration, and section 2-37(c) adds that a lawyer who
   does not comply with the order at all risks being presented to the Superior Court for
   discipline.
2. At the intake stage of a grievance complaint. Practice Book section 2-32(a)(2)(A)
   defines the ground: a complaint that raises only a fee dispute, without alleging a
   clearly excessive or improper fee. Section 2-32(a)(3) is the actual diversion
   authority built on that ground: it lets Statewide Bar Counsel stay the complaint and
   refer the parties to fee arbitration instead of investigating it as a disciplinary
   matter, makes the arbitration result dispositive of the complaint, and states that a
   party who refuses to use the CBA's no-cost arbitration service must pay the cost of
   the arbitration.

In both paths, the cost-shifting rule comes from the Practice Book itself, a rule of
court, not from the CBA Program's own Rules of Procedure, which do not contain it. It
is a financial deterrent to the lawyer declining, not a legal compulsion to arbitrate.

Outside of those two grievance-linked paths, a lawyer who was never referred through
either route can simply decline to consent to the Program, and the Program has no
independent power to compel it.

There is no dollar floor or ceiling stated in the Program's Rules of Procedure on what
the Program will hear. There is no charge for using the Program. The CBA's public
program page describes an arbitrator's decision as binding once the parties have
consented or been ordered in; the Rules of Procedure themselves do not use the word
"binding," but state that the award will say who prevails, the amount due, and payment
terms, and does not need to include findings of fact or legal reasoning, though the
arbitrator may add them. Whether a hearing is held, rather than a decision on written
submissions alone, is decided case by case at a scheduling conference with the
arbitrator; the current rules do not tie that choice to a dollar threshold. Where a
hearing is held, it ordinarily begins within 60 days of that scheduling conference, and
the arbitrator must issue the award within 30 days after the hearing concludes or the
last post-hearing submission is filed. No separate filing deadline or limitations period
specific to requesting CBA fee arbitration was found in the Program's own rules; treat
the general contract limitation period in section 7 as the practical outer limit instead.

The Connecticut Bar Association's public page for this program, including how to
request it, is at
<https://www.ctbar.org/public/lawyer-client-fee-dispute-resolution-program>. The
Program's current Rules of Procedure are published at
<https://www.ctbar.org/docs/default-source/access-to-justice/dispute-resolution/lawyer-client-fee-dispute-rules-bog-approved.pdf>.

## 4. Bar complaint process

Connecticut calls this process a grievance, not a bar complaint, and it is not handled
by a bar association. It runs through the Connecticut Judicial Branch's Statewide
Grievance Committee system, which has three layers: a centralized Statewide Bar
Counsel who receives and screens every complaint, thirteen judicial-district grievance
panels that investigate complaints that survive screening, and the Statewide Grievance
Committee itself, which reviews panel findings and imposes discipline in matters
referred to it.

A grievance complaint is filed with the Statewide Bar Counsel's office, not with a local
panel directly. Bar Counsel reviews each complaint on intake and, under Practice Book
section 2-32(a)(2), can dismiss it without investigation on stated grounds, including
that it does not allege facts that would constitute misconduct, that it is a pure fee
dispute not alleging a clearly excessive or improper fee, or that it duplicates a matter
already decided. A complaint that is not dismissed at this stage is then forwarded to a
judicial-district grievance panel for investigation. A grievance complaint generally must
be filed within six years of the conduct complained of, subject to exceptions stated in
the Practice Book.

The grievance process is disciplinary, not compensatory, and Connecticut says this
directly in its own public materials for complainants: the process exists to protect the
public and the legal profession, and discipline is not the same thing as getting your
money back. That said, Practice Book section 2-37(a) does give a reviewing committee
or the Statewide Grievance Committee power, after a misconduct finding, to order a
range of sanctions, including restitution to the client and referral to CBA fee
arbitration as described in section 3 above. As with any discretionary sanction, do not
count on restitution as a predictable outcome of filing a grievance; it depends on a
misconduct finding being made at all, and on the reviewing body choosing that
particular sanction among several available to it.

The Judicial Branch's page for filing a grievance against an attorney is at
<https://www.jud.ct.gov/sgc/file_complaint_attorney.htm>. The Statewide Grievance
Committee's general public information page is at
<https://www.jud.ct.gov/sgc/for_public.htm>.

## 5. Client protection fund

Connecticut's fund is the Client Security Fund, established under Practice Book
sections 2-68 through 2-81 and administered by a Client Security Fund Committee
appointed under the Practice Book, not by a bar association. The Committee's own
description states the fund exists "to provide reimbursement to individuals who have
lost money or property as a result of the dishonest conduct of an attorney practicing
law in the State of Connecticut, in the course of an attorney-client relationship," and
that it is a remedy of last resort for a client who cannot get reimbursed from any other
source.

"Dishonest conduct" is defined narrowly, in the Committee's own words, as wrongful
acts in the nature of theft or embezzlement, or the wrongful taking of money, property,
or other things of value, in an attorney-client or fiduciary relationship arising from one.
The Committee's own materials state that it will, in limited circumstances, also
reimburse a loss based on a lawyer's refusal to refund unearned fees paid in advance.
Ordinary negligence or malpractice that does not rise to dishonest conduct does not
qualify.

The fund's stated exclusions include: a loss from investment services provided by an
attorney; a loss presented more than four years after it was discovered or should have
been discovered; a loss suffered by a close relative, business associate, partner, or
employee of the attorney who caused the loss; a loss resulting from malpractice or
negligence rather than dishonest conduct; and a loss covered by an insurance policy,
bond, or surety agreement to the extent already covered.

No published per-claim or aggregate dollar cap could be found for this fund. The
Committee's own Regulations state that it determines, "in its sole and absolute
discretion," the amount, order, and manner of payment on each approved claim, based
on the claims already approved, the claims being processed, and the amount available
in the fund at the time. This is a genuine absence, not an unconfirmed number left out
of caution: the Committee's own governing document describes a discretionary,
fund-balance-dependent payment process rather than a stated maximum.

The Client Security Fund's main page, including how to file a claim, is at
<https://www.jud.ct.gov/CSF/>. Its published Regulations are at
<https://www.jud.ct.gov/CSF/regs.htm>, and a public FAQ page is at
<https://www.jud.ct.gov/CSF/FAQ.htm>.

## 6. Small claims or equivalent court

Connecticut hears small claims cases in the Small Claims session of the Superior
Court, not in a separately named court. The dollar limit is set by General Statutes
section 51-15, not by the Practice Book, at $5,000 for most claims, rising to $15,000
for claims against a home improvement contractor or a new home construction
contractor. Practice Book chapter 24 governs the procedure once a case is in small
claims.

Lawyers may appear in small claims matters; appearing is permitted, not required, and
either side may be self-represented. This kit did not find a rule requiring a corporate
party to be represented by counsel in Connecticut small claims specifically, and did not
confirm whether small claims procedures or fees vary by judicial district beyond the
statewide dollar limits above; a reader should confirm local filing details with the clerk
of the Superior Court location where they would file. General Statutes section 51-15 is
published at <https://www.cga.ct.gov/current/pub/chap_870.htm>.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A dispute over what you owed your lawyer, or what your lawyer owed you back, under
a fee agreement is a contract claim, and Connecticut splits the limitation period by
whether the contract is written or a purely oral, unperformed agreement. General
Statutes section 52-576 sets a six-year period for an action on an account, or on any
simple or implied contract, or on any contract in writing; a written fee agreement, or
one already performed in whole or in part, generally falls under this six-year period.
General Statutes section 52-581 sets a separate, shorter three-year period, but only for
an action founded on an express oral contract or agreement that was never reduced to
writing and never confirmed by a signed note or memorandum; Connecticut's own
case-annotation notes to these statutes describe the three-year period as applying only
to a still-executory oral contract, one where performance has not yet occurred, while
an executed oral contract falls under the six-year period instead. If your fee agreement
was written, or the work was already performed, expect the six-year period to apply.

Legal malpractice is different from a fee dispute, and this kit does not cover running a
malpractice claim. For reference only: legal malpractice in Connecticut sounds in tort,
not contract, and General Statutes section 52-577 sets a three-year period for any
action founded upon a tort, running from the date of the act or omission complained
of. This is an occurrence-based period, not a discovery-based one; the statute's own
case-annotation notes describe it as running from when the lawyer's conduct occurred,
not from when the client discovered the injury. Connecticut's medical-malpractice-style
discovery statute, General Statutes section 52-584, which gives a two-year discovery
period and a three-year repose period, does not by its own terms cover attorneys.
Connecticut case-annotation notes to section 52-577 do describe two judicially created
exceptions that can extend the effective deadline in a legal malpractice case: a
"continuous representation doctrine" that tolls the period while the same lawyer
continues representing the client on the same matter, and a rule that a malpractice
claim is not ripe, and the period does not begin to run, until a final judgment is
entered in the underlying matter the malpractice affected. These two doctrines come
from Connecticut's own official annotated statutes rather than from independently
reading the underlying court opinions.

Do not treat any of this as legal advice about your own deadline. If you suspect
malpractice rather than a billing or communication problem, that is outside what this
kit covers, and given how short and unforgiving this three-year occurrence-based
period is, you should get your own lawyer's advice quickly. General Statutes chapter
926, covering all of these limitation periods, is published at
<https://www.cga.ct.gov/current/pub/chap_926.htm>.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

Connecticut's Rules of Professional Conduct include a Scope section, preceding the
numbered rules, that says a violation of a rule should not itself give rise to a cause of
action against a lawyer, and should not create any presumption that a legal duty was
breached. The same section adds that because the rules do establish standards of
lawyer conduct, a violation may be used as evidence of a breach of the applicable
standard of conduct in a case that is otherwise a recognized claim, such as a
malpractice action. This tracks the ABA model's own Scope language with no
deviation found.

In practical terms, a rule violation on its own does not give you a lawsuit against your
lawyer in Connecticut. Your actual routes to money are the ones covered above: a
contract claim over the fee itself under section 7, CBA fee arbitration under section 3
where both sides submit to it or a grievance body orders it, the Client Security Fund
under section 5 if the conduct was genuinely dishonest rather than merely a fee
dispute, or a separate malpractice claim if that applies, which is outside this kit.
