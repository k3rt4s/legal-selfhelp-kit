# North Dakota

This pack is for a person in North Dakota who has a fee, billing, communication, or
file-return dispute with their own lawyer. It does not cover legal malpractice as a claim to
run yourself. Retrieved 2026-08-20.

What a North Dakota reader most needs to know, that a reader in another state does not:

- North Dakota has a statewide attorney fee arbitration program, run by the State Bar
  Association of North Dakota (SBAND), not the courts, through two regional panels. Those
  panels are named for seven of North Dakota's eight judicial districts; the newest district
  is not named in either panel's list.
- North Dakota bars a lawyer from holding your file for unpaid fees outright. Rule 1.19 of the
  Rules of Professional Conduct says a lawyer may not assert a retaining lien and may not
  condition return of your file on payment of the lawyer's fee.
- North Dakota bans nonrefundable fees and nonrefundable retainers entirely, and bars any
  agreement that restricts your right to terminate the representation or to a refund of
  unearned fees. This is Rule 1.5(g), and it goes beyond the model rule.
- The Client Protection Fund is also SBAND-administered, not court-administered. Its own rule
  text sets a two-year filing deadline from when you knew or should have known of the
  dishonest conduct, but the same rule's own Comment describes the same deadline as five
  years. The pack uses two years, the operative rule text, and flags the conflict.
- North Dakota's Small Claims Court has no appeal. Using it waives both sides' appeal rights.
  The defendant's only escape is removing the case to district court before judgment, and
  removal shifts attorney's fees to the plaintiff if the plaintiff still wins.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct, numbering and adoption

North Dakota adopts the Rules of Professional Conduct by order of the North Dakota Supreme
Court. Cite them as "N.D.R. Prof. Conduct" followed by the rule number, or "Rule" followed by
the number in a letter, for example "Rule 1.5." North Dakota does not renumber the rules a
client is most likely to need: Rule 1.4 (communication), Rule 1.5 (fees), Rule 1.15
(safekeeping property), and Rule 1.16 (declining or terminating representation) sit at the
same numbers a reader would expect from the model rules. North Dakota also has Rule 1.19
(client files, papers, and property), a rule the model rules do not have at all under any
number.

The official publication point is the North Dakota Courts website, at
<https://www.ndcourts.gov/legal-resources/rules/ndrprofconduct>, which lists every rule with
its own effective date. Individual rules were read directly: Rule 1.4 at
<https://www.ndcourts.gov/legal-resources/rules/ndrprofconduct/1-4>, effective 4/1/2026; Rule
1.5 at <https://www.ndcourts.gov/legal-resources/rules/ndrprofconduct/1-5>, effective 7/1/2022;
Rule 1.15 at <https://www.ndcourts.gov/legal-resources/rules/ndrprofconduct/1-15>, effective
4/1/2026; Rule 1.16 at <https://www.ndcourts.gov/legal-resources/rules/ndrprofconduct/1-16>,
effective 8/1/2025; and Rule 1.19 at
<https://www.ndcourts.gov/legal-resources/rules/ndrprofconduct/1-19>, effective 8/1/2006. Each
rule page also carries its own version history, so a reader can confirm nothing more recent
has superseded these dates.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

Rule 1.5(a) lists the same eight reasonableness factors as the model rule, worded almost
identically, with no factor added and none removed. If you are comparing your bill against the
factors themselves, North Dakota tracks the model list.

The real deviations sit in the rest of Rule 1.5, and the biggest one is Rule 1.5(g):

- Nonrefundable fees and nonrefundable retainers are prohibited outright.
- Any agreement that purports to restrict a client's right to terminate the representation, or
  that unreasonably restricts a client's right to a refund of unearned or unreasonable fees,
  is prohibited.

This is a flat ban, not a factor to weigh. If your fee agreement calls any portion of your
retainer "nonrefundable," or says you give up your refund right by signing, that provision is
itself a rule violation in North Dakota, independent of whether the amount charged was
otherwise reasonable.

Rule 1.5(f) is a related, narrower point: a lawyer may charge a flat or fixed fee for future
services, and once paid, that money becomes the lawyer's property and can go straight into the
lawyer's operating account rather than a trust account. Rule 1.15(c) makes this an express
exception to the general rule that advance fees go into trust until earned. A flat fee is not
automatically "nonrefundable" under (g) just because it was earned on payment under (f); the
two provisions work together, and an unearned portion of a flat fee is still refundable.

Rule 1.4(c) and (d) add a disclosure requirement the model rule does not have: a lawyer must
tell a client in writing, at the time of engagement, if the lawyer does not carry professional
liability insurance. This does not apply to in-house counsel, short-term limited legal
services under Rule 6.5, or emergency representation.

Rule 1.5(b) does not add a writing requirement beyond the model rule; it still says the basis
or rate of the fee should "preferably" be communicated in writing, not that it must be.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

North Dakota runs a statewide attorney fee arbitration program under the SBAND Board of
Governors' Rules on Arbitration of Fee Disputes. There is no single public rules page; the
current rules document is published at
<https://www.sband.org/resource/resmgr/docs/for_public/rules_on_arbitration_of_fee_.pdf>.

The Board of Governors appoints two regional fee arbitration panels: one for the Southeast,
East Central, Northeast Central, and Northeast Judicial Districts, and one for the South
Central, Southwest, and Northwest Judicial Districts. That is seven of North Dakota's eight
current judicial districts; the North Central Judicial District, created in 2014, is not named
in either panel's list, and no newer version of this rules document could be found. If you are
in the North Central district, ask SBAND directly which panel handles your case.

How it works:

- Either the lawyer or the client can start the process, by filing a signed written petition
  and a signed arbitration agreement with the Executive Director of SBAND.
- The other party then has 20 days from receiving the petition, sent by certified mail, to
  sign and return the arbitration agreement. Not responding within 20 days counts as rejecting
  arbitration, and if either side rejects it, SBAND takes no further action and the parties are
  left to their other remedies. Participation is not mandatory on either side.
- If a court already has statutory authority to fix the fee, or the fee is the subject of
  pending litigation, it cannot go to this arbitration program at all.
- Disputes under $1,000 are heard by a single unchallenged panel member. Disputes of $1,000 or
  more go to a three-member board, two lawyers and one lay person, unless both sides agree in
  writing to a single arbitrator.
- Each side gets up to three peremptory challenges to panel members.
- The hearing must be held within 30 days of SBAND receiving the signed petition and agreement
  from both parties, and a written decision is due within 15 days after the matter is
  submitted for decision.
- A party may bring their own lawyer to the arbitration at that party's own expense; legal
  rules of evidence do not apply.
- The decision is final and binding on both parties, in the rules document's own words. A
  decision can be filed with the district court under N.D.C.C. ch. 32-29.3 to be confirmed and
  entered as a judgment.

No filing fee is stated anywhere in the rules document. No fee to file was found, and this
program has no confirmed dollar ceiling on how much can be arbitrated. Nothing in the rules
document requires the lawyer to notify the client of a right to arbitrate before suing for
fees.

The rules document is a scanned image, not machine-readable text, and it was read by optical
character recognition rather than a text layer. See the flagged table in

## 4. Bar complaint process

North Dakota's Disciplinary Board, established in 1965, oversees attorney discipline, at
<https://www.ndcourts.gov/supreme-court/committees/disciplinary-board>.
A complaint is filed in writing, signed and dated, with the Office of Disciplinary Counsel, 125
Slate Dr., Ste. 2, P.O. Box 2297, Bismarck, ND 58502-2297, phone 701-328-3925. You do not need
a lawyer to file.

How it works: Disciplinary Counsel reviews the complaint and decides whether an investigation
is warranted, and can summarily dismiss it. If not dismissed, Disciplinary Counsel
investigates, then refers the matter to one of three district Inquiry Committees (Southeast,
Northeast, or West), which can dismiss the complaint, issue an admonition, impose probation
with the lawyer's consent, or direct that formal proceedings begin. Formal proceedings, when
there is probable cause of misconduct warranting a public reprimand, suspension, or
disbarment, go to a hearing panel, with review by the Supreme Court available afterward.

Everything found describes a single complaint pathway through the Office of Disciplinary
Counsel. No separate informal or consumer-assistance intake track, distinct from this process,
was found on either the ndcourts.gov Disciplinary Board page or SBAND's public pages. If your
issue is really a billing disagreement rather than misconduct, expect this process to focus on
whether the lawyer's conduct violated the rules, not on getting your money back.

No filing deadline was found in the sections of the North Dakota Rules for Lawyer Discipline
reviewed for this pack (forms of discipline, additional procedure, probation, notice of
status, and professional trusteeships). This is an absence in what was checked, not a
confirmed rule or court holding that no deadline exists; if timing of an old complaint matters
to you, ask the Office of Disciplinary Counsel directly.

Discipline is not a refund, but North Dakota's discipline rules do include a money remedy the
Board can order as part of a sanction. Rule 1.3.A of the Rules for Lawyer Discipline lists, as
available forms of discipline, both "restitution to persons financially injured" and
"reimbursement to the client protection fund," alongside reprimand, suspension, probation, and
disbarment. This does not mean restitution is ordered in every case, or that filing a
complaint is a substitute for a fee arbitration or court claim; it means the possibility exists
as one form of discipline among several, at the Board's discretion.

## 5. Client protection fund

The Client Protection Fund of the State Bar Association of North Dakota is administered by
SBAND, not the courts, under its own Rules for Client Protection Fund, adopted January 26, 2008
and amended November 22, 2014. This pack reads the current live copy at
<https://www.sband.org/resource/resmgr/docs/for_public/CPFRules.pdf>, confirmed identical in
substance to the 2008/2014 text preserved on the Wayback Machine; see the flagged table in
[verification_nd.md](verification_nd.md).

What it covers: losses caused by a lawyer's dishonest conduct arising out of a client-lawyer or
fiduciary relationship. "Dishonest conduct" is defined as theft, embezzlement, or wrongful
taking or conversion of money or property, and expressly includes failing to refund unearned
fees as required by Rules 1.5, 1.15, and 1.16, and borrowing from a client with no intention or
reasonable ability to repay.

What it excludes: the rules state this is not intended to cover bona fide fee disputes. Losses
to a lawyer's own spouse, children, parents, grandparents, siblings, partners, associates, or
employees are excluded, as are losses already covered by a bond, surety, or insurance policy to
the extent of that coverage.

Filing deadline: Rule 9.B says a claim must be filed no later than two years after the claimant
knew or should have known of the dishonest conduct. This pack uses two years. The rules' own
Comment [5], directly beneath that same rule, instead describes "a five-year limitation on the
filing of claims" running from the same trigger. This is an internal conflict in the source
document itself, not a drafting choice made here. Two years is used because it is the operative
rule text and is independently corroborated by SBAND's own Client Protection Program claim
form, which asks whether the loss occurred "in the last two years." See the flagged table in
[verification_nd.md](verification_nd.md), and confirm the current deadline with SBAND before
you rely on it.

Caps: under Rule 10.A, the amount recoverable by any one claimant in a twelve-month period is
capped at $25,000 or 10% of the total fund, whichever is smaller, unless the Fund has procured
insurance covering a higher amount. The total recoverable by all claimants for losses caused by
any one lawyer is capped at $75,000 in a twelve-month period, or 25% of the total fund,
whichever is smaller, absent applicable insurance coverage. These figures are set in the rule
text itself, not left to case-by-case Board discretion.

No requirement that a disciplinary complaint be on file first was found in the eligibility
rule. SBAND's own claim form does ask whether you have filed a complaint with Disciplinary
Counsel, but does not condition eligibility on a "yes" answer in the text reviewed. Send a
completed claim form to Executive Director, SBAND, P.O. Box 2136, Bismarck, ND 58502-2136.

## 6. Small claims or equivalent court

North Dakota's small claims equivalent is Small Claims Court, a division of district court
under N.D.C.C. ch. 27-08.1.

The jurisdictional limit is $15,000, for either a claim to recover money or a claim to cancel
an agreement involving material fraud, deception, misrepresentation, or false promise, where
the agreement amount is $15,000 or less. A claim must also be brought within six years of the
debt or the last payment on it.

Lawyers are allowed to appear in Small Claims Court. N.D.R.Ct. 10.2(a), effective 3/1/2025,
lets a party be represented by a lawyer admitted to practice in North Dakota. A legal entity
such as a corporation or LLC may instead be represented by an officer, owner, director,
trustee, or employee authorized to act for it, without a lawyer.

Filing fee: $202 to file a claim affidavit, per the official North Dakota court fee schedule at
<https://www.ndcourts.gov/Media/Default/district-courts/Court-Fees.pdf>. Filing a counterclaim
or answer in a small claims case carries no fee. If the case is dismissed without prejudice
because it cannot fairly be decided in Small Claims Court, the filing fee is refunded to the
plaintiff.

Appeal and removal, the most important trap in this section: Small Claims Court decisions
cannot be appealed by either party in the ordinary sense. Under N.D.C.C. section 27-08.1-04, electing
to use Small Claims Court is irrevocable for the plaintiff, and the plaintiff waives the right
to appeal to any other court from its decision. The defendant likewise waives the right to
appeal once served with the order for appearance, unless the defendant instead elects to remove
the case to district court before the small claims hearing. Under N.D.C.C. section 27-08.1-02,
the defendant has only twenty days from service of the claim affidavit to request a hearing or
elect removal; not responding in that window lets the plaintiff take a default judgment.
Removal costs a separate $160 fee, also on the official fee schedule. If the defendant removes the case and the plaintiff still
prevails, the district court must award the plaintiff reasonable attorney's fees. If the
defendant then appeals the district court's judgment to the North Dakota Supreme Court and
loses again, the Supreme Court must award the prevailing appellee reasonable attorney's fees as
well. There is no trial de novo option and no record-review appeal path from a Small Claims
Court judgment itself; removal before judgment is the only escape from the finality of that
forum.

No rule specific to suing your own lawyer in Small Claims Court was found; the $15,000 limit
and the process above apply the same as to any other claim.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A fee agreement dispute, such as a lawyer's failure to refund an unearned fee, is generally a
contract claim. North Dakota's limitation period for an action on a contract, obligation, or
liability, express or implied, is six years, at N.D.C.C. section 28-01-16(1), read from the official
North Dakota Century Code PDF at
<https://www.ndlegis.gov/cencode/t28c01.pdf>. This section does not draw a distinction between
a written and an oral contract; both fall under the same six-year period, subject to
cross-referenced provisions in N.D.C.C. sections 28-01-15 and 41-02-104 that were not independently
read for this pack.

Legal malpractice, a claim that the lawyer's own negligence caused you harm, needs its own
counsel rather than self-help. It is not covered further in this kit beyond the deadline,
included so you know a clock exists and can get counsel in time. North Dakota's limitation
period for "an action for the recovery of damages resulting from malpractice" is two years, at
N.D.C.C. section 28-01-18(3), same source as above. That same subsection contains a discovery-rule
and six-year-repose provision, but its text limits that extension by name to "a physician or
licensed hospital." Nothing in the statute text extends that discovery rule or repose period to
a lawyer. This pack does not assert a discovery rule for legal malpractice against a lawyer in
North Dakota; whether North Dakota courts have applied one by case law was not researched and
is not asserted here. See the gap list in [verification_nd.md](verification_nd.md).

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

The Rules of Professional Conduct are a disciplinary code. The Scope section, Comment [4],
states directly that violation of a rule "should not itself give rise to a cause of action
against a lawyer nor should it create a presumption in such a case that a legal duty has been
breached," while also noting that because the rules establish standards of conduct, a
violation "may be evidence of breach of the applicable standard of conduct." North Dakota does
not give a client a private cause of action for a rules violation by itself.

Your actual money route runs through the other forums in this pack instead: a contract claim in
Small Claims Court or district court (Section 6), SBAND's statewide fee arbitration program
(Section 3), or the Client Protection Fund where the conduct was dishonest rather than merely a
bad bill (Section 5). Getting the lawyer disciplined (Section 4) is a separate goal from
getting money back, and it runs through a separate forum, though North Dakota's discipline
rules do allow restitution as one possible sanction.
