# Oklahoma: handling a dispute with your own lawyer

This pack is for someone in Oklahoma who has a problem with their own lawyer over fees,
billing, communication, or getting their file back. It is not for someone who wants to sue a
lawyer for malpractice. Retrieved 2026-08-20.

What is different about Oklahoma:

- Oklahoma publishes its rules of professional conduct as an appendix to a title of its
  statutes, not as a standalone rules volume. The correct citation is Title 5, Chapter 1,
  Appendix 3-A of the Oklahoma Statutes, not a free-standing "Supreme Court Rule."
- Oklahoma recognizes a lawyer's right to hold a client's file to secure payment of a
  disputed fee, both as a matter of old case law and under a specific statute. That is the
  opposite of states that flatly bar this. If your lawyer is holding your file over unpaid
  fees, this pack explains what that lien can and cannot do.
- There is no statewide, bar-run fee arbitration program in Oklahoma. What exists is a
  patchwork of local county bar programs, of which this pack could confirm one in detail.
- Oklahoma caps contingent fees by statute at 50 percent of net recovery, a hard number the
  ABA model rules do not have.
- The Oklahoma Bar Association's Client Security Fund pays out of a single annual pool with
  no fixed per-claim dollar cap, unlike states that publish a flat maximum per claimant.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct: numbering and adoption

Oklahoma's Rules of Professional Conduct are adopted by the Oklahoma Supreme Court and
published as Appendix 3-A to Chapter 1 of Title 5 of the Oklahoma Statutes. The current
version was adopted by Oklahoma Supreme Court order 2007 OK 22, effective January 1, 2008.
That order is the instrument to cite if you need to point to the adoption itself, not just
the rule text.

Cite a rule in a letter the way the rest of the country does for this text: "Rule 1.5,"
"Rule 1.4," and so on. Oklahoma did not renumber the rules relative to the ABA model. If you
go looking for the duty to communicate, the fee rule, the safekeeping-property rule, or the
duty on termination, they sit at the same numbers you would expect: Rule 1.4, Rule 1.5, Rule
1.15, and Rule 1.16.

The official publication point is the Oklahoma State Courts Network, the state's own
judiciary-run legal research site, not a commercial database:
<https://www.oscn.net/applications/oscn/index.asp?ftdb=STOKST5&level=1>. That index page
lists Title 5, Chapter 1, and the rules live under it as Appendix 3-A. A reader unfamiliar
with Oklahoma's citation practice will not find "Chapter 1" intuitive for a set of ethics
rules; the placement inside a statute title, as an appendix, is the state's actual structure
and is worth naming precisely so you do not sound unprepared when you cite it.

The Scope section of the rules, numbered paragraphs 19 through 22, states plainly that a
rule violation is not by itself a basis for civil liability. Section 8 below covers this in
more detail.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

Oklahoma's Rule 1.5(a) lists the same eight reasonableness factors as the ABA model rule,
in the same order, with no factor added or removed. Rule 1.5(b), on communicating the scope
of representation and the basis of the fee, also tracks the model closely: it says the
communication should preferably be in writing, without imposing a fixed dollar threshold
that forces a writing above some amount. If you are used to a state that requires a writing
above $1,000 or some similar figure, Oklahoma does not have that trigger. That is a real
finding, not an oversight: it was checked directly against the current rule text on OSCN,
and no dollar-threshold writing requirement exists in Rule 1.5(b) itself.

Two things Oklahoma has that the model rules do not:

1. A statutory 50 percent cap on contingent fees. Title 5, section 7 of the Oklahoma
   Statutes caps a contingent fee at 50 percent of the net amount of the judgment or
   compromise recovered, in the statute's own words. The statute does not itself define
   what counts toward that net figure. It also voids certain personal-injury and
   wrongful-death fee contracts secured through the intervention of a layman, association,
   or corporation for compensation, or where a layman, association, or corporation has a
   direct or indirect interest in the recovery. This is a hard percentage ceiling the ABA
   model does not have.
   <https://www.oscn.net/applications/oscn/DeliverDocument.asp?CiteID=64834>
2. A recognized lien on the client's file to secure payment of a disputed fee. Oklahoma has
   long recognized a general, possessory or retaining lien that lets a lawyer hold property
   or money of the client that is in the lawyer's hands, to secure payment of a proper fee.
   This traces to old Oklahoma Supreme Court decisions (Roxana Petroleum Co. v. Rice, 1925;
   In Re Choate, 1935; In Re Hays, 1941), discussed in the Oklahoma Bar Association's own
   published ethics opinion on the subject, Ethics Opinion No. 295. Separately, Title 5,
   section 6 gives a lawyer a statutory charging lien on a client's cause of action or
   judgment. That lien attaches from the filing of the suit or of a counterclaim, takes
   effect once the lawyer serves notice on the opposing side, and notice is not required if
   the lawyer instead files the pleading in court and endorses on it the words "Lien
   claimed." Together these mean a fee dispute can lawfully delay the return of your file
   in Oklahoma, in a way it would not in a state that bars the practice outright.
   <https://www.oscn.net/applications/oscn/DeliverDocument.asp?CiteID=64833>

How old and how settled this is matters, because it tells you something unusual and it works
against you as the reader. Ethics Opinion 295 was adopted March 16, 1979, nearly thirty years
before Oklahoma adopted its current Rules of Professional Conduct by Oklahoma Supreme Court
order 2007 OK 22, effective January 1, 2008. The opinion's own reasoning cites DR 5-103(A)(1)
and Ethical Consideration 5-7, provisions of the old Code of Professional Responsibility that
the 2008 rules replaced, not the current rules. The Oklahoma Bar Association still publishes
the opinion, with no notice that it has been withdrawn or superseded, and this pack found no
later ethics opinion addressing the same question. The current rules do not appear to cut
against a retaining lien: Rule 1.16(d) says a lawyer "may retain papers relating to the
client to the extent permitted by other law," and its Comment 9 repeats that a lawyer "may
retain papers as security for a fee only to the extent permitted by law." Rule 1.15(d)
similarly excuses a lawyer from promptly delivering property when delivery is not required
because it is "otherwise permitted by law." Read together, the current rules point back to
the same outside law, the old case law and the section 6 statutory lien, that Opinion 295
relied on, rather than displacing it. But no current rule or later opinion independently
confirms the point, so treat this as a long-standing but dated reading rather than settled
current doctrine, and get advice from a lawyer before assuming it controls your situation.
<https://www.okbar.org/ethics/ethics-opinion-no-295/>
<https://www.oscn.net/applications/oscn/DeliverDocument.asp?CiteID=448890>
<https://www.oscn.net/applications/oscn/DeliverDocument.asp?CiteID=493000>

That same ethics opinion is worth reading closely if your dispute is specifically about
getting your file back. It draws a real line: if the fee is not in dispute, the lawyer must
return everything you gave them and anything in the file you already paid for, and can only
charge you for copies if that is the lawyer's normal practice for current clients too. If
the fee is in dispute, the lawyer's duties around holding the file are treated as a matter
of ordinary commercial law around the lien, not a separate ethics rule, and the opinion says
the lawyer should be bound by the same return-and-cooperate conduct once the fee dispute is
actually resolved.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

This is the big one, and in Oklahoma the honest answer is that there is no statewide
program, so far as this pack could find. The Oklahoma Bar Association's own consumer-facing
lawyer complaint page and its published "Lawyers and Legal Fees" consumer brochure both
describe what the Office of the General Counsel does and does not do, and neither one
mentions a bar-run mediation or arbitration service for fee amount disputes. Both instead
tell a client with a billing question to raise it directly with the lawyer.
<https://www.okbar.org/gc/complaint/>

What exists instead is a set of local, county-level bar association programs. This pack
could directly confirm one in real detail: the Tulsa County Bar Association's Fee
Arbitration Committee. It is free to use, and its decisions bind the parties because the
parties agree to submit to it, not because either side can be forced into the process. A
panel drawn from a quorum of at least 12 committee members, a mix of lawyers and
non-lawyers, decides by majority vote. A binding award can be enforced by a later court
action if the losing side does not pay voluntarily. The committee does not resolve client
grievances about a lawyer's conduct, only fee amount disputes.
<https://www.tulsabar.com/?pg=Committee>

The Oklahoma County Bar Association has a similarly named committee, the Fee Grievance and
Ethics Committee, that investigates complaints against lawyers and can be deputized through
the state bar to look into a complaint. Its own description, read directly, describes an
investigate-and-recommend function, not a confirmed arbitration procedure with a binding
award. This pack does not present the Oklahoma County program as an arbitration program on
the strength of what could be confirmed; if you are in that county, contact the committee
directly and ask what it can actually do for a fee dispute rather than relying on this pack
for that county's procedure. Other counties may have their own bar committees that this pack
did not attempt to survey, so treat any list here as incomplete rather than complete.
<https://www.okcbar.org/page/CommitteesDivisionsSections>

A separate route worth checking is your own fee agreement. Some Oklahoma retainer
agreements include a clause requiring arbitration of any fee dispute, as a private contract
matter between you and the lawyer rather than a bar program. Read your fee agreement for
such a clause before assuming there is no arbitration route available to you at all.

Because there is no statewide bar program, a reader with a straightforward fee dispute in
Oklahoma is more likely to end up negotiating directly, asking a local county bar committee
if one exists and covers the amount involved, or filing in small claims or district court,
than in states that run a single program covering everyone.

## 4. Bar complaint process

The Oklahoma Bar Association's Office of the General Counsel investigates complaints that a
lawyer violated the Rules of Professional Conduct, operating under the oversight of the
Professional Responsibility Commission. A complaint is filed in writing and must be signed.
<https://www.okbar.org/gc/complaint/>

The General Counsel's own public description lists the kinds of complaints it does
investigate, such as a lawyer failing to account for money held on a client's behalf, not
responding to a client, being untruthful, or not following through on what was promised, and
separately tells a client who thinks a bill is too high to call the lawyer and discuss it
rather than treating that alone as grounds for a complaint. This pack reads that as the
office not functioning as a fee mediator or arbitrator, though the office's own page does not
use those words. It points a client with a fee disagreement toward discussing it directly
with the lawyer or pursuing the routes covered in Section 3 and Section 6 of this pack. Where
a fee is alleged to be extortionate or obtained by fraud, that can become a genuine ethics
complaint rather than a simple billing dispute, so if there is more going on than a
disagreement over the bill, say so plainly in your complaint.

This pack could not find a published deadline for filing a grievance against a lawyer,
after a targeted search of the Rules Governing Disciplinary Proceedings. That is recorded in
the verification file as an absence finding, not a confirmed "no deadline," because a
targeted search is not the same as reading every provision. If a filing deadline matters to
your situation, confirm directly with the Office of the General Counsel before relying on
there being none.

Discipline against a lawyer is not a refund. The disciplinary process can result in a
private reprimand, a public censure, suspension, or disbarment, none of which puts money
back in your pocket directly. Oklahoma's rules do let a review authority consider whether a
lawyer later made restitution to a client when deciding on reinstatement after suspension or
disbarment, but that is a factor in the lawyer's own path back to practice, not a mechanism
that orders your money returned to you. If getting money back is the goal, Section 3 and
Section 6 are the routes for that, not a bar complaint by itself.

## 5. Client protection fund

The Oklahoma Bar Association's Clients' Security Fund is funded by bar dues, not by client
fees or tax dollars, and is administered by a Client Security Fund Committee under rules
approved by the Oklahoma Supreme Court. <https://www.okbar.org/gc/clients-security-fund/>

It covers losses caused by a lawyer's dishonest conduct, which explicitly includes a
lawyer's refusal to refund an unearned advance fee where the lawyer did little or no work,
treated as a form of conversion of the client's money. It excludes ordinary malpractice or
negligence, a fee that is simply disputed as excessive rather than stolen, losses to the
lawyer's own family, partners, or employees, and any loss already covered by a bond or
insurance or otherwise reimbursed.

There is no fixed per-claim dollar cap published in the fund's rules. Instead, the fund
draws on a single annual appropriation of $100,000, and if approved claims in a given year
add up to more than that, the fund prorates the payouts among all approved claimants for
that year rather than paying each one in full. Awards are discretionary, described in the
fund's own rules as a matter of grace rather than a right a claimant can enforce like a
judgment.

Before the fund will pay, the lawyer generally must have died, been adjudged mentally
incompetent, completed a disciplinary proceeding, or resigned or let their license lapse
after a disciplinary proceeding against them had already started. A claim ordinarily has to
be filed within 5 years of the loss. Contact the Office of the General Counsel to start a
claim: P.O. Box 53036, Oklahoma City, OK 73152, 405-416-7000 or 800-522-8065 toll free.

## 6. Small claims court

Oklahoma's small claims court is created by the Small Claims Procedure Act and runs inside
the state's district courts. The statewide jurisdictional limit is $10,000, and this figure
is set by statute rather than varying by county.
<https://www.oscn.net/applications/oscn/DeliverDocument.asp?CiteID=438809>

Nothing in the Small Claims Procedure Act bars either side from hiring a lawyer, and nothing
requires it; the statute says the hearing is meant to be informal, with the sole object of
dispensing speedy justice, which is why small claims court is generally used by people
representing themselves. Libel and slander claims cannot be brought in small claims court,
but a garden-variety fee dispute is a contract or money claim and fits.

If either side wants the case handled as a regular civil case instead, or if the amount in
controversy exceeds $10,000, the case can be transferred out of the small claims track into
the ordinary civil docket.
<https://www.oscn.net/applications/oscn/DeliverDocument.asp?CiteID=94808>

Appeal from a small claims judgment goes to the Oklahoma Supreme
Court, in the same manner as an appeal in any other civil case; Oklahoma's statute does not
describe a separate small-claims-specific trial-from-scratch process on appeal, unlike some
states' automatic trial de novo. <https://www.oscn.net/applications/oscn/DeliverDocument.asp?CiteID=94812>

The filing fee is $45 for claims of $5,000 or less; above that, the regular Title 28 civil
filing fee schedule applies instead of the flat small claims fee.
<https://www.oscn.net/applications/oscn/DeliverDocument.asp?CiteID=439760>

One rule worth knowing if you plan to hire your own lawyer to bring a small claims case: in
an uncontested small claims judgment, the attorney fee the court will approve for the
plaintiff's own lawyer is capped, ordinarily at 10 percent of the judgment, or up to 25
percent if the request is documented and the court approves the higher amount. This pack did
not find any small claims rule written specifically for suits against a lawyer as opposed to
any other defendant; the ordinary small claims rules described above are what applies.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A written contract claim in Oklahoma must be brought within 5 years. An oral or implied
contract claim must be brought within 3 years. A fee agreement dispute is ordinarily a
contract claim, so which of these two periods applies can turn on whether your fee
agreement was in writing. Title 12, section 95(A)(1) and (A)(2).
<https://www.oscn.net/applications/oscn/DeliverDocument.asp?CiteID=439150>

A claim for an injury to rights that does not arise on contract, the general tort bucket
that legal malpractice claims are usually understood to fall into, must be brought within 2
years under section 95(A)(3) of the same statute. This pack presents that 2-year period as
the one most likely to govern a legal malpractice claim, based on the general classification
of the statute's own language, but it could not locate a specific published Oklahoma Supreme
Court decision squarely classifying legal malpractice claims for limitations purposes, and
it did not find a case establishing a discovery rule specific to legal malpractice. Oklahoma
does have a statutory discovery rule for medical malpractice, but that statute applies to
health care providers, not to attorneys, so it does not transfer over. Both of these gaps
are recorded in the verification file rather than guessed at. This pack also did not find a
separate statute of repose specific to legal malpractice claims in Oklahoma; if a repose
period exists, it was not located.

This kit does not cover how to bring a malpractice claim. These deadlines are here so you
know a clock is running and can get your own counsel in time, not so you can run the claim
yourself.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

No. The Scope section of Oklahoma's Rules of Professional Conduct, in the paragraph
numbered 20, states that violation of a rule should not by itself give rise to a cause of
action, and should not create a presumption that a legal duty has been breached. It says the
rules are designed to structure the disciplinary process, not to serve as a basis for civil
liability, and that nothing in the rules should be read to expand a lawyer's substantive
legal duties.

The version of the Scope section this pack could confirm, read in full through its closing
paragraphs, did not include an additional sentence some other states' versions carry, about
a rule violation being usable as evidence of the standard of care in a malpractice case.
That is recorded as a genuine absence finding in the verification file: the sentence was not
found in the fetched text, which is different from Oklahoma having affirmatively rejected
the idea. If whether a rule violation can be used as evidence in a malpractice case matters
to you, that is a malpractice-strategy question outside what this kit covers, and it needs
its own legal advice.

Your actual money routes in Oklahoma are the ones covered above: a contract claim over the
fee itself, a local county bar fee arbitration program where one exists and covers your
situation, or the Client Security Fund if the lawyer's conduct was dishonest rather than
merely a billing disagreement.

## Before you rely on this

Confirm the current text of any rule yourself before you rely on it. Check the rule text on
the Oklahoma State Courts Network at
<https://www.oscn.net/applications/oscn/index.asp?ftdb=STOKST5&level=1>, check the Oklahoma
Bar Association's own pages for the complaint process and the Client Security Fund at
<https://www.okbar.org/gc/>, and if a local fee arbitration program matters to your case,
contact that county bar association directly to confirm it is still running on the terms
described here. See references/verification_ok.md for exactly what was checked, what was
only loosely sourced, and what could not be confirmed at all.
