# Alabama: handling a dispute with your own lawyer

This section is for a person in Alabama who has a problem with their own lawyer over
fees, billing, communication, getting their file back, or an advance payment that was
never earned or never refunded. It does not cover legal malpractice claims or how to
run one yourself. Retrieved August 20, 2026.

What is different about Alabama:

- Alabama's fee rule bars a "clearly excessive fee," not an "unreasonable fee." That is
  a higher bar for a client to clear than the model rule's language. Read section 2.
- Alabama has no statewide fee arbitration program, and the state bar's own complaint
  process says in writing that it does not handle fee disputes. Two local county bar
  associations run their own free, binding fee arbitration committees, but they only
  reach clients in those counties. Read section 3 before you assume a state process exists.
- Alabama has a specific statute, the Legal Services Liability Act, that controls claims
  against a lawyer for a breach of the standard of care, separately from the ordinary
  contract and tort deadlines. Read section 7 carefully; the deadline that applies to your
  situation depends on how your claim is framed.
- Alabama's Client Security Fund has a firm three-year filing deadline from when you knew
  or should have known of the loss, extended to six years only while a bar grievance is
  pending. Read section 5.
- Alabama's disciplinary rules let a hearing officer impose restitution as a condition of
  probation. It is discretionary and it is not the same thing as suing for your money.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct: numbering and adoption

Alabama's rules of professional conduct for lawyers are the Alabama Rules of Professional
Conduct, adopted and amended by the Alabama Supreme Court by its own orders. There is no
separate chapter-and-colon numbering scheme like some states use; you cite the rules
directly, for example "Rule 1.5, Ala. R. Prof. Cond." The rules are organized the same way
the ABA Model Rules are, so a rule you know by number in another state, such as 1.4 or
1.5, sits at the same number in Alabama.

The Alabama Supreme Court amends individual rules by order from time to time. The most
recent confirmed example is an order dated May 13, 2025, amending Rules 7.1, 7.2, 7.3, and
8.5 and a related disciplinary rule, effective January 1, 2026. See
<https://www.alabar.org/assets/2025/05/Order-on-Alabama-Rules-of-Professional-Conduct-and-Rules-of-Disciplinary-Procedure.pdf>.
That order did not touch Rule 1.5, the fee rule. This pack could not confirm, to a primary
source, the exact date the current Rules of Professional Conduct were first adopted; do not
rely on any specific 1991 date you may see elsewhere without checking it yourself.

The official publication point for the current rule text is the Alabama Judicial System's
own site, rule by rule, in PDF. The index is at
<https://judicial.alabama.gov/library/RulesBarConduct>. Rule 1.5, the fee rule, is at
<https://judicial.alabama.gov/docs/library/rules/cond1_5.pdf>.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

Alabama's fee rule, Rule 1.5(a), departs from the model rule in two ways that matter.

First, the standard itself is different. The model rule bars an "unreasonable fee."
Alabama's Rule 1.5(a) bars a "clearly excessive fee." Alabama's own comment to the rule
explains this was a deliberate choice: Alabama never adopted the older Model Code's
"clearly excessive fee" language as a disciplinary standard through a comparable path, and
the current rule keeps that wording rather than switching to "unreasonable." A word choice
like this does real work. "Clearly excessive" reads as a higher bar to clear than
"unreasonable," which matters if you are trying to convince a bar committee or a court that
a bill crossed the line.

Second, Alabama's list of reasonableness factors has nine items, not the model's eight. The
first eight track the model rule's factors closely: time and labor and difficulty of the
work, whether taking the case foreclosed other work, the customary local fee, the amount at
stake and the result, time limitations, the length of the lawyer-client relationship, the
lawyer's experience and reputation, and whether the fee is fixed or contingent. Alabama
adds a ninth factor at Rule 1.5(a)(9): whether there is a written fee agreement signed by
the client. Alabama's own comment confirms this factor is new relative to the prior
disciplinary rules and was not part of the earlier Alabama Code of Professional
Responsibility.

Alabama does not otherwise widen or narrow the contingent-fee prohibitions in Rule 1.5(d):
domestic relations fees contingent on securing a divorce or on the amount of alimony,
support, or property settlement, and contingent fees for representing a criminal defendant,
are both barred, matching the model rule. This pack found no dollar cap or sliding scale on
contingent fees generally, though other statutes may cap fees in specific matter types,
such as workers' compensation, which is outside the scope of this pack. Source: Rule 1.5,
Ala. R. Prof. Cond., at
<https://judicial.alabama.gov/docs/library/rules/cond1_5.pdf>.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

Alabama does not run a statewide fee arbitration or mediation program. This pack found no
Alabama Supreme Court rule, Alabama State Bar program, or statute establishing one.

The Alabama State Bar's own official complaint brochure states plainly that fee disputes
are not handled through the bar's grievance and complaint system. The brochure lists
resolving disputed attorney fees, and resolving debts a lawyer owes, among the things the
complaint process cannot do, and says directly that fee disputes generally do not involve
questions of ethics or professional conduct. See
<https://www.alabar.org/assets/2025/04/Complaint-form_brochure-2012.pdf>. Rule 1.5's
comment says only that if a fee arbitration procedure has been established, a lawyer should
conscientiously consider submitting to it. That sentence is conditional. It does not create
a procedure; it only asks a lawyer to use one if it exists.

At least two county bar associations run their own free fee arbitration or fee dispute
committees, confirmed directly from their own pages:

- The Birmingham Bar Association's Fee Arbitration Committee is free to use, and its
  decision is final and binding, not subject to reconsideration, rehearing, or appeal to
  any other person, organization, or court. A request is made by submitting a fee dispute
  form to the bar's office. See <https://birminghambar.org/?pg=Fee_arbitration>.
- The Mobile Bar Association's Fee Dispute Committee works the same way: free, and the
  decision is final and binding with no appeal. See
  <https://mobilebarassociation.com/page/FeeDisputewithaLawyer>.

Neither page states a dollar floor or ceiling, a filing deadline, or whether the lawyer can
be forced to participate against their will. Read plainly, both appear to require the
lawyer's agreement to submit, the same way most local bar fee committees work elsewhere.
This pack could not confirm whether other Alabama county bar associations run a similar
committee; this list is almost certainly incomplete. If your lawyer belongs to a different
county bar, check that bar association's own site before assuming no local option exists.

If your lawyer is not in Birmingham or Mobile, or does not agree to a local committee, your
practical path is a written demand to the lawyer followed by small claims or circuit court,
covered in section 6, or the Client Security Fund in section 5 if the loss involved
dishonest conduct rather than a plain billing disagreement.

## 4. Bar complaint process

The Alabama State Bar's Office of General Counsel, through its Disciplinary Division,
receives and investigates complaints against lawyers. See
<https://www.alabar.org/office-of-general-counsel/disciplinary-division/>. The bar's Ethics
Division is a separate track that answers a lawyer's own ethics questions and is not the
place to file a complaint against a lawyer.

This pack could not confirm a live online complaint-submission portal separate from the
Office of General Counsel's own page and mailing address. The disciplinary division's page
above is the correct starting point; it explains the intake process and where to send a
written complaint. This pack found no separate informal, consumer-assistance track distinct
from the formal complaint process at the state level. If you have a pure billing
disagreement rather than a claim of dishonesty, incompetence, or misconduct, filing it as a
disciplinary complaint is unlikely to produce a refund, because the bar's own brochure
excludes fee disputes and debt disputes from what the process handles. See
<https://www.alabar.org/assets/2025/04/Complaint-form_brochure-2012.pdf>.

This pack found no stated deadline for filing a disciplinary complaint against an Alabama
lawyer, and no source stating affirmatively that no deadline exists. Treat this as an open
question and file promptly rather than waiting.

Discipline is not a refund. The Office of General Counsel investigates and can pursue
sanctions ranging from private reprimand to disbarment, through the disciplinary process set
out in the Alabama Rules of Disciplinary Procedure. Those rules do include one limited
restitution mechanism: Rule 21(b) lists "refunding and/or making restitution" among the
conditions a hearing officer or panel may impose as a condition of probation when probation
is part of the outcome. This is discretionary, tied to a probation sanction rather than
guaranteed in every case, and confirmed directly from the rule text at
<https://judicial.alabama.gov/docs/library/rules/disproc21.pdf>. It is not a general
restitution order available on request, and it is not a substitute for pursuing your money
through a fee dispute, small claims, or the Client Security Fund.

## 5. Client protection fund

Alabama's Client Security Fund is administered by a committee under the Alabama State Bar,
governed by the Rules Governing the Alabama State Bar Client Security Fund. The Fund's own
rules are published at
<https://www.alabar.org/for-the-public/client-security-fund/>.

The Fund reimburses losses caused by the dishonest conduct of an Alabama lawyer acting
either as the client's own lawyer or in a fiduciary capacity for the client, such as theft
or misappropriation of client funds. It does not cover an ordinary fee dispute, a
disagreement about the quality of work, or a claim that sounds in legal malpractice or
negligence rather than dishonesty. The Fund does not reimburse a person, firm, or
association, such as a bonding company, that has already been compensated for assuming that
risk of loss.

The per-claimant cap is $75,000 arising from an instance or course of dishonest conduct.
The aggregate cap that all applicants may recover from a single instance or course of
dishonest conduct is $200,000. A separate category of claims tied to court-appointed
fiduciary conduct under the Rules of Disciplinary Procedure carries its own $25,000
per-claim figure, subject to the committee's discretion to allow more. Awards are
discretionary; the Fund is described in its own rules as a matter of grace, not a matter of
right, and payments can be made in full or in part.

The filing deadline is three years after the applicant knew or should have known of the
reimbursable loss. If a grievance against the lawyer is filed with the bar's disciplinary
system and is still pending, that filing extends the window, but an application can never be
filed later than six years after the applicant knew or should have known of the loss, even
if a grievance is still pending. There is no requirement in the rules, as read, that a
disciplinary complaint be filed first, though filing one can extend your Fund deadline as
described above.

Source for this entire section: Rules Governing the Alabama State Bar Client Security Fund,
published at <https://www.alabar.org/for-the-public/client-security-fund/>.

## 6. Small claims or equivalent court

Alabama's small claims forum is the Small Claims Division of the district court in each
county. The jurisdictional limit is $6,000, exclusive of interest and costs, set by Ala.
Code Section 12-12-31. This pack confirmed this figure from a Wayback Machine snapshot,
dated October 4, 2023, of the official Alabama Legislature code database page for this
section, because the legislature's current live code site returns an identical
JavaScript shell for every section and could not be used directly. Snapshot:
<https://web.archive.org/web/20231004195628/http://alisondb.legislature.state.al.us/alison/codeofalabama/1975/12-12-31.htm>.

This pack found no evidence that the $6,000 limit varies by county; the statute sets one
statewide figure for the small claims docket. The Alabama Rules of Small Claims Procedure
allow a party to appear without a lawyer and direct the court to accommodate parties who are
not represented, but this pack found no rule in the Small Claims Procedure Rules that bars a
lawyer from appearing; a corporate party ordinarily needs a lawyer to appear in Alabama
courts generally, as in most states, though this pack did not independently confirm a
small-claims-specific exception or requirement on that point. This pack found no rule
specific to suing a lawyer in small claims court. Filing fees vary by county and were not
independently confirmed here; check with the clerk of the district court where you would
file.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

Alabama has a specific statute that controls a claim against a lawyer for breach of the
standard of care: the Alabama Legal Services Liability Act, Ala. Code Sections 6-5-570
through 6-5-581. This is the statute the introduction to this pack flagged; do not assume
the state's general tort or contract periods apply to a claim framed around how your lawyer
handled your matter.

The Act states there is only one form and cause of action against a legal service provider
in Alabama for such a claim, called a "legal service liability action," and it defines that
action broadly to embrace claims that would otherwise be framed in either contract or tort.
Confirmed from a Wayback snapshot, dated June 6, 2021, of the official Alabama Legislature
code database:
<https://web.archive.org/web/20210606030232/http://alisondb.legislature.state.al.us/alison/codeofalabama/1975/6-5-573.htm>
(Section 6-5-573) and the companion definitions section, Section 6-5-572, same snapshot
date, same source.

The limitation period for a legal service liability action under Section 6-5-574 is two
years from the act or omission giving rise to the claim, with a discovery rule: if the
claimee did not discover the injury within that two-year period, the action may still be
commenced within six months of discovery, or should-have-discovered, whichever is earlier,
but never more than four years after the act or omission. The only exception written into
that four-year cap covers acts before August 1, 1987. This pack looked for a fraud-concealment
exception to the cap and did not find one stated in the statute, so do not assume one exists.
Confirmed from a Wayback snapshot dated June 6, 2021 of the same official code database,
Section 6-5-574:
<https://web.archive.org/web/20210606030239/http://alisondb.legislature.state.al.us/alison/codeofalabama/1975/6-5-574.htm>.

Separately, Alabama's general statutes name a specific, longer period for a different kind
of claim against a lawyer: Ala. Code Section 6-2-34(8) sets a six-year period for actions
against attorneys "for failure to pay over money of their clients or for neglect or
omission of duty." Section 6-2-34(4) sets the same six-year period for actions on a written
promise. Confirmed from a Wayback snapshot dated February 26, 2018 of the official code
database, Section 6-2-34:
<https://web.archive.org/web/20180226115550/http://alisondb.legislature.state.al.us/alison/codeofalabama/1975/6-2-34.htm>.

These two statutes create real tension that this pack cannot resolve for you. The Legal
Services Liability Act says it is the only cause of action against a legal service provider
and defines itself broadly enough that a claim over an unrefunded advance fee or an unpaid
balance could arguably fall inside it, subject to the shorter two-year or four-year period,
even though Section 6-2-34(8) appears to describe exactly that situation, with a six-year
period. This pack did not find a case or a state publication resolving which statute
controls a fee-refund claim that is not framed as a breach of the standard of care. If your
claim is purely "give me my file and refund what you did not earn," and does not allege the
lawyer handled your matter incompetently, get the actual controlling deadline confirmed by a
lawyer before you rely on either number, because guessing wrong here can end your claim
before you file it.

Alabama also sets a general three-year period for actions on an open or unliquidated
account, at Ala. Code Section 6-2-37(1), which could bear on a simple billing dispute
depending on how the account was structured; this pack did not resolve whether that
provision or Section 6-2-34 governs a typical retainer dispute, and flags this as unresolved
rather than guessing.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

Alabama's Rules of Professional Conduct state, in the Preamble and Scope section, that
violation of a rule "should not give rise to a cause of action nor should it create any
presumption that a legal duty has been breached." Confirmed directly from the Preamble text
at <https://judicial.alabama.gov/docs/library/rules/condpre.pdf>.

This pack specifically checked whether Alabama's version carries the same qualifier the ABA
Model Rules' Scope section has, that a violation may still be used as evidence of a breach
of the standard of care in an otherwise recognized claim such as malpractice. This pack did
not find that qualifying language anywhere in the Alabama Preamble text. Alabama's stated
rule reads as a flatter statement than the model's: no cause of action, and no presumption
of a breached duty, without the model's separate evidentiary carve-out. This pack cannot
tell you from this text alone whether an Alabama court would still admit a rule violation as
evidence in a malpractice case decided under different law; that is a malpractice strategy
question and is outside the scope of this kit.

Your actual money routes in Alabama are the ones covered above: a written demand and, if
needed, small claims or circuit court under section 6, a local county bar fee arbitration
committee where one exists under section 3, and the Client Security Fund under section 5 if
the loss involved dishonest conduct. This pack found no separate Alabama fee-forfeiture
doctrine confirmed to a primary source; if you believe your lawyer's conduct was serious
enough to forfeit the whole fee rather than just reduce it, that is a question for a lawyer,
not something this pack can walk you through.

## Before you rely on this

Confirm the current text of any rule or statute cited here for yourself before you act on
it, especially section 7's competing limitation periods, because acting on the wrong one can
end your claim. Start with the Alabama Rules of Professional Conduct at
<https://judicial.alabama.gov/library/RulesBarConduct>, the Alabama State Bar's disciplinary
and Client Security Fund pages at <https://www.alabar.org/>, and the official Code of
Alabama. See [verification_al.md](verification_al.md) for exactly what was checked, how, and
where the search stopped.
