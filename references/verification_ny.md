## Gap list

1. Whether New York imposes any deadline on filing a disciplinary grievance complaint, or
   whether (as in some states) no deadline applies at all, was not confirmed either way. Search
   covered the Third Department's own complaint instructions and the Second Department's
   complaint page; neither states a deadline or its absence. No case law equivalent to
   Tennessee's laches holding was searched for, because the search stopped once the two
   department pages proved silent on the question rather than affirmative, and the pack does not
   state a deadline that was never confirmed.
2. The current statewide Part 137 filing fee, or a representative range of local filing fees,
   was not located as a number. The rule itself only requires that any fee charged be
   reasonably related to actual cost; no arbitral body's specific fee schedule was searched,
   because Part 137 is administered by many local arbitral bodies and no single confirmable
   statewide figure exists to report.
3. Small claims dollar limits, filing fees, and appeal rules for courts outside New York City,
   specifically City Courts and Town and Village Justice Courts under the Uniform City Court Act
   and Uniform Justice Court Act, were not confirmed. Search stopped after confirming the NYC
   Civil Court Act figures directly; the parallel Uniform Court Acts were not independently
   opened and read for this pack, so no figure for outside-NYC courts appears in Section 6.
4. No dollar figure for what an ordinary (non-malpractice) contingent fee is customarily
   charged in New York, for example the commonly quoted one-third figure, was confirmed as a
   rule. Judiciary Law Section 474 leaves compensation to agreement; no statute or rule fixing
   a percentage for ordinary contingent fees was located, and none is stated in the pack.
5. No New York statute or opinion stating in as many words that a Rules of Professional
   Conduct violation creates no private cause of action was located. The pack rests that point
   on Scope Comment 12, which the Appellate Division did not adopt as rule text, and Section 8
   of the pack says so on its face. Search stopped at the compiled rules themselves.
7. Whether a New York court has held that a Rules of Professional Conduct violation is
   admissible as evidence of a standard of care in a malpractice case, beyond what Scope
   Comment 12 itself says, was not independently researched with a named case. The kit does not
   cover malpractice strategy, so this was not pursued past the point Comment 12's own text
   supports.
8. Any statute-of-repose provision applicable to a New York legal malpractice claim, separate
   from the three-year CPLR 214(6) period and the continuous representation doctrine, was not
   found and is not assumed to exist. No further search was done once CPLR 214 itself, read
   directly, showed no repose language.
9. No dollar figure for what any particular remedy typically recovers, in any forum in this
   pack. There is no verified dataset behind such a number, and none was sought.
10. Whether New York's Fee Dispute Resolution Program administering bodies vary their intake
   process (for example, online filing versus mail-only) by judicial district was not
   researched beyond what the official Part 137 rule text itself describes procedurally; local
   administering-body websites were not surveyed one by one.
