# Indiana

This pack is for a person in Indiana who has a fee, billing, communication, or file-return
dispute with their own lawyer. It does not cover legal malpractice as a claim to run yourself.
Retrieved 2026-08-20.

What an Indiana reader most needs to know, that a reader in another state does not:

- Indiana has no court-run, statewide, mandatory fee arbitration program. Admission and
  Discipline Rule 23, Section 5(b) only makes it permissive for a bar association to attempt
  resolving a fee dispute; it does not create or require a program. The only program this
  pack could confirm, the Indiana State Bar Association's Attorney Fee Dispute Resolution
  (AFDR) process, is voluntary on both sides: a member attorney "can elect to participate, but
  are not obligated to," it only covers ISBA member attorneys, and its decisions are final with
  no appeal. See Section 3. This changes the realistic path more than anything else in this
  pack: your leverage comes from small claims court or the bar complaint process, not from a
  binding arbitration right.
- Rule 1.5(d) adds an Indiana-specific carve-out the model rule does not have: a contingent fee
  is still barred in domestic relations and criminal defense matters generally, but Indiana
  allows a contingent fee specifically for collecting a domestic-relations debt after judgment,
  as long as the lawyer advises the client in writing of the alternative ways to collect it.
- The Indiana State Bar Association also runs the only client protection fund this pack could
  confirm, the Clients' Financial Assistance Fund (CFAF). It is a discretionary, member-funded
  program, not a court fund, capped at $15,000 per claimant and $50,000 total per dishonest
  lawyer, and it generally requires a grievance to already be on file with the Disciplinary
  Commission. An August 2024 snapshot of the Fund's page stated it had paused accepting new
  applications; a live fetch of the same page on 2026-08-20 shows a full application process
  with no pause notice, but that is not a confirmed statement that the Fund is currently open.
  See Section 5 and the gap list.
- Indiana's small claims jurisdictional limit is $10,000 statewide in both the Circuit Court
  small claims docket (IC 33-28-3-4) and the Superior Court small claims docket (IC
  33-29-2-4), the same figure in both statutes. No county-level dollar variation could be
  confirmed for either docket, despite this being worth checking given Marion County's
  historically separate small claims court structure. See Section 6.
- Indiana treats a written fee agreement that only promises payment of money the same as an
  oral one for limitation purposes: both run six years (IC 34-11-2-9 for written contracts to
  pay money, IC 34-11-2-7 for contracts not in writing). A ten-year period exists only for
  written contracts that are not for the payment of money (IC 34-11-2-11), which a typical fee
  agreement is not.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct, numbering and adoption

The Indiana Supreme Court adopts Indiana's Rules of Professional Conduct directly by court
rule. Cite them as "Rule" followed by the number, for example "Rule 1.5," the same numbering
the ABA Model Rules use. Indiana does not renumber the rules a client is most likely to need:
Rule 1.4 (Communication), Rule 1.5 (Fees), Rule 1.15 (Safekeeping Property), and Rule 1.16
(Declining or Terminating Representation) sit at the numbers a reader would expect.

The official publication point is the Indiana courts' rules site,
<https://rules.incourts.gov/pdf/PDF%20-%20Professional%20Conduct/professional-conduct.pdf>. The
Rules of Professional Conduct were adopted effective January 1, 1987, and the version fetched
for this pack was updated effective October 1, 2024. Individual rules carry their own effective
dates within that document: Rule 1.4 and Rule 1.5 are each effective January 1, 2005, Rule 1.16
is effective January 1, 2005, and Rule 1.15 was most recently amended effective October 1, 2024.

Lawyer discipline itself, including the grievance and Disciplinary Commission process described
in Section 4, is governed by a separate court rule, the Indiana Rules for Admission to the Bar
and the Discipline of Attorneys, Rule 23, published at
<https://rules.incourts.gov/Content/admission-discipline/rule23/current.htm>, currently
effective January 1, 2021, with the specific procedural sections fetched for this pack effective
July 1, 2023.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

Indiana's Rule 1.5(a) lists the same eight reasonableness factors as the ABA Model Rule, in the
same order, with no additions and no material rewording. This was confirmed by reading the rule
text directly rather than assumed; "no deviation found" is itself the finding for the factor
list.

Where Indiana does deviate is elsewhere in Rule 1.5:

- Rule 1.5(d) bars a contingent fee in a domestic relations matter or a criminal defense matter,
  the same prohibition the model rule states, but Indiana adds a carve-out the model rule does
  not have: a contingent fee is allowed specifically for legal representation in collecting a
  domestic relations debt after judgment, provided the lawyer clearly advises the client in
  writing of the alternative ways to collect that debt.
- Rule 1.5(b) and 1.5(c), covering communicating the fee basis and the written requirements for
  a contingent fee agreement, track the model rule with no deviation found.
- Comment 9 to Rule 1.5, "Disputes over Fees," states that where a bar association has
  established a fee dispute resolution procedure, a lawyer must comply with it when it is
  mandatory and should conscientiously consider it when it is voluntary. This comment is the
  rule-level hook behind the fee arbitration picture in Section 3; Indiana's own procedure is
  voluntary, not mandatory, so this comment does not create a client-side right to compel
  arbitration.

No Indiana-specific writing threshold, contingent-fee percentage cap, or nonrefundable-fee rule
beyond what the model rule already states was found in Rule 1.5.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

Indiana does not run a statewide, court-administered, mandatory fee arbitration program. What
exists sits at two levels, and both are weaker than a reader might expect:

- Admission and Discipline Rule 23, Section 5(b) permits a bar association to attempt resolving
  a fee dispute between a lawyer and client, and states that if the association's own rules make
  that procedure mandatory for its members, a member lawyer must submit to it. The rule itself
  creates no statewide program and does not require any bar association to have one.
- The Indiana State Bar Association's Attorney Fee Dispute Resolution (AFDR) program is the only
  program this pack could confirm exists. It is run by the ISBA's Attorney Fee Dispute
  Resolution Committee, described at <https://www.inbar.org/page/AFDR>. That page is gone: it
  returned HTTP 404 on repeated attempts on 2026-08-20 and 2026-08-21, and the Wayback Machine's
  own crawl history shows the same page redirecting to the ISBA's site-wide 404 page as early as
  August 2025, so this is a confirmed dead page, not a bot-blocked one. A candidate replacement
  document found by search, a PDF of the program's policies and procedures hosted on the ISBA's
  member-management platform, also could not be retrieved (HTTP 403, access denied). The content
  below is confirmed instead from a Wayback Machine snapshot of the original page dated
  November 11, 2020,
  <https://web.archive.org/web/20201111190606/https://www.inbar.org/page/AFDR>. Participation is
  voluntary on both sides: the ISBA's own page states that when a client initiates the process,
  "member attorneys can elect to participate, but are not obligated to participate." It only
  covers ISBA members and fee-sharing disputes between attorneys; a lawyer who is not an ISBA
  member cannot be brought into it at all. Decisions are made by the Committee and are final,
  with no appeal.

No dollar ceiling or floor, filing fee, filing deadline, or pre-suit notice requirement for the
AFDR program could be confirmed from any source this pack could reach; see the gap list. The
Rules of Procedure document referenced on the archived AFDR page could not be separately located
and fetched.

Because the only confirmed program is voluntary for the lawyer, do not treat a fee dispute with
a non-ISBA-member lawyer, or with an ISBA member who declines to participate, as having any
arbitration forum available at all. Small claims court (Section 6) is the realistic path.

## 4. Bar complaint process

The Indiana Supreme Court Disciplinary Commission handles attorney discipline statewide. A
grievance is filed using the Commission's Request for Investigation form, described at
<https://www.in.gov/courts/ojar/di-process/complaint/>, and mailed to Indiana Supreme Court
Disciplinary Commission, 251 N. Illinois Street, Suite 500, Indianapolis, IN 46204, or filed
electronically as described on that page. The Commission's phone number published there is
(317) 232-1807.

Indiana's own complaint guidance states plainly that a fee dispute usually does not belong in
the disciplinary process: "matters that do not involve substantial claims of misconduct or that
involve disputes over the amount of fees, unless the fees are clearly unreasonable, are not
handled through the lawyer discipline system." That same page states the Disciplinary Commission
does not recover a client's money, and points a fee-dispute complainant toward small claims court
and toward the ISBA's Clients' Financial Assistance Fund instead.

Once a grievance raises a substantial question of misconduct, the process described at
<https://www.in.gov/courts/ojar/di-process/> runs from an Executive Director review, through a
possible caution letter, to formal disciplinary proceedings before the Indiana Supreme Court.
Possible outcomes include a private or public reprimand, a suspension for a set period, a
suspension with reinstatement conditioned on the lawyer proving fitness, and permanent
disbarment. None of these outcomes returns money to the client. This pack found no separate
Indiana disciplinary restitution mechanism that can order a lawyer to refund money as part of a
disciplinary sanction, and found no stated deadline for filing a grievance in the sections of
Rule 23 reviewed for this pack; see the gap list for the limits of that search.

## 5. Client protection fund

The Indiana State Bar Association administers the Clients' Financial Assistance Fund (CFAF), a
voluntary, member-funded program, not a court-created or state-mandated fund, described at
<https://www.inbar.org/page/cfaf>. Its Rules of Procedure, adopted August 23, 2019 by the ISBA,
state that "all payments shall be a matter of grace," that no client or member of the public has
any right in the Fund, and that the Committee holds sole discretion over whether to recommend an
award, subject to Board of Governors approval for larger awards (see below). This pack could not
confirm any right of appeal from a denial, and does not state that one exists.

What it covers: losses caused by a lawyer's dishonest act or misappropriation of client funds or
property. Fee disputes and ordinary malpractice are not what the Fund covers; only dishonest
conduct qualifies.

Requirements and exclusions confirmed from the CFAF Rules of Procedure:

- The lawyer must have been an Indiana Bar member at the time of the conduct, and the claimant
  must be an Indiana resident or the underlying matter must have been an Indiana matter.
- A disciplinary grievance ordinarily must already be on file against the lawyer before a CFAF
  claim can proceed, unless the lawyer is deceased, disbarred, or has resigned from the bar.
- A claimant has 2 years from discovery of the loss to file a notice of loss, extendable for
  legal disability or other equitable reasons.
- Attorney fees the claimant paid to pursue the CFAF claim itself are excluded from
  reimbursement.
- The dishonest lawyer's close relatives and law partners are excluded from receiving CFAF
  payments.
- An emergency interim payment of up to 25 percent of the maximum possible award is available
  before the full claim is resolved.

The dollar caps: $15,000 per claimant, and $50,000 total that can be paid out of the Fund for
the conduct of any one lawyer. An award between $10,001 and $15,000 requires approval by the
ISBA Board of Governors. Once the $50,000 aggregate for a lawyer is exhausted, no more than an
additional $10,000 total can be paid beyond that cap.

As of August 2024, an ISBA-authored snapshot of the CFAF page stated the Fund had paused
accepting new applications, with a note to check back in summer 2024,
<https://web.archive.org/web/20240830182724/https://www.inbar.org/page/cfaf>. On 2026-08-20 the
live page at <https://www.inbar.org/page/cfaf> was reachable directly and describes a full
application process (eligibility, required records, how long review takes) with no pause notice
on it. That absence of a pause notice is not a confirmed statement that the Fund is currently
open, so call the ISBA directly before assuming the Fund is accepting applications.

## 6. Small claims or equivalent court

Indiana's small claims forum sits inside the regular trial courts rather than as a separate
court system: a "small claims docket" within a Circuit Court (IC 33-28-3) or within a Superior
Court (IC 33-29-2), depending on which court has jurisdiction in that county.

The jurisdictional dollar limit is $10,000 in both dockets:

- IC 33-28-3-4 (Circuit Court small claims docket): civil actions up to $10,000, and landlord
  possessory actions where rent due does not exceed $10,000.
- IC 33-29-2-4 (Superior Court small claims and misdemeanor division): the same $10,000 figure,
  for the same two categories of case.

A plaintiff or counterclaiming defendant may waive the excess of a larger claim to bring it
within the $10,000 limit. No county-level or township-level variation in this dollar limit could
be confirmed for either docket; this is worth rechecking directly with your county's small
claims court before filing, because Indiana's small claims structure has historically varied by
county and Marion County in particular has run its own township-based small claims courts.

Statewide procedural rules, the Indiana Small Claims Rules, effective January 1, 2025, and
published at
<https://rules.incourts.gov/pdf/PDF%20-%20Small%20Claims/small-claims.pdf>, apply to "all courts
of the State of Indiana, including Marion County Small Claims Courts." These rules govern how a
case is filed and heard; they do not themselves set or vary the jurisdictional dollar limit,
which is set by the statutes above. No small claims rule specific to suing a lawyer was found.

Lawyer appearance rules, corporate-party representation requirements, filing fees, and appeal or
trial de novo mechanics were not confirmed from a source this pack could reach; see the gap
list.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

Indiana's general limitation statute is IC 34-11-2, and the relevant periods for a fee dispute
or a malpractice claim are:

- Written contract for the payment of money, such as a signed fee agreement promising to pay
  the lawyer: 6 years, under IC 34-11-2-9, for contracts executed after August 31, 1982.
- Oral contract, or any contract not in writing: 6 years, under IC 34-11-2-7, the same period as
  the written-for-money contract above.
- Written contract that is not for the payment of money: 10 years, under IC 34-11-2-11. A
  typical attorney fee agreement, which promises payment of money, falls under the 6-year period
  above rather than this one.

Legal malpractice: Indiana's leading case on this point, the Indiana Supreme Court's decision in
Shideler v. Dwyer, 275 Ind. 270, 417 N.E.2d 281 (1981), is confirmed as a real, correctly cited
opinion through CourtListener's case search metadata; its full text was not read for this pack.
This pack could not independently confirm from a primary source read in full which specific
limitation statute and accrual rule Indiana courts currently apply to a legal malpractice claim,
so the malpractice period, its discovery rule, and any statute of repose are not stated as
confirmed law in this pack; see the flagged table and the gap list.

Every dollar figure and deadline above comes from the confirmed or flagged tables in
[verification_in.md](verification_in.md); nothing in this section is a prediction about how a
court would apply these periods to a specific set of facts.

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

The Rules of Professional Conduct are a disciplinary code, not a basis for a lawsuit by
themselves. Indiana's own Scope section, comment 20, states that a violation of a Rule should
not itself give rise to a cause of action against a lawyer, and should not create a presumption
that a legal duty was breached. Indiana does not give a client a private cause of action for a
rules violation.

Indiana's Scope comment goes further than simply denying a cause of action, though: the same
comment 20 states that the Rules "may be used as non-conclusive evidence that a lawyer has
breached a duty owed to a client," and that "a lawyer's violation of a Rule may be evidence of
breach of the applicable standard of conduct." So while a rule violation cannot be sued on by
itself, it can be used as evidence if a malpractice claim is otherwise available.

Your actual money route runs through the other forums in this pack instead: a contract claim in
small claims or another court (Section 6), the ISBA's voluntary AFDR process if the lawyer is a
participating member (Section 3), or the Clients' Financial Assistance Fund where the conduct
was dishonest rather than merely a bad bill or a billing disagreement (Section 5). Getting the
lawyer disciplined (Section 4) is a separate goal from getting money back, and it runs through a
separate forum that does not itself return money.
