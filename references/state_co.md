# Colorado

This pack is for a person in Colorado who has a fee, billing, communication, or file-return
dispute with their own lawyer. It does not cover legal malpractice as a claim to run yourself.
Retrieved 2026-08-20.

What a Colorado reader most needs to know, that a reader in another state does not:

- Colorado runs attorney fee arbitration through the Colorado Bar Association's Legal Fee
  Arbitration Committee (LFAC), not through the courts or the disciplinary office. It is free,
  and once both sides agree in writing to submit, the award is binding and conclusive on both
  parties. The catch is that agreement: except when a judge orders the case to LFAC or the
  state's attorney regulator refers it there, both you and your lawyer have to consent. See
  Section 3.
- Colo. RPC 1.5(f) treats every advance fee, retainer, or flat fee as the client's own money
  until the lawyer actually earns it, and requires it to sit in the lawyer's trust account until
  then. Colo. RPC 1.5(g) goes further and bans nonrefundable fees and nonrefundable retainers
  outright, along with any agreement that restricts your right to a refund of unearned or
  unreasonable fees or your right to fire the lawyer. If your lawyer told you a retainer was
  "nonrefundable," that term itself may not be enforceable. See Section 2.
- Colorado's attorney regulation runs through the Office of Attorney Regulation Counsel (OARC),
  described as "an independent office of the Colorado Supreme Court," not a voluntary bar
  association program. OARC's own FAQ says a plain disagreement over fees is usually referred to
  the LFAC, mediation, or the civil courts rather than pursued as discipline. See Section 4.
- The Attorneys' Fund for Client Protection caps a payout at $50,000 per claim and $100,000
  total against a single attorney, and you generally have to file your disciplinary complaint
  with OARC first. Fee disputes and malpractice are excluded outright; the Fund only pays for
  dishonest conduct such as theft or conversion of client money or property. See Section 5.
- Colorado small claims court generally bars lawyers from appearing, but a lawyer sued in small
  claims can still represent himself or herself, because self-representation is the rule for
  every individual party. Under C.R.S. 13-6-407(4), once any attorney appears in the case, even
  the defendant lawyer appearing pro se, the other side may then also bring in counsel. This is
  the state's own answer to what looks at first like a contradiction, and it matters directly
  for a reader suing their own lawyer. See Section 6.

## 1. Rules of professional conduct, numbering and adoption

The Colorado Supreme Court adopts the Colorado Rules of Professional Conduct (Colo. RPC)
directly, as an appendix to the Colorado Rules of Civil Procedure. Cite them as "Colo. RPC"
followed by the rule number, for example "Colo. RPC 1.5." The current rules were adopted by the
Colorado Supreme Court effective January 1, 2008, and have been amended many times since; the
version of Rule 1.5 in force reflects all rule changes adopted and effective as of December 10,
2025, the most recent amendment date confirmed for this pack.

Colorado follows the ABA model numbering closely for the rules a reader is most likely to need:
Colo. RPC 1.4 (communication), 1.5 (fees), and 1.16 (declining or terminating representation) sit
at the numbers a reader would expect. One numbering difference to know: Colorado split the model
Rule 1.15 (safekeeping property) into five separate rules, 1.15A through 1.15E, covering general
duties, account requirements, use of trust accounts, required records, and approved institutions.
A reader looking only for "Rule 1.15" should look at 1.15A first.

The official publication point for the full rule set, with the Preamble, Scope, and all Comments,
is the Colorado Bar Association's own posting at
<https://www.cobar.org/Portals/COBAR/repository/rules_of_prof_conduct.pdf>. The rule text itself,
including Rule 1.5, is also available in current form through CBA's practice-tools portal.

## 2. Deviations from the model reasonableness factors

Colo. RPC 1.5(a) lists the same eight reasonableness factors as the ABA model rule, with no
addition or subtraction. The value in this section, for Colorado, is not in the factor list; it
is in what the rule requires around fees that the model rule does not.

Colo. RPC 1.5(b) tracks the model rule on written communication of the fee basis or rate before
or within a reasonable time after the representation begins, with a duty to communicate later
changes in writing as well.

Colo. RPC 1.5(f), with no counterpart in the model rule, states plainly that fees are not earned
until the lawyer confers a benefit on the client or performs a legal service. Advances of
unearned fees are the client's property and must be deposited in the lawyer's trust account,
under Colo. RPC 1.15B(a)(1), until earned. If the advance is property rather than money, the
lawyer must hold it separately from the lawyer's own property under Colo. RPC 1.15A(a). This is
the opposite of the small minority of states that let a lawyer treat a signed retainer as the
lawyer's own money on receipt.

Colo. RPC 1.5(g), also with no model counterpart, is a flat prohibition: "Nonrefundable fees and
nonrefundable retainers are prohibited." It also bars any agreement that restricts your right to
terminate the representation or that unreasonably restricts your right to a refund of unearned or
unreasonable fees. A reader who signed something calling a retainer "nonrefundable" should treat
that label as suspect rather than final.

Colo. RPC 1.5(h) separately defines and regulates a "flat fee," a fixed amount for specified
services regardless of time or effort. A flat fee agreement, or part of one, taken in advance of
being earned must be described in writing covering the services included, the amount and timing
of payment, and how much is earned if the representation ends early. If a dispute arises over
whether a flat fee has been earned, Colo. RPC 1.5(h)(2) requires the lawyer to follow Colo. RPC
1.15A(c) for the disputed portion, which functions much like the trust-account dispute-holding
duty applied elsewhere in the rules.

Colo. RPC 1.5(c) and (d) track the model rule closely on contingent fees: a signed writing before
or within a reasonable time after the representation begins, a bar on contingent fees in criminal
defense and in domestic relations matters where payment is contingent on securing a divorce or on
the amount of support, maintenance, or property settlement, and a requirement to retain the
signed agreement for seven years after the matter ends.

## 3. Attorney fee arbitration

This is the section that most changes the realistic path for a Colorado reader.

Colorado does not run fee arbitration through the courts or through the state's disciplinary
office. It runs through the Colorado Bar Association's Legal Fee Arbitration Committee (LFAC), a
joint committee of the Colorado Bar Association and the Denver Bar Association, governed by the
CBA's own bylaws. There is no cost to either party to use it. The CBA's own account of the LFAC
process is at
<https://cl.cobar.org/features/legal-fee-dispute-arbitration-through-the-cba/>.

Participation requires the parties to mutually agree that the dispute be arbitrated by the LFAC.
There are two exceptions where agreement is not required: a Colorado judge can issue an order
assigning a matter to LFAC arbitration, or the Office of Attorney Regulation Counsel can refer a
matter there. Outside those two situations, both the client and the lawyer have to consent, most
often because the fee agreement itself contains a clause requiring LFAC resolution, though
parties without such a clause can still jointly agree afterward.

Once both parties agree, each completes an online arbitration request form, and the LFAC liaison
sends each side paperwork to complete and return within 45 days, including documents on the
nature and dollar amount of the dispute. An arbitrator is then assigned, typically a practicing
Colorado attorney with no monetary interest in the matter. The Committee arbitrates roughly 25 to
50 matters a year statewide. The arbitrator's
decision, once adopted by the Committee, is signed and mailed to both parties, and both parties
have already agreed in writing that the award will be binding and conclusive.

The Office of Attorney Regulation Counsel's own public guidance treats LFAC as the recommended
first stop for a genuine billing disagreement, stating that a disagreement over fees is usually
not evidence of misconduct and that most such disputes are referred to LFAC, another arbitration
forum, mediation, or the civil courts, rather than pursued through the complaint process. See
Section 4.

No local or county-level fee arbitration program separate from the statewide CBA/Denver Bar
committee was found for this pack.

## 4. Bar complaint process

The Office of Attorney Regulation Counsel (OARC), described on its own site as "an independent
office of the Colorado Supreme Court," reviews complaints against attorneys. A complaint can be
filed online at
<https://www.coloradolegalregulation.com/complaints/file_complaintagainstatty/>, or by phone at
(303) 457-5800 or toll free (877) 888-1370. Filing connects a complainant to OARC's central
intake division, where either a non-lawyer investigator or an attorney will ask about the
complaint; intake staff will not give legal advice.

OARC's own materials draw a clear line for a billing disagreement: OARC states that most
disputes over how fees were used or whether they were earned are referred out to the LFAC, a
different arbitration forum, mediation, or the civil courts, rather than treated as a disciplinary
matter. A reader whose only issue is the size or fairness of a bill, with no claim of dishonesty
or misconduct, should expect a complaint on that basis alone to be redirected rather than
investigated as discipline.

Under C.R.C.P. 242.12, a request for investigation against an attorney must be filed within five
years of the underlying conduct. Depending on what intake finds, a matter can be dismissed,
resolved through diversion (an alternative to formal discipline for minor misconduct), or
forwarded for further investigation and possible formal discipline before the Office of the
Presiding Disciplinary Judge. Proceedings before a formal complaint is filed are generally kept
confidential.

OARC's own FAQ states directly what the office cannot do: it has no jurisdiction over civil or
criminal proceedings, cannot remove or replace your attorney, cannot offer legal advice, and
cannot help recover attorney fees or overturn an unfavorable ruling in your case. Discipline is
not a refund. Under C.R.C.P. 252.16, a lawyer cannot charge you a fee to help you file a claim
with the Attorneys' Fund for Client Protection (Section 5), which is the closer route if money
was actually taken dishonestly rather than merely disputed.

## 5. Client protection fund

The Attorneys' Fund for Client Protection is created and governed by C.R.C.P. 252 and
administered by the Colorado Supreme Court through OARC, with public information at
<https://www.coloradolegalregulation.com/complaints/faq_cpf/>.

What it covers: a loss of money or property caused by the dishonest conduct of a Colorado-
licensed attorney who served the client as an attorney or in a court-appointed fiduciary role.
"Dishonest" conduct includes theft, embezzlement, or conversion of client money or property. The
Fund may also pay claims in some circumstances when an attorney has died or become disabled, and
it is described as a remedy of last resort for losses that cannot be recovered elsewhere.

What it excludes: OARC's own FAQ states plainly that claims involving fee disputes, unfortunate
or ill-advised investments placed through an attorney, and professional negligence or malpractice
are not eligible. The Fund does not pay interest on claims.

Caps: a limit of $50,000 per claim, and an aggregate maximum of $100,000 for all claims against a
single attorney.

Deadline: a claim must be filed within three years of the date you knew or should have known
about the dishonest conduct, a discovery-rule deadline rather than a fixed date from the loss
itself.

Prerequisite: OARC's guidance directs a person who believes they lost money or property to
dishonest conduct to start by filing a disciplinary complaint against the attorney with OARC
(Section 4) before pursuing the Fund.

## 6. Small claims or equivalent court

Colorado's small claims court is a division of the county court, established under C.R.S.
13-6-402. The jurisdictional limit is $7,500, exclusive of interest and costs, under C.R.S.
13-6-403(1)(a); the same section separately covers claims to enforce homeowners' association
assessments, restrictive covenants, replevin, and contract rescission or specific performance up
to the same dollar limit. No county-level variation in that dollar limit was found; it is set at
the state level by statute.

Under C.R.S. 13-6-407(2)(a)(I), an individual party in small claims court "shall represent
himself or herself." An attorney is generally barred from appearing, except pro se, as an
authorized employee or partner of an organization that is itself a party, or as otherwise
permitted by supreme court rule. C.R.S. 13-6-407(4) then states: "If an attorney appears, as
permitted in subsection (2) or (3) of this section, the other party or parties in the case may be
represented by counsel, if such party or parties so choose." Read together, if you sue your own
lawyer and that lawyer represents himself or herself, which is a permitted appearance under
subsection (2), that appearance triggers your own right under subsection (4) to bring in your own
attorney too, even though small claims court otherwise discourages lawyers on both sides.

Appeal is governed by C.R.S. 13-6-410: either party may appeal, a record is made of the
proceedings, and a tape recording of the trial satisfies any transcript requirement on payment of
a nominal fee. Appeal follows county court rules and general appeal procedure, including the
right to counsel on appeal.

No small claims filing fee figure or fee schedule specific to Colorado's small claims division
was located in the sources reviewed for this pack; confirm the current fee directly with the
county court clerk before filing. No rule specific to suing a lawyer, beyond the representation
rule above, was found.

## 7. Statutes of limitation

A fee agreement dispute, such as a lawyer's failure to refund an unearned fee, is ordinarily a
contract claim. Under C.R.S. 13-80-101(1)(a), all contract actions, including personal contracts,
must be commenced within three years after the cause of action accrues. Colorado's general
limitation statutes do not separately shorten the period for an oral contract; the three-year
period in section 13-80-101 applies to contract actions generally, regardless of the theory or
against whom the suit is brought.

Under C.R.S. 13-80-102(1)(a), tort actions, including negligence claims, must be commenced within
two years. Legal malpractice against your own lawyer, a claim that the lawyer's own negligence
harmed you, needs its own counsel rather than self-help; it is included here only so you know a
clock exists, not as something to run yourself. Because Colorado malpractice claims can sound in
either tort or contract depending on how they are pleaded, the applicable period may be two years
or three years depending on the theory chosen; this pack does not resolve which theory fits a
particular set of facts, and that choice is exactly the kind of judgment a malpractice claim
needs its own lawyer for.

The general discovery rule for accrual, C.R.S. 13-80-108(1), states that a cause of action for
injury to person, property, reputation, relationship, or status accrues on the date both the
injury and its cause are known or should have been known through reasonable diligence.
C.R.S. 13-80-108(6) states the same discovery-rule accrual specifically for breach of contract,
warranty, or trust: the cause of action accrues when the breach is discovered or should have been
discovered through reasonable diligence.

No statute of repose specific to legal malpractice was found. A commonly cited figure for a
four-year legal malpractice repose period traced back to C.R.S. 13-80-102.5, but that statute, read
directly, is titled "Limitation of actions - medical or health care" and applies only to health-
care institutions and health-care professionals as it defines them, physicians, nurses, dentists,
and similar licensed practitioners; it does not apply to attorneys. That claim was cut from this
pack rather than included on the strength of a source that got it wrong. See the gap list in
[verification_co.md](verification_co.md).

## 8. Private right of action for a rules violation

The Colorado Rules of Professional Conduct are, by their own terms, a disciplinary code, not a
basis for a civil lawsuit. Comment [20] to the Preamble and Scope states: "Violation of a Rule
should not itself give rise to a cause of action against a lawyer nor should it create any
presumption in such a case that a legal duty has been breached... They are not designed to be a
basis for civil liability." Colorado does not give a client a private cause of action for a rules
violation by itself.

The same comment goes on to say that, because the rules establish standards of conduct, "in
appropriate cases, a lawyer's violation of a Rule may be evidence of breach of the applicable
standard of conduct." The CBA's own published annotation to the rule cites a Colorado Court of
Appeals decision, Moye White LLP v. Beren, 2013 COA 89, 320 P.3d 373, for the point that Colorado
courts may look to the rules to determine whether an attorney breached a standard of care, even
though the rules themselves do not create the duty. This pack does not independently confirm the
holding of that case beyond the CBA's own annotation citing it; a reader considering a malpractice
claim should raise this with their own counsel rather than rely on it directly.

Your actual money route, separate from any malpractice claim, runs through the other forums in
this pack: a contract claim in small claims or another court (Section 6), the CBA's fee
arbitration committee (Section 3), or the Attorneys' Fund for Client Protection where the conduct
was dishonest rather than merely a bad bill (Section 5). Getting the lawyer disciplined
(Section 4) is a separate goal from getting money back, and it runs through a separate forum.
