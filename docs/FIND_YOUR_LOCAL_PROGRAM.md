# Find your local fee arbitration program

## Read your state pack first

Open section 3 of your own state pack, `references/state_XX.md`. For many states
that section is the complete answer: a statewide program with its own rules, or a
clear statement that no program of any kind exists. If your state pack already
names the program, its rules, and who runs it, stop here and use that. You do not
need this file.

Use this file only when your state pack says the program is run locally, county by
county or district by district, and does not name every local body, or when it says
the local layer is incomplete or unconfirmed. Some state packs will say this in
plain words. If yours does, that is your cue to keep reading.

## Before you search: whose county matters

Search by the county or city where the lawyer practices, not where you live. A fee
arbitration program is almost always run by the bar association local to the
lawyer's office, not to the client's address. If the lawyer's office and your home
are in different counties, use the lawyer's.

## The search procedure

Work through these in order. Stop as soon as you find a program page that is
clearly live and clearly on-topic. Do not stop at the first search result if it is a
law firm's marketing page, a directory listing, or a summary written by someone
other than the bar or the court.

### Step 1: the state bar's own site

Go to the official website of your state's bar association or the state agency that
licenses lawyers. This is often the same site named in section 4 of your state pack
for the disciplinary authority. Look for a page or menu item called one of:

- Fee arbitration
- Fee dispute resolution
- Attorney-client fee dispute
- Client assistance program
- Consumer assistance program
- Lawyer-client fee arbitration

These pages sometimes list every local committee the state bar knows about, and
sometimes just point you to county and metropolitan bars. Either way, this is the
right starting page.

Search phrasing that works: `[state name] bar association fee dispute` or
`[state name] fee arbitration committee district`.

### Step 2: the county or metropolitan bar association where the lawyer practices

If the state bar's page points to local committees, or if step 1 turns up nothing,
go to the bar association for the county, city, or metropolitan area where the
lawyer's office is. Most metropolitan areas with any bar association activity at
all have one. Look for a page or section called one of:

- Fee dispute resolution
- Fee arbitration committee
- Attorney-client mediation
- Lawyer referral and information service (some of these also run fee dispute
  intake)

Search phrasing that works: `[lawyer's county] bar association fee dispute` or
`[lawyer's city] bar fee arbitration`.

### Step 3: the local court's self-help or civil division page

If neither the state bar nor the local bar has a program, check the self-help
center or civil division page for the trial court in the county where the lawyer
practices. Some jurisdictions route fee disputes through a court-annexed program
rather than a bar association. Look for a page called one of:

- Self-help center
- Civil division, fee disputes
- Alternative dispute resolution, attorney fees

## Confirm before you rely on anything you find

Finding a page is not the same as finding a live program. Before you use what you
found, confirm these directly from the program's own page, or by phoning the
number on that page:

1. The program still exists and takes new filings. A page with no recent date, or
   language that reads like it has not been updated in years, is a reason to call
   and check, not a reason to assume it is current.
2. Whether the lawyer can decline to participate, or is bound once you file.
3. Any dollar floor or ceiling on what the program will hear.
4. Any filing deadline.
5. Whether there is a filing fee, and how much.
6. Who exactly to send the form to: a name, an office, an address, or a portal.

Get all six from the program's own page or from a phone call to the program
itself. Do not take these from a third-party directory, a law firm's summary, or a
general legal information site. Those go stale and are not the authority.

## If nothing turns up, or nobody answers

Some counties genuinely have no program. If you worked through all three steps and
found nothing live, or you found a program and could not get anyone to confirm it
still operates, treat this the same as your state pack saying no program exists.
Fall back to the routing in `docs/DECISION_TREE.md`, question 6, which already
covers the no-program case and sends you to question 7.

## Write down what you find

Once you confirm a program, write it into your tracker: the program's name, its
web address, the phone number you called, the date you called, and the answers to
the six confirmation questions above.

If you are willing, consider opening an issue or a pull request on this repo so the
next person searching the same county has less work to do. See
`CONTRIBUTING.md` for the citation standard a contribution needs to meet: a
program added to a state pack needs a primary source and a retrieval date, the same
as any other claim in this kit.

## For the model

If you are the model helping someone through this search, do not guess a program
name, a phone number, a deadline, or a dollar figure. Do not fill a gap with
something that sounds plausible or with what a neighboring county does. If a search
result is not confirmed, say so.

"I could not confirm a local program in your county" is a correct and complete
answer when that is true. It is not a failure to report. The standard this kit
holds to is that an omission the user can see and act on is safe, and an invented
program name, deadline, or phone number is not. When in doubt, say what you do not
know and point back to the search steps above so the user can finish the search
themselves.
